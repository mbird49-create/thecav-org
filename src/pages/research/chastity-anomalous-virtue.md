## Abstract

Is chastity becoming an anomalous virtue in modern, mainstream society? This study treats that as an open question. Nothing here assumes that chastity is declining, or that it should be preserved. It defines chastity in the virtue sense as sexual restraint that is chosen for a reason the person endorses, and keeps it apart from sexlessness caused by circumstance. Because "chastity" covers several distinct things, the study splits the question into four parts: chastity broadly, premarital abstinence, vowed celibacy, and marital fidelity. It then tests each against General Social Survey microdata, CDC and NCHS surveys, Gallup and Pew polling, Japan's National Fertility Survey, Britain's Natsal, and Catholic vocation counts. The evidence suggests the parts are moving in different directions, and that the question has no single answer.

**Working definition.** In this study a virtue counts as *anomalous* in a reference population (here, a country) when fewer than 20% of that population hold it, on the best available national measure; a share of 20-25% is treated as borderline. Attitude surveys measure stated norms, not chosen restraint, so they are an upper bound on conviction, not a measure of it.

**Keywords:** chastity; premarital abstinence; sexlessness; marital fidelity; vowed celibacy; brahmacharya; ʿiffa; tzniut; purity culture; Chronological Ethics; Semantic Ethics; Sacred Axiology

## About this study

The Center for the Study of Anomalous Virtues (CAV), a hybrid human-AI research organization, prepared this study with sources current as of 20 October 2026. Figures were checked against primary data, official releases, or the original papers where these could be reached. Figures computed by CAV directly from public microdata are marked "CAV computation." Claims that could not be fully verified are tagged **[uncertain]**, and known holes in the evidence are tagged **[gap]**.

**Labels used throughout.**
- **Evidence**: documented findings and data.
- **Interpretation (descriptive)**: what the data show, as an empirical claim about behavior, attitudes, or trends.
- **Interpretation (normative)**: what those findings would mean for *virtue*. Normative claims depend on a view of what virtue is, and the data alone cannot settle them.

## Executive summary

1. **Two different things are moving in opposite directions.**
   - In the United States, the *stated norm* that sex belongs only in marriage has weakened steadily. In the GSS, the share saying premarital sex is "always wrong" fell from 31.5% in the 1970s to 14.7% in 2021-2024 (CAV computation). In Gallup's May 2025 poll, 68% of US adults call sex between an unmarried man and woman "morally acceptable."
   - Over the same years, the *behavior* of not having sex has risen among young adults. In the GSS, past-year sexlessness among 18-29 year olds rose from 15.4% (men) and 12.6% (women) in 1988-1995 to 19.2% and 22.1% in 2021-2024 (CAV computation). Ueda et al. (2020), working from the same GSS data, found past-year sexual inactivity among men aged 18-24 rose from 18.9% (2000-2002) to 30.9% (2016-2018).
2. **The rise in behavior appears to be mostly circumstance, not conviction.** Among never-married 18-29 year olds in the GSS with no partner in the past year, the share who also say premarital sex is always or almost always wrong fell from about 45-49% (1988-2013) to about 28% (2014-2018 and 2021-2024; small samples). The drop came in 2014-2018, before the GSS moved to web interviews, so the mode change cannot explain it. Part of it is compositional: as norm-holders shrink, their share of the sexless falls even if their own behavior is unchanged. Ueda et al. tie male inactivity to low income, part-time work and unemployment.
3. **Fidelity is not anomalous.** Gallup (2025) finds 89% call an extramarital affair "morally wrong," and Pew's 40-country survey (2014; fielded 2013) found a median of 78% calling it unacceptable. Self-reported infidelity in the GSS is roughly flat.
4. **Vowed celibacy is shrinking where measured.** US Catholic religious sisters fell from 178,740 (1965) to 33,135 (2025), per CARA. Secular forms of restraint are visible in the media, but are not documented as practiced virtue.
5. **Provisional verdict.** By the working definition above, premarital abstinence as a stated norm is anomalous in the United States and Britain, and borderline in Japan. In the US it was already a minority view in the 1970s (31.5%) and is a shrinking minority today (14.7%, GSS 2021-2024). In Britain, 13% called sex between unmarried adults morally unacceptable (Pew 2014; survey fielded 2013), and 6% told British Social Attitudes in 2012 that premarital sex is "always wrong." In Japan the figure was 21% (Pew 2014; survey fielded 2013). It is not anomalous globally: the 40-country median was 46%, and in Pakistan 94%. In countries like Pakistan, though, Pew measures acceptability, not chosen virtue, and chosen and imposed restraint cannot be separated (§4). The claim that the virtue is "migrating" into secular forms is not supported by current data. The question stays open.

## 1. Definitions, and what the data measure

**Evidence (definitions in primary texts).**
- The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* (CCC 2337) defines chastity as "the successful integration of sexuality within the person and thus the inner unity of man in his bodily and spiritual being." CCC 2348 says "All the baptized are called to chastity," and CCC 2349 that people should cultivate it "in the way that is suited to their state of life," quoting a patristic source: "There are three forms of the virtue of chastity: the first is that of spouses, the second that of widows, and the third that of virgins."
- The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints' manual *Gospel Principles*, ch. 39: "We are to have sexual relations only with our spouse to whom we are legally married. No one, male or female, is to have sexual relations before marriage."
- Pali Vinaya, *pārājika* 1: a monk who "has sexual intercourse, even with a female animal, he is expelled and excluded from the community" (Bhikkhu Brahmali, trans., SuttaCentral).
- Gandhi, *Autobiography*, part III ch. 8: "Brahmacharya means control of the senses in thought, word and deed."

**Four working categories** (CAV's own construct):
- **(A) Chastity broadly:** sexual restraint and fidelity appropriate to one's state of life. It includes conjugal chastity, so it is not the same as "no sex."
- **(B) Premarital abstinence:** no sexual intercourse before marriage.
- **(C) Lifelong or vowed celibacy:** monastic, clerical, or religious vows.
- **(D) Marital fidelity:** no sex outside marriage while married.

| Data | What it measures | Category it can speak to | What it cannot tell us |
|---|---|---|---|
| GSS PARTNERS, SEXFREQ | Number of partners and frequency, past 12 months | None directly: sexlessness from any cause | Motive |
| GSS PREMARSX | Stated norm on premarital sex | B (as a value) | Behavior |
| GSS XMARSEX, EVSTRAY | Norm on, and self-report of, extramarital sex | D | Underreporting |
| YRBS, NSFG "ever had sex" | Virginity by age | B (behavior only) | Motive |
| NSFG "main reason" | Self-reported motive, teens 15-19 | B (conviction vs. other reasons) | Adults; trend |
| IPSS Japan | Behavior and partly motive, never-married 18-34 | B, partly | Religious motive |
| Natsal (UK) | Past-month frequency | None directly | Motive |
| CARA | Count of vowed persons | C | Inner observance |
| Gallup, Pew | Stated moral acceptability | B and D as values | Behavior |

**Interpretation (descriptive).** No large survey measures chastity as a virtue directly. Virtue requires both a disposition and a reason, and surveys mostly capture behavior *or* attitude, rarely both in the same person. The best available bridges are joining GSS behavior and attitude for the same respondents (§3.1) and the NSFG's "main reason" question for teenagers (§3.5).

**Interpretation (normative).** For this study, "virtue" implies chosen restraint for a reason the agent endorses. A sexless life caused by isolation, poverty, or illness is not, by this standard, chastity. The study keeps the two apart throughout.

## 2. Chosen restraint versus circumstantial sexlessness

**Evidence.**
- Ueda et al. (2020) report that men with lower income and with part-time or no employment were more likely to be sexually inactive, and that the increase was concentrated among unmarried men (unmarried men 18-44: 16.2% to 24.4%).
- Wellings et al. (BMJ 2019, Natsal) found falling sexual frequency was steepest among people aged 25 and over and among the married or cohabiting, alongside "an increase in the proportion reporting that they would prefer sex more often."
- IPSS (16th Japanese National Fertility Survey, 2021): among never-married 18-34 year olds, 33.5% of men and 34.1% of women were "not particularly interested in a relationship with the opposite sex." Only 21.1% of never-married men and 27.8% of women had a dating partner.
- NSFG 2015-2019 (NCHS NHSR No. 196, Table 5): among teenagers 15-19 who had never had sex, the main reason was "Against religion or morals" for 32.5% of girls and 26.2% of boys.

**Interpretation (descriptive).** A working test separates four sources of not having sex:
1. **Conviction:** the person holds a norm against sex in their current state and acts on it.
2. **Preference without moral content:** low interest, asexual orientation, or other priorities.
3. **Constraint:** no partner, poverty, illness, disability, caregiving, living with parents.
4. **Displacement:** time and attention spent elsewhere (hypothesized, weakly evidenced).

Only (1) is chastity in the virtue sense; (2) may overlap with it when reflectively endorsed; (3) and (4) are circumstance. The data in §3 suggest that (1) is a shrinking share of the young sexless.

**Interpretation (normative).** Reading the so-called "sex recession" as a moral revival would mistake involuntary loneliness for virtue, which would be both inaccurate and unkind to those for whom it is a loss. Treating all abstinence as repression would erase people who choose it for reasons they endorse. Both errors are possible.

## 3. Data

### 3.1 GSS: sexlessness, attitudes and the conviction test (CAV computation)

Source: GSS 1972-2024 cumulative file, release 3a, weighted with WTSSPS. "Sexless" means no sexual partners in the last 12 months. Cells with fewer than 80 cases are suppressed in period tables.

**Evidence: past-year sexlessness, ages 18-29 (weighted %).**

| Period | Men | Women |
|---|---|---|
| 1988-1995 | 15.4 | 12.6 |
| 1996-2005 | 14.0 | 11.5 |
| 2006-2013 | 14.3 | 12.6 |
| 2014-2018 | 21.1 | 15.6 |
| 2021-2024 | 19.2 | 22.1 |

Single years are noisy. All adults reporting no sex in the past year rose from 18.5% (1988-1995) to 26.4% (2021-2024).

**Evidence: attitudes ("always wrong," %).**

| Decade | Premarital sex | Extramarital sex |
|---|---|---|
| 1970s | 31.5 | 70.8 |
| 1980s | 26.8 | 74.0 |
| 1990s | 25.7 | 79.1 |
| 2000s | 26.2 | 81.4 |
| 2010s | 19.8 | 77.4 |
| 2021-2024 | 14.7 | 67.6 |

- Premarital sex "always wrong," ages 18-29: 13.3% (1970s), 17.9% (2000s), 8.2% (2021-2024).
- Among weekly attenders, "always wrong" is fairly stable (53.2% in the 1970s, 47.9% in 2021-2024), but weekly attendance among 18-29 year olds fell from 21.7% to 11.6%.

**Evidence: the conviction test.** Never-married respondents aged 18-29. PREMARSX is asked of a subsample, so the numbers are small.
- Those who say premarital sex is "always wrong" are far more often sexless (48.9-66.2% across periods) than those who say it is "not wrong at all" (9.5-17.0%):

| Period | Sexless, "always wrong" (%) | n | Sexless, "not wrong at all" (%) | n |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1988-1995 | 66.2 | 72 | 11.0 | 372 |
| 1996-2005 | 48.9 | 122 | 10.1 | 484 |
| 2006-2013 | 54.5 | 92 | 11.3 | 429 |
| 2014-2018 | 50.0 | 42 | 9.5 | 276 |
| 2021-2024 | 61.0 | 16 | 17.0 | 179 |

  Among people who hold no norm against premarital sex, sexlessness was flat at about 10-11% through 2018 and rose to 17.0% only in 2021-2024 (web-era waves).
- The share of sexless never-married 18-29 year olds who say premarital sex is "always" or "almost always" wrong was 44.8% (1988-1995), 49.3% (1996-2005), 47.3% (2006-2013), then 28.1% (2014-2018) and 27.6% (2021-2024). The drop occurred in 2014-2018, when the GSS was still interviewing in person; web interviewing began with the 2021 wave. Mode effects therefore cannot explain it.
- **Compositional effect.** As norm-holders shrink as a share of young adults, their share of the sexless falls even if their own sexlessness is unchanged. Part of the drop is that arithmetic. The rest reflects rising sexlessness among non-holders, visible here only in 2021-2024.

**Caveat: survey mode.** In 2021-2024 the GSS mixed web and in-person interviews, and the two differ a lot. Extramarital sex "always wrong": 62.8% (web) versus 76.7% (in-person). Sexlessness at 18-29: 23.7% (web) versus 14.1% (in-person). Mode was not randomly assigned. The 2021-2024 figures should not be read as a clean continuation of the earlier series.

**Interpretation (descriptive).**
1. Conviction still strongly predicts behavior at the individual level.
2. The population holding that conviction has shrunk, especially among the young.
3. Sexlessness rose anyway, so the post-2014 rise is drawn increasingly from people who do not hold the norm. That pattern fits circumstance better than revived conviction.
4. The 2020s drop in "extramarital sex always wrong" coincides with the switch to web interviews and may be partly a measurement artifact. Gallup's telephone series does not show a collapse.

### 3.2 Ueda et al., JAMA Network Open (2020)

**Evidence.** Ueda et al. analyze the same GSS, so their results confirm CAV's computation rather than corroborate it independently. Using GSS 2000-2018, ages 18-44: past-year inactivity among men 18-24 rose from 18.9% to 30.9% (aOR for trend 1.20; 95% CI 1.04-1.39). Among women 18-24 the change, 15.1% to 19.1%, was not significant. Men 25-34: 7.0% to 14.1%; women 25-34: 7.0% to 12.6%.

**Interpretation (descriptive).** CAV's own GSS series (§3.1) agrees in direction for men and shows the female increase arriving later, in the 2021-2024 waves, after Ueda's sample ends.

### 3.3 Twenge, Sherman and Wells, Archives of Sexual Behavior (2017)

**Evidence.** Among those aged 20-24, 15% of those born in the 1990s had no sexual partners since age 18, versus 6% of those born in the 1960s (GSS, N = 26,707). The shift was more pronounced among women and absent among Black Americans and the college-educated.

**Interpretation (descriptive).** This is a cohort effect on adult virginity, not a measure of motive. **[uncertain: mechanism not established]**

### 3.4 YRBS: US high school students

**Evidence.** High school students who had ever had sexual intercourse (CDC YRBS): 54.1% (1991), 46.8% (2013), 38.4% (2019), 30.0% (2021), 31.6% (2023). The 2021 survey was collected during the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have affected both behavior and response. The 2025 YRBS reports released so far do not give an "ever had sex" figure; Stateline (Sept 2026) reports that currently sexually active students fell to about 19% in 2025. **[uncertain: not confirmed from a CDC table]**

**Interpretation (descriptive).** Adolescent abstinence has risen strongly as a behavior over three decades, while adult approval of premarital sex also rose.

### 3.5 NSFG: virginity and motive

**Evidence.** NCHS NHSR No. 196 (2023), NSFG 2015-2019: 59.5% of girls and 61.3% of boys aged 15-19 have not had sex.

| Main reason for not having had sex | Girls 15-19 | Boys 15-19 |
|---|---|---|
| Against religion or morals | 32.5 | 26.2 |
| Haven't found the right person yet | 25.3 | 35.3 |
| Don't want to get (a female) pregnant | 15.9 | 11.1 |
| Don't want an STD | 4.4 | 4.2 |
| In a relationship, waiting for the right time | 5.6 | 4.7 |
| Other | 16.2 | 18.6 |

**Interpretation (descriptive).** About 19% of all girls 15-19 and about 16% of all boys are abstaining for a moral or religious reason (CAV computation). This is the cleanest direct measure of chastity as conviction in the study, and it shows conviction as a substantial minority motive. The trend in this item across survey cycles was not retrieved. **[gap]**

### 3.6 US attitudes: Gallup and Pew

**Evidence.** Gallup Values and Beliefs poll, May 2025 (n = 1,003): sex between an unmarried man and woman is morally acceptable to 68% (78% of those 18-34); an extramarital affair is wrong to 89%; sex between teenagers is wrong to 51%. Pew (2014; survey fielded 2013): 30% of Americans called premarital sex morally unacceptable.

**Interpretation (descriptive).** Young adults are more approving of premarital sex while being less sexually active. The gap between value and behavior is widest among the young.

### 3.7 Infidelity: norm and self-report

**Evidence.** GSS ever-married respondents who report ever having sex outside marriage: men 21.0% (1990s) to 17.5% (2021-2024); women 11.9% to 14.1% (CAV computation). Pew (2014; survey fielded 2013): a median of 78% across 40 nations call an affair morally unacceptable; France was the only country below half (47%).

**Interpretation (descriptive).** Marital fidelity is the most stable and widely shared part of chastity broadly. Underreporting is likely. **[uncertain: no validation data]**

### 3.8 Japan

**Evidence (IPSS 2021).** 47.0% of never-married men and 52.5% of never-married women aged 18-34 report no sexual experience. Among married couples with wives under 50, 37.9% had had intercourse in the last month. Response rate: 55.9%. Pew (2014; survey fielded 2013): 21% of Japanese call sex between unmarried adults unacceptable.

**Interpretation (descriptive).** Japan is the clearest case where abstinence is widespread and conviction is rare. The rising "not interested" share is ambiguous.

### 3.9 United Kingdom: Natsal

**Evidence (Wellings et al., BMJ 2019).** Ages 16-44 with no sex in the past month: women 28.5% (1991), 23.0% (2001), 29.3% (2012); men 30.9%, 26.0%, 29.2%.

**Evidence (attitudes).** Pew (2014; survey fielded 2013): 13% in Britain called sex between unmarried adults morally unacceptable, 38% acceptable, 44% not a moral issue. British Social Attitudes: "always wrong" fell from 16% (1983) to 6% (2012) (BSA 30); in 2017, 75% said premarital sex is "not wrong at all" and 8% "always" or "mostly" wrong (BSA 34).

**Interpretation (descriptive).** Britain's decline in frequency is concentrated among couples and people over 25, so it is not an abstinence story. Natsal-4 results were not retrieved. **[gap]**

### 3.10 Virginity pledges

**Evidence.** Bearman and Brückner (*American Journal of Sociology*, 2001) and Rosenbaum (*Pediatrics*, 2009) are widely summarized as finding that pledges delayed first intercourse only for some adolescents and in some settings, and that five years on, pledgers did not differ from matched non-pledgers. **[uncertain: summaries only; primary papers not read]**

### 3.11 Cross-national norms (Pew 2014; survey fielded 2013)

**Evidence.** A median of 46% across 40 nations saw sex between unmarried adults as morally unacceptable: 94% in Pakistan, 30% in the United States, 21% in Japan.

## 4. Religious traditions: stated norm versus practice

**Christianity.** The Catechism treats chastity as a virtue for every state of life, including marriage. CARA counts for the US: religious sisters 178,740 (1965) to 33,135 (2025); brothers 12,096 to 3,290; priests 59,426 to 33,462. Worldwide, sisters fell from 1,004,304 (1970) to 589,423 (2023), while priests were roughly level (419,728 to 406,996). Vocation counts measure entry into a celibate state, not fidelity to it.

**Islam.** Qur'an 24:30-31: "Tell the believing men to reduce [some] of their vision and guard their private parts. That is purer for them," with a parallel instruction to women. *ʿIffa* (chastity, continence) is a named virtue in Islamic ethics. **[uncertain: classical sources not opened]** Where restraint is backed by law and family honor, chosen and imposed restraint are hard to separate. No behavioral data for Muslim-majority countries was retrieved. **[gap]**

**Judaism.** Orthodox sexual ethics (*tzniut*, *niddah*) regulate timing and boundaries *within* marriage, treat marital intimacy as a duty, and generally treat lifelong celibacy as undesirable. **[uncertain: halakhic sources not opened]** Here chastity is not abstinence.

**Hinduism.** Brahmacharya names both the student life stage and sense-restraint more broadly. Gandhi took the vow in 1906 while married. His later brahmacharya "experiments" raise questions of consent and power that any treatment of him as an exemplar must address. **[uncertain: scholarly accounts not read in the primary]**

**Buddhism.** Monks are expelled for sexual intercourse (*pārājika* 1). For laypeople, the third precept is a fidelity norm, not celibacy. No ordination-trend data was retrieved. **[gap]**

**Latter-day Saints.** The law of chastity combines high norm clarity with institutional costs for breach. No practice data was retrieved. **[gap]**

**Interpretation (normative).** Equating religious chastity with virginity, as 1990s purity culture often did, narrows the traditions' own definitions.

## 5. New secular forms

**Evidence.**
- **Asexuality.** Bogaert (*Journal of Sex Research*, 2004) reported about 1% of a British national probability sample as never having felt sexual attraction. **[uncertain: figure from secondary summary]**
- **"Boysober."** *The New York Times* (Feb 2024) reported a 2024 social-media trend of taking a year off sex and dating. **[uncertain: media reports only; no prevalence data]**
- **Purity-culture backlash.** Joshua Harris, author of *I Kissed Dating Goodbye* (1997), writes that his publisher agreed to discontinue the book in 2018, and apologizes for harms it caused. Linda Kay Klein's *Pure* (2019) documents shame-based purity messaging and its effects.

**Interpretation (descriptive).** Asexuality is an orientation, not restraint of attraction, so counting it as "chastity migrating" would confuse the two. "Boysober"-style trends frame restraint as self-care and are usually temporary; their scale is unknown. The backlash is a critique of one *method* of teaching chastity, not necessarily of restraint itself.

**Interpretation (normative).** A secular chastity, if one exists, would be restraint chosen for reasons the person endorses, such as care for others, honesty, or integrity, without shame-based enforcement. The evidence that such a form is spreading is currently anecdotal.

## 6. Hypothesis tests

| Hypothesis | Result |
|---|---|
| H1. Chastity is becoming rarer as a stated value | **Supported for premarital abstinence** in the US (GSS 31.5% to 14.7%) and Britain (BSA 16% to 6%, 1983-2012); **not supported for fidelity** (Gallup 89%; Pew median 78%) |
| H2. Restraint is rising in practice | **Supported as behavior** (YRBS, GSS, Ueda, Natsal, IPSS) |
| H3. The rise reflects conviction | **Largely not supported.** The conviction share of the young sexless fell; inactivity tracks income and work; Natsal finds more people wanting more sex. Conviction remains a real minority motive among teens (NSFG) |
| H4. Chastity is migrating into secular forms | **Not supported (insufficient evidence)** |
| H5. Vowed celibacy is becoming anomalous | **Supported for Catholic religious life** in the US and for women religious worldwide; not measured for other traditions |
| H6. The pattern is cross-culturally uniform | **Not supported** (Pakistan 94% vs Japan 21%) |

**What would count as migration rather than circumstance:** a reason the person endorses independent of partner scarcity; durability beyond a trend cycle; a measurable forgone opportunity; a supporting community or practice; and survey evidence, not only media reports. None of the secular forms reviewed currently meets the last two.

## 7. Verdict table

Descriptive and normative judgments are kept in separate columns. "Anomalous" means held by fewer than 20% of a national population on the best available measure; 20-25% is borderline.

| Component | Descriptive finding | Normative reading (open) |
|---|---|---|
| Marital fidelity (D) | Strong, stable norm; self-reported infidelity roughly flat | **Not anomalous** |
| Premarital abstinence as conviction (B) | US 14.7% "always wrong" (GSS 2021-2024), a shrinking minority; Britain 13% (Pew) and 6% (BSA 2012); Japan 21% (Pew); 40-country median 46%; Pakistan 94% (Pew 2014; survey fielded 2013) | **Anomalous in the US and Britain; borderline in Japan; not anomalous globally.** Stated norm only, an upper bound on chosen conviction |
| Premarital abstinence as behavior | Rising | **Mostly circumstantial; not evidence of virtue** |
| Vowed celibacy (C) | US sisters down 81% since 1965 (to 2025); world sisters down 41% since 1970 (to 2023) (CARA; CAV computation) | **Anomalous and shrinking (Catholic data only)** |
| Secular "migration" | Visible in media, unmeasured | **Not supported; open** |
| Chastity broadly (A) | Mixed by component | **No single trajectory; the question must be split** |

"Anomalous" is relative to a reference population. By the threshold above, premarital abstinence as a stated norm is anomalous in the United States and Britain and borderline in Japan. In Pakistan it is the majority stated norm, but Pew measures acceptability, not chosen virtue, and where restraint is backed by law and family honor, chosen and imposed restraint cannot be separated (§4). A Western trend should not be presented as a global one.

## 8. Open questions and limits

- **Adult motive:** no US adult survey seen here asks *why* people are not having sex. The NSFG reason item covers teenagers only.
- **Survey mode:** the 2021-2024 GSS mixes web and in-person interviews, which affects both sexlessness and attitude estimates.
- **Small cells:** the GSS conviction test rests on 60-140 sexless respondents per period.
- **Not read in the primary [uncertain]:** the pledge studies, Bogaert, the Gandhi scholarship, and several religious texts.
- **No data [gap]:** India, behavioral data for Muslim-majority countries, Natsal-4, Add Health trends, LDS practice, and Buddhist ordination trends.
- About 30 figures in an early working draft could not be traced to a source and were removed.
