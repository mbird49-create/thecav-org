## Abstract

Is forgiveness largely forgotten, or no longer widely practiced? This study poses that question rather than assuming its answer, and tests it against the evidence. It treats forgiveness as a candidate *anomalous virtue*: a concept that looks universal at first sight, but whose working forms are thick, culturally specific, and lose meaning in translation. Part 1 surveys culturally particular forms: traditional Native Hawaiian *hoʻoponopono*, ubuntu-framed restorative justice, Amish forgiveness, Pashtun *nənawāta*, Jewish *teshuvah* and *mechilah*, Christian pardon traditions, Islamic *ʿafw* within *qiṣāṣ*, Arab *sulha*, Acholi *mato oput*, Albanian Kanun reconciliation, and Japanese apology–pardon practices. Part 2 reviews forgiveness and its absence in geopolitics: South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission, Franco-German reconciliation, Rwanda's gacaca courts, a randomized trial in Sierra Leone, Northern Ireland, the Balkans, and feud and revenge cycles. Part 3 answers the title question. Forgiveness in general has not been forgotten; talk of forgiveness and thin, therapy-style forgiveness have spread. What has lost institutional ground are the thick, communal, ritual forms that specify who forgives whom, before which witnesses, and in exchange for what. The aim is analytic clarity, not advocacy for forgiveness as an unqualified good.

**Keywords:** forgiveness; anomalous virtue; *hoʻoponopono*; *nənawāta*; *teshuvah*; *ʿafw*; *sulha*; *mato oput*; Kanun; truth and reconciliation; transitional justice; feud; Semantic Ethics; Chronological Ethics

## Summary of findings

- **Has forgiveness been forgotten? Not in general, but its thick forms have lost ground.** No long-run survey series exists. In Google Books, use of the word "forgiveness" fell from about 1850 to 1950, then rose steeply after 1980. The answer the evidence supports is narrower: *thick, communal, ritualized* forms have lost institutional ground, while *thin*, therapeutic forgiveness has spread.
- **Generic forgiveness is thin; the forms that actually operate are thick.** Modern Anglophone and therapeutic usage treats forgiveness as an inner, individual release of resentment. The traditions reviewed here are *structured, social, and conditional*. They specify who forgives whom, in what order, before which witnesses, and in exchange for what (confession, compensation, a shared meal, a ritual drink). That specificity is what fits CAV's "anomalous virtue" frame.
- **The concept itself has a history.** David Konstan argues that the full modern idea of interpersonal forgiveness was absent in classical antiquity. Martha Nussbaum traces the "transactional" model to Jewish and Christian institutions. Both arguments are contested, but both support treating forgiveness as particular rather than universal.
- **Well-sourced candidates:** traditional Native Hawaiian *hoʻoponopono*, Pashtun *nənawāta* (nanawatai), Jewish *teshuvah/mechilah*, Islamic *ʿafw* within *qiṣāṣ*, Arab *sulha*, Acholi *mato oput*, Albanian Kanun blood reconciliation, and Amish forgiveness. Each needs caveats: commercialization, romanticization or "invented tradition," and patriarchal or coercive features.
- **The geopolitical evidence is mixed.** Accepting the TRC's "truth" went with more reconciled racial attitudes in South Africa (Gibson). A randomized trial in Sierra Leone found that reconciliation forums raised forgiveness and social capital *and* worsened depression, anxiety, and PTSD. Rwanda's gacaca courts delivered mass accountability alongside retraumatization and fair-trial failures. The ICTY convicted 90 people without producing shared acknowledgment in Bosnia and Serbia.
- **The best-known geopolitical success, Franco-German reconciliation, rested on institutions more than on forgiveness as such.** Since 1963, about 10 million young people have taken part in Franco-German Youth Office exchanges. Lind argues that German contrition *followed* reconciliation rather than producing it.
- **Customary exits from revenge can work at scale when tied to a collective cause.** Kosovo's 1990–92 campaign to "forgive the blood" reconciled an estimated 1,200–2,500 feuds, and violent deaths fell. Feud killings partly returned after 1999.
- **The costs of having no working exit from revenge are documented** (blood-feud self-confinement in Albania, Northern Ireland's unresolved legacy disputes). So are the costs of badly designed forgiveness: pressure on victims and psychological harm.

## 1. Introduction: has forgiveness been forgotten?

Is forgiveness largely forgotten, or no longer widely practiced? This study treats that as a question, not a premise, and Part 3 answers it directly. Answering it well first requires asking what kind of thing forgiveness is.

The Center for the Study of Anomalous Virtues (CAV), a hybrid human-AI research organization, studies highly specific, often hard-to-translate concepts of moral excellence as potential diplomatic and ethical infrastructure, not as generic universal values. Forgiveness seems universal at first sight. This study argues that the generic concept is real but thin, and that the practices that actually do social work are thick, culturally specific, and lose meaning in translation.

**Generic forgiveness (Interpretation).** In contemporary psychology, forgiveness is mainly *intrapersonal*: the injured person lets go of resentment and of the urge to retaliate, without needing the offender's participation. Reconciliation is treated as separate. This model has a solid evidence base. A meta-analysis of 54 reports found that forgiveness interventions outperform no-treatment and alternative-treatment conditions on forgiveness outcomes (Wade, Hoyt, Kidwell & Worthington 2014). A 2024 trial of a self-directed REACH workbook in five countries (n = 4,598) reduced unforgiveness and slightly reduced depression and anxiety symptoms (Ho et al. 2024).

**Is the generic concept itself particular? (Evidence: scholarly argument.)**
- Konstan (*Before Forgiveness*, 2010) argues that Greek and Roman sources lack interpersonal forgiveness in the full sense: a bilateral process in which the wrongdoer's remorse and moral change prompt the victim to give up resentment. Ancient sources relied instead on appeasement, excuse, and clemency. Reviewers dispute where he draws the line (e.g., Cairns 2011).
- Nussbaum (*Anger and Forgiveness*, 2016) criticizes "transactional" forgiveness (confession, contrition, apology, conditional pardon) as an institutional Judeo-Christian inheritance, while recognizing unconditional strands within those traditions.
- Arendt (*The Human Condition*, 1958) called Jesus of Nazareth "the discoverer of the role of forgiveness in the realm of human affairs," framing forgiveness as the political remedy for the irreversibility of action.

**Interpretation.** If even the generic Western concept has a genealogy, then CAV's useful question is not "do people forgive?" but "*which institutional forms exist, what do they require, and what do they accomplish?*"

Sources were checked against publisher, journal, official, or institutional pages where those could be reached. ### 1.2 Working distinctions

**Working distinctions used below.**
- **Forgiveness:** the injured party gives up resentment or a claim to revenge.
- **Pardon:** an authority, heir, or deity remits punishment.
- **Reconciliation:** a relationship is restored.
- **Settlement:** compensation and ritual close a dispute, whatever anyone feels inside.

Many of the practices below fuse these categories, and that fusion is part of what makes them anomalous.

## 2. Part 1: Culturally particular forms of forgiveness

### 2.1 Hoʻoponopono (Native Hawaiian)

- **Term:** "setting to right" (*hoʻo*, to make; *pono*, right or balanced).
- **Context (Evidence).** The canonical source is Pukui, Haertig, and Lee, *Nānā I Ke Kumu*, Vol. 1 (1972). The book came out of a culture committee at the Queen Liliʻuokalani Children's Center, where Mary Kawena Pukui explained Hawaiian concepts to clinicians. Pukui defined hoʻoponopono as restoring "good relationships among family, and family and supernatural powers." She described taking part in it within her own ʻohana for decades.
- **Structure.** A senior family member or respected outsider leads, and all talk goes through that leader. The process runs:
  1. opening prayer (*pule*);
  2. statement of the problem, then each layer of grievance in turn, under a demand for absolute truthfulness;
  3. optional cooling-off periods (*hoʻomalu*);
  4. confession and repentance (*mihi*) answered by mutual release (*kala*) from the wrong (*hala*);
  5. closing prayer, after which the matter is sealed.
- **Who forgives whom:** everyone, mutually. **Requires:** presence, a leader, full truth, and closure.
- **How it differs from generic forgiveness:** collective, facilitated, and spiritually framed, with release that is *binding*.
- **Modern versions.** Clinical and social-work adaptations are documented in Shook (1985). Morrnah Simeona's "Self-I-Dentity through Hoʻoponopono" turned the process into an individual practice. The self-help version popularized by Vitale and Hew Len (*Zero Limits*, 2007) reduces it to a four-phrase mantra.
- **Flags.** The mantra version is not traditional hoʻoponopono, and marketing it as "ancient Hawaiian" misrepresents it. The account of practice before Pukui is thin **[uncertain]**.

### 2.2 Ubuntu-based restorative justice (Southern Africa)

- **Term:** *ubuntu* (Nguni), "humanness," often glossed by the proverb *umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu* ("a person is a person through other persons").
- **Context (Evidence).** The 1993 Interim Constitution's postamble declared "a need for understanding but not for vengeance, a need for reparation but not for retaliation, a need for ubuntu but not for victimisation," and required amnesty for politically motivated acts. Tutu's *No Future Without Forgiveness* (1999) made ubuntu the moral grammar of the TRC.
- **Who forgives whom:** victims were *invited*, not required, to forgive. The state granted amnesty in return for full disclosure.
- **How it differs from generic forgiveness:** personhood is relational, so forgiveness restores the offender's humanity and the community's, not only the victim's peace.
- **Flags (strong).** Gade (2011) traces written ubuntu discourse from 1846 and finds the ubuntu–proverb link appearing in writing only around 1993–95, during the transition. His 2012 interview study finds that South Africans of African descent disagree on what ubuntu means. Wilson (2001) argues that the TRC's "religious-redemptive" discourse served to legitimize the new state and ignored retributive justice practices in townships. **Interpretation:** ubuntu works better as a frame than as a bounded practice.

### 2.3 Amish forgiveness (Anabaptist, North America)

- **Terms:** *Gelassenheit* (yieldedness to God and community); *uffgevva* ("giving up").
- **Context (Evidence).** On 2 October 2006, twenty years before the date of this study, a gunman killed five girls at the West Nickel Mines Amish school in Pennsylvania and then himself. Amish neighbours expressed forgiveness to his family within days and attended his burial (Kraybill, Nolt & Weaver-Zercher 2007).
- **Who forgives whom:** victims' families and the community forgive the offender, and they reach out to the offender's family. **Requires:** nothing from the offender. Forgiveness is unconditional, anticipatory, and practiced long before any crisis.
- **How it differs from generic forgiveness:** a communal discipline rather than an individual therapeutic achievement. The authors separate it from legal pardon and from denial of grief.
- **Flags.** The episode is easily turned into an emblem. Concerns that expectations of forgiveness pressure abuse victims within closed communities have been raised in journalism **[uncertain: not verified in peer-reviewed sources]**.

### 2.4 Pashtunwali: *nənawāta* (nanawatai) and *badal*

- **Terms:** *nənawāta*, "entering [the house of the offended party]"; *badal*, "exchange."
- **Context (Evidence, Rzehak 2011).** Rzehak calls *nənawāta* "a ritual of forgiveness." The offender goes to the injured family's house to admit guilt, sometimes with a mullah, elders, or women of his family. In older forms he wore a cord around his neck and held grass in his mouth. The ritual draws on the duties of hospitality and asylum owed even to an enemy, and "a Pashtun is obliged to accept" it. Blood money (*khunbahā*) is paid, and once the ritual is accepted the feud must end. *Badal* covers reciprocity for good deeds as well as revenge; Western accounts often reduce it to blood feud. A proverb Rzehak cites praises a man who took revenge after a hundred years and still said he had been in a hurry.
- **Who forgives whom:** the victim's patrilineal group forgives the offender's group.
- **How it differs from generic forgiveness:** forgiveness is requested through self-humiliation, then *socially obligatory to grant*. What it restores is the balance of honour between groups.
- **Flags.** Rzehak criticizes military "human terrain" literature for turning Pashtunwali into countable tenets. Compensation could include giving girls in marriage, which the Taliban banned by decree in 1998 (Rzehak). Popular Western stories that mix sanctuary with nanawatai should be treated cautiously **[uncertain]**.

### 2.5 Jewish *teshuvah* and *mechilah*

- **Terms:** *teshuvah*, "return" (repentance); *mechilah*, the injured party's forgoing of a claim.
- **Context (Evidence).** Mishnah Yoma 8:9 holds that Yom Kippur atones for wrongs against God, but not for wrongs between people until the wronged person is appeased. Maimonides (*Hilkhot Teshuvah* 2:9–10) sets out the procedure. The offender makes restitution and asks forgiveness. If refused, he returns with three companions, up to three times, after which the burden passes to the person refusing. The injured party is told not to be cruel and to forgive wholeheartedly once sincerely asked.
- **Who forgives whom:** *only* the injured person.
- **How it differs from generic forgiveness:** conditional, procedural, and *non-transferable*. That rule frames the debate over proxy forgiveness for crimes against the dead, as in Wiesenthal's *The Sunflower*.
- **Flags.** Practice varies across communities. Source strength is high because the norms are codified.

### 2.6 Christian pardon traditions

- **Terms:** Greek *aphesis*, "release."
- **Context (Evidence).**
  - Core texts: the Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:12, 14–15); forgiving "seventy-seven times" or "seventy times seven," depending on translation (Matthew 18:21–22); Luke 23:34.
  - Catholic penance requires contrition, confession, and satisfaction (Catechism §1450ff.).
  - Collective church repentance: John Paul II's "Day of Pardon" (12 March 2000), prepared by the International Theological Commission's *Memory and Reconciliation*.
  - The Polish bishops' 1965 letter to the German bishops, "we forgive and ask for forgiveness," was a landmark of intergroup forgiveness. Poland's communist government attacked it.
- **How it differs from generic forgiveness:** anchored in divine forgiveness, *mandated* rather than optional, and in Catholic practice mediated sacramentally.
- **Flags.** The tradition is plural. Treating it as the template for all forgiveness is the bias CAV's frame is meant to correct.

### 2.7 Islamic *ʿafw*, *ṣulḥ*, and *muṣālaḥa*

- **Terms:** *ʿafw*, "effacement" or pardon; *ṣulḥ*, settlement; *muṣālaḥa*, reconciliation.
- **Context (Evidence).** Qur'an 42:40: "the retribution for an evil act is an evil one like it, but whoever pardons and makes reconciliation, his reward is [due] from Allah" (Sahih International). Qur'an 2:178 permits equal retaliation (*qiṣāṣ*) for homicide while praising pardon by the victim's kin, with compensation (*diya*).
- **Who forgives whom:** in homicide, the *victim's heirs*, not the state, hold the right to pardon.
- **How it differs from generic forgiveness:** pardon is a legal right of the injured party, coupled with compensation, and religiously praised but not compelled.
- **Flags.** Coercion of poor families and unequal blood-money valuations in modern state practice are reported concerns **[uncertain: specific studies not verified]**.

### 2.8 Arab *sulha* (Levant)

- **Context (Evidence).** Irani and Funk (1998) describe *sulh* and *musalaha* as communal rituals of mediation and arbitration aimed at restoring harmony and honour rather than punishing individuals. Pely (2011) compares sulha among Palestinian citizens of Israel with Western mediation. He finds it differs on confidentiality, venting, and neutrality, and above all in the central role of *honour*. Typical elements are a delegation of notables (*jaha*), a truce with a good-faith payment (*ʿatwa*), and a public handshake and shared meal.
- **Who forgives whom:** the victim's clan forgives the offender's clan, through mediators.
- **How it differs from generic forgiveness:** public, lineage-based, and honour-restoring. The ceremony can come before inner change.
- **Flags.** Patriarchal structure; it often runs alongside state prosecution. "Musalaha" is also the name of a Christian reconciliation NGO, so the terms should not be confused.

### 2.9 Acholi *mato oput* (northern Uganda)

- **Term:** "drinking the bitter root" (of the *oput* tree).
- **Context (Evidence).**
  - The Liu Institute's *Roco Wat I Acoli* (2005) describes it as a process for killings within or between related clans: elders mediate, the killing is voluntarily acknowledged, compensation is paid, and both clans drink a bitter brew.
  - A 2007 survey of 2,875 adults (Pham et al.) found that 57% wanted returning LRA members to take part in traditional ceremonies.
  - Mato oput was the ceremony most often named (48%), but rarely in the non-Acholi Lango (9%) and Teso (16%) regions.
  - Given four options, only 8% preferred "peace with traditional ceremonies."
- **How it differs from generic forgiveness:** clan-to-clan, with truth and compensation *required*, sealed in a shared bodily act.
- **Flags (strong).** Tim Allen (2006, 2007) argues that mato oput was romanticized and promoted, partly with donor support, as an alternative to the ICC. He notes that it was designed for disputes between known parties, not mass atrocity, and that promoting it reinforced stereotypes of Acholi people as naturally forgiving. Baines (2007) is more sympathetic but also documents its limits.

### 2.10 Albanian blood-feud reconciliation under the Kanun

- **Terms:** *gjakmarrja*, "blood-taking"; *besa*, sworn word or truce; to "forgive the blood" (Alb. *falja e gjakut* **[uncertain: spelling to be confirmed by an Albanian speaker]**).
- **Context (Evidence).** The *Kanun of Lekë Dukagjini* (compiled by Gjeçovi; trans. Fox 1989) institutionalizes revenge but also truces, protected mediators, and reconciliation, ending in a shared "blood meal." In 1990–92 a student-led movement, fronted by folklorist Anton Çetta, revived the older practice of mass reconciliation in Kosovo. Estimates of feuds reconciled range from about 1,200 (Mangalakova 2004) to 2,000–2,500 (Luci 2014). Families pardoned the blood "in the name of the youth, the people and the flag" (Clark 2001, quoted in Marsavelski et al. 2016).
- **How it differs from generic forgiveness:** a public renunciation of a *duty* to kill, often dedicated to a collective cause.
- **Flags.** The Kanun is patriarchal. Feud statistics are unreliable (Alston 2010).

### 2.11 Japanese apology–pardon practices (included with caution)

**Evidence.** Wagatsuma and Rosett (1986) compare how apology works socially and legally in Japan and the United States. Haley (1989) describes a "second track" in Japanese criminal justice in which confession, repentance, compensation, and the victim's pardon bring leniency. Critics note its paternalistic and coercive features.

**Interpretation.** The documented material concerns *apology-centred* institutions rather than a distinct forgiveness concept. Popular claims about specific Japanese "forgiveness words" were not verified and are omitted.

## 3. Part 2: Forgiveness, its absence, and geopolitics

### 3.1 South Africa's TRC (1996–2003)

**Evidence.**
- The Human Rights Violations Committee received more than 21,000 victim statements, and about 2,000 victims testified publicly.
- The Amnesty Committee received 7,112 applications and granted 849.
- Gibson's 2001 national survey (n > 3,700) found that accepting the TRC's account of the past was associated with more reconciled racial attitudes. Interracial contact also independently predicted reconciliation. Gibson reports that the relationships differed by race and were weaker among Black respondents (Gibson 2004a, 2004b).
- His 2002 study found broad opposition to amnesty in principle, but perceptions of fair procedure and restorative elements made amnesty decisions more acceptable.
- The 2023 Reconciliation Barometer reports more interracial interaction among Black South Africans than in the early 2000s, alongside low overall interracial trust.

**Interpretation.** The TRC produced truth acceptance and a globally influential model, but not forgiveness as such. Forgiveness was invited, and Wilson (2001) argues the redemptive discourse sidelined victims who wanted justice. Gibson's reading is that the TRC's even-handed "all sides had dirty hands" truth made reconciliation easier, which is a political finding, not a moral endorsement of forgiveness.

### 3.2 Franco-German reconciliation

**Evidence.**
- Milestones: the Schuman Declaration (1950), the Élysée Treaty (22 January 1963), and the Franco-German Youth Office (1963). The Office reports about 10 million participants in more than 400,000 programmes.
- Feldman (2012) presents Germany's reconciliation with France, Israel, Poland, and the Czech Republic as deliberate policy built on leadership, institutions, symbolic gestures, and civil-society ties.
- Krotz and Schild (2013) call the relationship "embedded bilateralism."
- Lind (2008) argues that reconciliation advanced in the 1950s–60s while West German contrition was still modest, and that Japanese apologies often triggered domestic backlash that undercut them abroad.
- Luttwak (1994) credits Moral Re-Armament's Caux meetings, which emphasized personal repentance among French and German elites.

**Interpretation.** This is the strongest case of enemies becoming partners, but the causal weight of forgiveness is hard to isolate. Luttwak's claim is difficult to test. The best-supported mechanisms are institutional and symbolic. For CAV, forgiveness looks like one *ingredient* of reconciliation infrastructure, and sustained *encounter* (youth exchange) may carry it across generations better than declarations do.

### 3.3 Rwanda's gacaca courts (2002/2005–2012)

**Evidence.**
- *Gacaca* takes its name from the Kinyarwanda word for the grass where disputes were traditionally heard. It ran through 12,103 courts with about 169,000 elected lay judges.
- By its closure on 18 June 2012 it had tried 1,958,634 cases involving 1,003,227 people. About 86% of cases ended in conviction, and about 13% of convictions rested on guilty pleas or confessions, which earned reduced sentences.
- Clark (2010) finds real achievements in justice and some reconciliation.
- Ingelaere (2016), drawing on 1,917 observed trials, finds a drift from confession and restoration toward accusation and retribution.
- Brounéus found retraumatization and threats among women witnesses (2008, n = 16). Her survey of 1,200 Rwandans found that witnesses had higher depression and PTSD than non-witnesses after controls (2010).
- Pham, Weinstein, and Longman (2004) linked greater trauma exposure to less openness to reconciliation.
- HRW (2011) documented fair-trial deficits, intimidation, and the exclusion of RPF crimes.
- The government's 2020 Barometer reports 94.7% "reconciliation." Critics warn that self-reports in a tightly governed setting may be inflated.

**Interpretation.** Gacaca shows a state using a forgiveness-adjacent tradition at enormous scale, with confession traded for leniency. The result was historic accountability, contested evidence of durable forgiveness, and documented harm to witnesses.

### 3.4 Sierra Leone: the best causal evidence

**Evidence.** Cilliers, Dube, and Siddiqi (2016) randomized Fambul Tok's community reconciliation forums across 200 villages (2,383 respondents). The forums increased forgiveness of perpetrators, social networks, and contributions to public goods. They also *increased* depression, anxiety, and PTSD. Both kinds of effect persisted at 31 months.

**Interpretation.** This is the most important single finding for CAV. Forgiveness rituals can build social infrastructure while imposing individual psychological costs, so any diplomatic use needs trauma support built in.

### 3.5 Northern Ireland

**Evidence.**
- CAIN's Sutton Index records 3,532 conflict-related deaths (1969–2001).
- The 1998 Good Friday Agreement ended most political violence but left the legacy of the past unresolved.
- The 2023 Legacy Act's conditional immunity scheme was found incompatible with the European Convention on Human Rights by the Belfast High Court in February 2024.
- On 14 October 2025 the government introduced a Troubles Bill to repeal and replace the Act, together with a draft Remedial Order removing immunity **[uncertain: status as of October 2026 not verified]**.
- Hewstone, Cairns, and colleagues (2006) found intergroup contact positively related to out-group attitudes, perspective-taking, and trust, even among those with worse experience of the conflict, in a study of the predictors of intergroup forgiveness.
- After the 1987 Enniskillen bombing killed his daughter, Gordon Wilson said: "I bear no ill will. I bear no grudge."

**Interpretation.** Northern Ireland shows that a peace settlement can hold without a forgiveness settlement, and it shows the cost of that gap: decades of litigation and repeated failed legacy frameworks. The 2023 immunity scheme amounted to state "pardon without forgiveness," and it failed.

### 3.6 The Balkans

**Evidence.**
- The ICTY (1993–2017) indicted 161 people, sentenced 90, and acquitted 19.
- Orentlicher (2018) finds it delivered justice and spurred domestic prosecutions, but denial persisted in Bosnia and Serbia and its judgments were absorbed into competing ethnic narratives (see also Subotić 2009; J. N. Clark 2014).
- The RECOM regional truth commission initiative gathered more than half a million signatures but never won the intergovernmental backing it needed.

**Interpretation.** Retributive international justice neither produced nor was designed to produce forgiveness, and its effect on reconciliation in the communities most affected appears weak.

### 3.7 Feuds and revenge cycles

**Evidence.**
- Marsavelski, Sheremeti, and Braithwaite (2016) compiled Kosovo homicide series. Rates were already low during the nonviolent resistance and fell close to zero during the 1990–92 reconciliation campaign. Feuds resurged in 2000–04, then declined. The authors treat the causal link as plausible, not proven.
- In Albania, UN Special Rapporteur Alston (2010) found blood feuds in decline but still corrosive, chiefly through families' self-confinement, and found the statistics unreliable.
- For Pashtun *badal*, Rzehak (2011) documents exits that coexist with norms allowing revenge to be delayed across generations: *nənawāta*, blood money, confiscation of weapons during talks (*baramta*), and peacemaking jirgas.

**Interpretation.** Revenge systems usually contain built-in exits. The real question is whether those exits are honoured, resourced, and politically backed. In Kosovo a shared cause produced what one participant called "a contagion of reconciliation," and the gains partly reversed once the cause was gone.

## 4. Part 3: Has forgiveness been forgotten, and at what cost?

This study's title asks whether forgiveness is largely forgotten or no longer widely practiced. Asked about forgiveness in general, the evidence answers no. Asked about its thick, communal, ritual forms, a narrower version of the claim holds up.

### 4.1 Measurable evidence

**Evidence: no long-run survey series.** The U.S. General Social Survey asked "I have forgiven those who hurt me" only in 1998. Then, 46.3% said "always or almost always" and 37.9% "often." No trend can be computed. Toussaint et al. (2001) found that older adults reported more forgiveness than younger adults, but cross-sectional data cannot separate ageing from generational change.

**Evidence: corpus data show a U-shape, not a decline.**
- Kesebir and Kesebir (2012) found that 37 of 50 virtue words (74%) declined in American books between 1901 and 2000. "Forgiveness" was among the 50, but the paper reports the aggregate.
- This study's own Google Books Ngram query (English 2019 corpus, smoothing 3, run 2 October 2026) found "forgiveness" at about 8.5 per million words around 1850 and about 1.9 around 1930–50. It rose to about 3.1 by 1990, 5.3 by 2000, and 10.1 by 2019. The U.S. and U.K. sub-corpora show the same pattern.
- Corpus frequency is a weak proxy for practice, and the corpus changes composition over time (Pechenick, Danforth & Dodds 2015).

**Evidence: adjacent indicators are mixed.** Forgiveness research and trials have multiplied (Wade et al. 2014; Ho et al. 2024). Affective polarization rose most in the U.S. but fell in Germany and Britain (Boxell, Gentzkow & Shapiro 2024). Polarization measures animosity, not forgiveness.

### 4.2 Interpretation: what is defensible

1. **"Forgiveness has been forgotten," in general, is not supported.** Talk of forgiveness, therapeutic forgiveness, and self-reported forgiveness are common or rising in the Anglophone world.
2. **A narrower thesis is plausible but under-measured.** *Thick* forms, with specified roles, compensation, and binding closure, have lost institutional ground in many settings. Plausible drivers:
   - state monopolies on justice;
   - urbanization and weaker lineage ties (noted for gacaca's urban courts and for the Kanun);
   - suppression of customary law (the Kanun was banned under Yugoslav and Albanian communism);
   - market adaptation that strips practices of their communal structure (commercial hoʻoponopono).

   This rests on case evidence, not systematic measurement.
3. **Thin forgiveness may have displaced thick forgiveness.** The spread of individual, therapeutic forgiveness may be part of the shift, not evidence against it: forgiveness became a private emotional skill rather than a public institution. This is the most promising hypothesis for CAV, and the least tested.

### 4.3 Costs

**Evidence: costs of absent or failed exits from revenge.**
- Feud self-confinement in Albania, including children kept from school (Alston 2010).
- Renewed feud killings in Kosovo once the campaign's cause was gone (Marsavelski et al. 2016).
- Northern Ireland's serial legacy failures (2023 Act; 2024 ruling; 2025 replacement bill).

**Evidence: costs of forgiveness practices themselves.**
- Psychological harm to people who testify or forgive in public (Brounéus 2008, 2010; Cilliers et al. 2016).
- Coerced or instrumentalized forgiveness (Allen 2006, 2007; Wilson 2001; HRW 2011).

**Interpretation.** Lacking an exit from revenge has visible costs, but "more forgiveness" is not an unqualified good. The diplomatic value lies in *well-designed* forgiveness infrastructure: voluntary participation, victims' voice, compensation, trauma support, and binding closure.

## 6. Open questions and gaps

1. **Measurement.** No cross-national, long-run measure of forgiveness practice exists. CAV could propose survey items that separate thin from thick forms.
2. **Decline thesis.** The thin-displaces-thick hypothesis needs historical and ethnographic testing, for example case counts from sulha committees, Albanian reconciliation NGOs, or Acholi cultural institutions.
3. **Psychological cost.** Can rituals keep the social gains while reducing trauma, for instance by pairing them with counselling or REACH-type interventions? No such trial was found.
4. **Victim consent.** How do obligatory forms (*nənawāta*, Maimonidean limits on refusal) protect victims who are not ready to forgive?
5. **Gender.** Most thick forms put men in charge of mediation. Exceptions deserve study: women in the Kosovo campaign (Luci 2014) and older women in Pashtun feud resolution (Rzehak 2011).
6. **Consultation.** Entries for living Indigenous and religious practices should be reviewed by practitioners and community scholars before they enter the CAV catalog.
7. **Unverified items.** Heirs'-pardon coercion, Amish victim-pressure claims, and the Northern Ireland bill's current status.
8. **East Asian materials.** A review of Japanese, Korean, and Chinese-language scholarship is needed before any East Asian entry.
---

## 7. Conclusion: has forgiveness been forgotten?

Not in general. Talk of forgiveness has risen since 1980, self-reported forgiveness is common, and therapeutic forgiveness interventions have a solid and growing evidence base. What the evidence points to instead is a change in *kind*. The thick forms surveyed in Part 1, with their specified roles, witnesses, compensation, and binding closure, have lost institutional ground in many settings, while forgiveness has spread as a private emotional skill rather than a public institution. That answer rests on case evidence rather than systematic measurement, and testing it is among the open questions in §6.

The geopolitical record in Part 2 cautions against simple nostalgia. Exits from revenge matter, and their absence has visible costs. But forgiveness rituals can build social infrastructure while harming the people who testify or forgive, and forgiveness invited or imposed by states can sideline victims. For CAV, forgiveness is best understood not as a single universal virtue to be revived, but as a family of particular, often untranslatable institutions, each with its own requirements, achievements, and risks.
