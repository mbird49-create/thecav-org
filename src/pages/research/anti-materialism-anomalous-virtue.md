## Abstract

Is anti-materialism losing its status as a regional or global anomalous virtue? This study treats that as an open question. It defines anti-materialism as a held conviction that acquiring goods beyond sufficiency is harmful, expressed in restraint that is chosen rather than imposed, and treats the cost of that restraint as the strongest available marker of conviction. It then compares twelve culturally specific forms, from Pali santuṭṭhi and Jain aparigraha to Andean sumak kawsay and Nordic lagom, with US and global data on value priorities, wealth goals, vowed religious life, household debt, and newer forms such as minimalism, degrowth, and FIRE. The organizing hypothesis is that the virtue is migrating from thick, costly forms into thinner ones. The evidence supports only a diluted form of that hypothesis: values and policy sympathy travel, while costly practice survives mainly inside thick communities that pass it on through family and institutions.

**Keywords:** anti-materialism; sufficiency; renunciation; voluntary simplicity; post-materialism; vowed poverty; Amish plainness; sumak kawsay; Chronological Ethics; Semantic Ethics; Sacred Axiology

## Executive summary

1. **The question is treated as open.** "Anti-materialism" here means a *conviction* that acquisition beyond sufficiency harms the person or community, expressed in *chosen* restraint.
   - It is not poverty, austerity, thrift for accumulation, or Inglehart's post-materialism. Post-materialism is about value priorities, not consuming less.
   - The test is whether the virtue is **disappearing** or **migrating**. Commodified minimalism counts as evidence against migration.

2. **Young Americans' stated money goals rose and are still at or near record highs.** HERI's own tables (first-year college students, "essential" or "very important"):
   - "Being very well off financially": 42.2% (1966), 36.2% (1970 low), 74.1% (1987), 81.9% (2015), 84.3% (2019).
   - "Developing a meaningful philosophy of life": 85.8% (1967), 43.1% (1987), 39.3% (2003 low), 49.8% (2019).

3. **US value priorities moved slightly toward post-materialism.** In the GSS 4-item battery, post-materialists went from 16.4% (1993) to 23.0% (2021) and materialists from 19.1% to 13.5% (CAV computation).
   - Inglehart (2008) documents a long Western shift. Western Europe went from 4:1 materialists in 1970 to slightly more post-materialists than materialists by 2006. The US reached 2:1 post-materialist by 2006, on Inglehart's index.
   - This is a shift in *priorities*. It is not evidence of restraint.

4. **Costly, vowed renunciation is collapsing in the West and in parts of Buddhist Asia. It is holding or growing in a few communities.**
   - **US Catholic religious sisters:** 178,740 (1965) → 33,135 (2025), a fall of 81.5% (CARA).
   - **Women religious worldwide:** 1,004,304 (1970) → 589,423 (2023) (CARA, from Vatican data).
   - **Thai monks and novices:** 355,295 (2012) → 288,342 (2022) (Thai scholarly analysis of Office of National Buddhism data). Monks fell from 256,984 (2023) to 245,645 (2024) (NSO yearbook 2025).
   - **Korea's Jogye Order:** new monastics fell from 528 (2000) to 61 (2022). Source: Korean press and a 2023 *Religions* article [partly uncertain].
   - **Amish (plain living by binding communal discipline):** grew from 177,910 (2000) to 410,955 (2025) (Young Center). That is mostly natural increase with high retention.
   - **Shakers:** three members (2025, NPR).
   - **Jain ascetics:** large and female-majority. Flügel counts 6,843 Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka ascetics in 1999. Growth claims rest mainly on journalism [uncertain].

5. **Behavior does not reflect conviction in aggregate.** US household debt was $18.771T in Q2 2026, up $4.6T since end-2019 (NY Fed). It is driven by housing and credit conditions, so it cannot measure conviction.

6. **The "migrating" forms are thin.**
   - **Degrowth:** support for degrowth *policies* without the label can be high, 72–82% in UK and US experiments (Krpan, Basso, Hickel & Kallis 2025, *Lancet Planetary Health*). A self-identified degrowth cluster is small in the one cluster study found.
   - **Minimalism and FIRE:** adherent figures come from commercial polls [uncertain]. FIRE is frugality for accumulation.

7. **State-adopted anti-materialist terms show a credibility gap.**
   - **Ecuador:** sumak kawsay is in the 2008 Constitution (Art. 14; Art. 71 on rights of nature). The Yasuní-ITT initiative was ended by Executive Decree 74 on 15 Aug 2013 after raising about US$13.3M of a US$3.6B target. The 20 Aug 2023 referendum (58.95% Sí) has been only partly implemented: about 24% of wells closed by late 2026 [secondary].
   - **Bhutan:** the GNH Index rose 0.743 → 0.756 → 0.781 (2010/2015/2022), with living standards the most-improved domain. About 2% of the population received Australian education visas in FY2022–23 (*The Bhutanese*, secondary).

8. **Provisional verdict: partial migration with dilution.**
   - Costly forms are declining sharply in the Christian West, Thailand, and Korea. They are holding among the Amish and probably among Jains.
   - Thin forms (priorities, aesthetics, policy sympathy) are stable or growing.
   - Most exported forms (lagom, wabi-sabi décor, "GNH") count as evidence against migration.
   - Confidence: moderate for the US and the Catholic world; low to moderate elsewhere.

## 1. Definitions and boundaries

### 1.1 Working definition
**Anti-materialism** in this brief has three parts:
- (a) a held *conviction* that acquiring and possessing goods beyond sufficiency is harmful (spiritually, morally, socially, or ecologically);
- (b) *expressed* in restraint;
- (c) where the restraint is *chosen* rather than imposed.

The **cost** of the restraint is the strongest available marker of conviction: income forgone, career or marriage given up, or a binding vow.

### 1.2 Neighboring concepts it must be kept apart from

| Concept | Core | Overlap with anti-materialism | Why it is not the same |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asceticism | Disciplined self-denial for a spiritual end (food, sex, sleep, comfort) | Often includes poverty | Broader: one can be a wealthy ascetic in diet, or a poor non-ascetic |
| Frugality / thrift | Economical use of resources | Low consumption | Often instrumental (saving to accumulate). Weber's "this-worldly asceticism" serves accumulation [concept: Weber 1905] |
| Voluntary simplicity | Lifestyle reducing consumption for non-material goods | Chosen, low consumption | Usually low-cost, reversible, individual. Strongest secular candidate for "migration" |
| Minimalism (2010s) | Owning fewer, curated things | Fewer possessions | Often aesthetic and commodified (decluttering services, design goods). Counted as evidence *against* migration where it is rebranded consumption |
| FIRE | Financial Independence, Retire Early | High savings rates, low consumption | Restraint is a means to wealth and freedom from work, not a rejection of acquisition |
| Degrowth | Planned downscaling of production and consumption in rich countries | Collective sufficiency | A political-economic program. Support may be policy preference, not personal practice |
| Inglehart's post-materialism | Priority of voice and self-expression over economic and physical security | Lower *priority* on material security | **Measures value priorities about national goals. A post-materialist can be a heavy consumer.** Inglehart's theory predicts it rises *with* affluence |
| Materialism (consumer psychology) | Richins & Dawson (1992): possessions as a measure of success, central to life, and a path to happiness. Kasser: relative importance of extrinsic goals (money, image, status) | Its inverse | Low materialism scores are not proof of restraint in behavior |

**Interpretation (descriptive)** This brief needs *three* kinds of evidence, and the literature rarely provides all three for the same population:
- **values:** surveys of goals and priorities;
- **behavior:** consumption, saving, debt;
- **cost:** vows, careers forgone.

**Interpretation (normative)** CAV should reserve "anomalous virtue" for the conviction-plus-cost form. The thin forms may be admirable. But treating them as the same virtue would let lifestyle marketing borrow the moral prestige of vowed renunciation.

## 2. Thick, culturally specific forms

Each entry gives the native term, gloss, why it doesn't translate, practice and cost, evidence on decline, and cautions. Scriptural references are to standard editions. Where they were not re-checked in this study they are tagged.

### 2.1 Pali Buddhist contentment: santuṭṭhi (Pali; Sanskrit saṃtuṣṭi)
- **Gloss:** contentment, satisfaction with what one has.
- **Doesn't translate because:** English "contentment" implies a mood. Santuṭṭhi is a *trained disposition* tied to a rule.
  - The monastic is "content with robes to protect the body and almsfood to maintain the belly" (the standard description in the Sāmaññaphala Sutta, DN 2) [uncertain: exact wording not re-checked].
  - Dhammapada 204 calls contentment the highest wealth (*santuṭṭhiparamaṃ dhanaṃ*).
- **Practice and cost:**
  - Monks hold no money under the Vinaya rule on gold and silver (Nissaggiya Pācittiya 18) [uncertain: rule number not re-checked]. They depend on alms and own four requisites.
  - Lay followers practice contentment within household life. Temporary ordination is common in Thailand, which lowers the cost signal of ordination counts.
- **Evidence on decline:**
  - **Thailand.** The NSO *Statistical Yearbook Thailand 2025*, Table 4.1, sourced from the Office of National Buddhism, gives:
    - 2023: 43,522 temples, 256,984 monks, 33,624 novices
    - 2024: 44,035 temples, 245,645 monks, 31,873 novices
  - These exclude the Chinese and Annamese nikāyas.
  - A Thai-language study in *Dhammadhara* (TCI journal) reports monks plus novices falling from 355,295 (2012) to 288,342 (2022), with the steepest falls in the Northeast. In the same period temples rose (43,471 in 2022).
  - Boonnoon (*Chulalongkorn Journal of Buddhist Studies*, 2013) finds that from 1999 to 2006 monks fell 6.30% and novices 35.87%. He attributes this to free public schooling, a changing rural labor market, and smaller families, explicitly "not … economic factors" in the narrow sense.
  - **Korea (Jogye Order, Mahāyāna but analogous renunciation).** New monastics: 528 (2000), 287 (2010), 131 (2020), 61 (2022). Sources: Korean press (천지일보, 매일경제) and the Korean Christian research outlet Mokhoe Data; context in a 2023 *Religions* 14(3):357 article on Jogye postulant education [uncertain: exact counts via secondary]. The Order has set a goal of 150 ordinations a year [uncertain].
  - Myanmar, Sri Lanka, and Taiwan: no verified time series retrieved [gap].
- **Cautions:**
  - Thai ordination falls are partly *imposed-restraint in reverse*. Novicehood historically served as free education for poor rural boys, so its decline tracks public schooling, not loss of conviction.
  - Lay santuṭṭhi has no survey measure.

### 2.2 Jain non-possession: अपरिग्रह aparigraha (Sanskrit/Prakrit)
- **Gloss:** non-possession; non-grasping.
- **Doesn't translate because:** it is the fifth of the five great vows (mahāvrata). Classical definition treats possession as *attachment* (mūrcchā), not just ownership (Tattvārtha Sūtra, ch. 7) [uncertain: verse number].
  - The vow binds inner states and outer objects together.
  - It extends to clothing among Digambara monks (sky-clad), while Śvetāmbara ascetics keep a minimal set of white robes, bowl, and whisk.
- **Practice and cost:**
  - *Dīkṣā* (initiation) is lifelong renunciation of property, family, and fixed residence, with travel on foot. It is among the highest-cost anti-materialist commitments documented.
  - Lay Jains take limited vows (*parigraha-parimāṇa*, a self-set limit on possessions) [concept standard; uncertain: survey of uptake].
- **Evidence on decline:**
  - **Scholarly counts.** Flügel, "Demographic Trends in Jaina Monasticism" (in *Studies in Jaina History and Culture*, Routledge 2006):
    - Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka orders in 1999: 6,843 ascetics (1,489 sādhus, 5,354 sādhvīs). The Tapā Gaccha alone had 6,027.
    - Sthānakavāsī ascetics in 2002: 3,331 (559 sādhus, 2,772 sādhvīs).
    - Flügel's broader finding is that mendicant numbers rose substantially in the 20th century, driven by female ascetics.
    - [Partly uncertain: figures taken from search excerpts of the chapter; the PDF on the SOAS repository returned errors in this study and was not opened.]
  - A 1995 estimate gives about 10,030 Śvetāmbara ascetics including 7,997 sādhvīs [uncertain: secondary compilation].
  - **Journalism.** BBC (2019) reports diksha rising from about 10–15 a year to about 250 in 2018 [uncertain: community sources; base unclear]. Times of India (2015) reports a "record" year [uncertain].
- **Cautions:**
  - Jain ascetic growth coexists with a highly commercially successful lay community. The tradition explicitly pairs lay wealth with ascetic renunciation, so it is not "the community is anti-materialist."
  - Female-majority renunciation may partly reflect gendered constraints (marriage pressures, family decisions), which complicates "chosen."
  - Ceremonial dīkṣā processions can be lavish displays, an ironic materiality.

### 2.3 Gandhian aparigraha
- **Gloss:** non-possession as a political and economic ethic.
- **Doesn't translate because:** Gandhi fused the Jain and Hindu vow with anticolonial economics: khadi (homespun), swadeshi (local production), and "trusteeship" of wealth. It is simultaneously a personal vow and a national program.
- **Practice and cost:** aparigraha is among the ashram observances (Gandhi, *From Yeravda Mandir*, 1930s) [uncertain: edition]. Ashram members gave up property.
- **Evidence on decline:** no measurable successor movement identified [gap]. Khadi survives as a state-supported industry and fashion label [uncertain: no figures retrieved].
- **Cautions:** Gandhi iconography is used heavily in Indian state and commercial branding. The ethic and the brand diverge.

### 2.4 Christian vowed poverty and Franciscan *altissima paupertas*
- **Native terms:** Latin *paupertas* (poverty) as one of the evangelical counsels (poverty, chastity, obedience); Franciscan *altissima paupertas* ("most high poverty").
- **Doesn't translate because:** it is a canonical *vow*, a legal status in canon law. The Franciscan version once claimed that friars owned nothing even collectively. Pope John XXII's bull *Cum inter nonnullos* (1323) declared heretical the claim that Christ and the apostles owned nothing, which shows the concept's radicalism.
- **Practice and cost:** lifetime vows. Members hold no personal property (or none in use without permission), with celibacy and obedience. The cost is lifetime income and family.
- **Evidence on decline (primary: CARA *Frequently Requested Church Statistics* workbook, downloaded 13 Oct 2026):**

| Religious sisters | 178,740 | 126,517 | 79,814 | 57,544 | 48,546 | 41,357 | 33,135 |
| Religious brothers | 12,096 | 7,941 | 5,662 | 4,690 | 4,200 | 3,801 | 3,290 |
| Religious priests | 22,762 | 22,771 | 15,092 | 12,811 | 11,710 | 10,308 | 10,279 |

| Worldwide | 1970 | 1990 | 2010 | 2020 | 2023 |

- Calculations:
  - US sisters fell 81.5% from 1965 to 2025: (178,740 − 33,135) / 178,740.
  - Worldwide sisters fell 41.3% from 1970 to 2023.
- **Regional distribution (CARA input from Vatican ASE 2022), religious sisters:**

- **Cautions:**
  - Decline in vowed life has many causes: secularization, expanded lay careers for women after Vatican II, and institutional scandal. It is not solely a decline of anti-materialist conviction.
  - Vowed poverty historically also gave women education and careers, an *imposed-to-chosen* ambiguity like Thai novicehood.

### 2.5 Islamic renunciation and contentment: زهد zuhd; قناعة qanāʿa
- **Gloss:**
  - *zuhd*: renunciation, detachment from the world (*al-dunyā*);
  - *qanāʿa*: contentment with what God has apportioned (*rizq*).
- **Doesn't translate because:**
  - Zuhd is primarily an *inner* detachment. Many classical authorities hold that a wealthy person can be a zāhid if wealth is "in the hand, not the heart" [concept: widely attributed; uncertain on attribution].
  - Qanāʿa is contentment framed theologically (trust in provision, *tawakkul*), not a lifestyle choice.
  - The Qur'an describes worldly life as "play and amusement" and competition in wealth (Q 57:20). A widely cited hadith says "richness is not abundance of goods but richness of the soul" (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, Kitāb al-Riqāq) [uncertain: hadith number].
- **Classical sources for the practice:**
  - The genre of *Kitāb al-Zuhd* collections (Ibn al-Mubārak, Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal, Ibn Abī al-Dunyā) [concept standard; editions not checked].
  - Al-Ghazālī's *Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn*, which devotes a book to poverty and renunciation (*Kitāb al-faqr wa-l-zuhd*) [uncertain: book number 34].
- **Practice and cost:** mostly lay disposition; there is no formal vow. Historic renunciant figures (e.g., early ascetics of Basra) preceded organized Sufism [uncertain].
- **Evidence on decline:** no quantitative series exists that this research could find [gap]. WVS religiosity items do not measure zuhd.
- **Cautions:** "Zuhd" appears in modern Islamic self-help and preaching markets [uncertain: no data]. Imposed poverty in poor Muslim-majority countries must not be read as zuhd.

### 2.6 Sufi poverty: فقر faqr (Arabic); درویش darvīsh (Persian)
- **Gloss:** spiritual poverty; existential neediness before God. *Faqīr* and *darvīsh* both name the poor one.
- **Doesn't translate because:**
  - Faqr is ontological (the creature *is* needy; Q 35:15, "you are the poor ones in need of God") before it is economic.
  - The saying "poverty is my pride" (*al-faqr fakhrī*) is popular in Sufi literature but considered weak or fabricated by hadith critics [uncertain: source of the critique not retrieved]. That is a caution about romanticized quotation.
- **Practice and cost:** order membership (*ṭarīqa*), dependence, itinerancy in some lineages, communal lodges. The cost varies widely.
- **Evidence on decline:** no series found [gap]. Order membership is rarely counted.
- **Cautions:** Western popular reception (Rumi quotation culture) detaches faqr from its Islamic frame. Heavy commercialization of "Rumi" [qualitative; uncertain].

### 2.7 Chinese "knowing enough": 知足 zhī zú
- **Gloss:** knowing what is enough; folk proverb 知足常乐 (contentment brings lasting happiness).
- **Doesn't translate because:** in the Daodejing it is a *strategic and cosmological* claim about safety and sufficiency, not a mood:
  - ch. 44: 知足不辱 ("know contentment and you suffer no disgrace");
  - ch. 46: 知足之足常足 ("the sufficiency of knowing sufficiency is lasting sufficiency").
- **Practice and cost:** a lay disposition; no vow. Buddhist and Daoist monastics (China and Taiwan) are the institutional form [no counts retrieved; gap].
- **Evidence on decline:**
  - WVS-based research (Chen et al., 2021, Strathclyde repository) describes China as largely materialist, with younger cohorts more post-materialist.
  - A 2025 *Social Indicators Research* age-period-cohort analysis (US, Japan, Türkiye, China) finds period effects can dominate cohort replacement, permitting materialist reversals.
  - "Lying flat" (躺平 tǎng píng) since 2021: one 2024 survey of 5,683 Chinese college students found 69.21% reported lying-flat behavior; another youth study found 11.1% likely to engage. A 2021 Shanghai survey (Hakuhodo HILL) found about 80% of 18–21-year-olds still wanted to succeed [all uncertain: abstracts or secondary].
- **Cautions:** tang ping is widely read as withdrawal under *constraint* (youth unemployment, housing costs, overwork). Under the CAV evidence standard, constraint-driven restraint is **not** anti-materialism, so it cannot confirm "migration" without evidence of conviction.

### 2.8 Japanese 侘寂 wabi-sabi and もったいない mottainai
- **Gloss:**
  - wabi-sabi: the beauty of austere, impermanent, imperfect things;
  - mottainai: regret or reproach at waste, at failing to honor a thing's worth.
- **Doesn't translate because:**
  - Wabi-sabi is an aesthetic-religious complex from the tea ceremony and Zen, not a lifestyle rule.
  - Mottainai has Buddhist overtones of respect for the value inherent in things (the word's root denotes something "unbecoming" to a thing's essence) [uncertain: etymology not re-checked].
- **Practice and cost:** tea practice (wabi-cha); repair (kintsugi); household thrift. Costs are low.
- **Evidence on decline:** no national series found [gap].
- **Commodification and re-use:**
  - **Wabi-sabi** entered Western design culture largely through Leonard Koren, *Wabi-Sabi for Artists, Designers, Poets & Philosophers* (1994). It is now a common interior-design style label. Selling "wabi-sabi" goods is commodified consumption and counts as evidence *against* migration [qualitative].
  - **Mottainai** was adopted by Kenyan Nobel laureate **Wangari Maathai** after a 2005 visit to Japan. She made it a global environmental slogan (reduce, reuse, recycle, plus respect), promoted at the UN. The Mainichi Shimbun launched the MOTTAINAI campaign with her (mottainai.info campaign history; MOFA Japan 2008 summit materials).
  - Normatively, this is a *legitimate* translation that kept the anti-waste core. But it reframes a reverence concept as recycling policy.
  - Marie Kondo's KonMari method (Japanese 2011, English 2014) is a further case: tidying popularized as "Japanese minimalism," with a product line [uncertain: dates and line not re-checked].

### 2.9 Anabaptist plainness: Gelassenheit, "plain" (Amish, Shakers)
- **Gloss:** *Gelassenheit*: yieldedness, submission to God and community; "plain" dress and life.
- **Doesn't translate because:** plainness is enforced by the *Ordnung* (unwritten community rules), not chosen item by item. Individual "simplicity" misses its communal, submissive core.
- **Practice and cost:**
  - Limits on technology, cars, and dress.
  - Leaving after baptism can bring shunning (*Meidung*).
  - The cost is binding; for Shakers it included celibacy and communal property.
- **Evidence:**
  - **Amish (Young Center, Elizabethtown College, "Amish Population Profile, 2025"):** 410,955 in North America (June 2025), up from about 177,910 in 2000 (+131%). There are 3,114 districts, and the population doubles about every 20 years. The US state table totals 404,575.
  - **Shakers:** three members at Sabbathday Lake, Maine, the last active community (NPR, 19 Aug 2025, a new member raising the count from two). The Shaker peak was 3,842 members in 1850 (Bainbridge 1984, as cited in the Addendum) [uncertain: source seen only in secondary citation].
- **Interpretation (descriptive):**
  - Amish growth is mostly **fertility and retention**, not conversion. It shows that a thick form can *persist and grow* when embedded in family and community institutions.
  - Shaker extinction shows the opposite for a celibate form that depends on recruitment.
- **Cautions:**
  - "Amish-made" furniture, tourism, and Amish business success (often substantial) commodify plainness [qualitative].
  - Amish plainness is collective and coercive in a way the CAV evidence standard must flag. It is chosen at baptism, by free adult choice with an open back door, and enforced by discipline thereafter. See the [Addendum on Amish commercialization](#addendum-amish) below for the commercialization question.

### 2.10 Andean good living: sumak kawsay (Kichwa); suma qamaña (Aymara); buen vivir
- **Gloss:** fullness of life in community and with nature.
- **Doesn't translate because:** it is a relational cosmology (community, ancestors, Pacha Mama), not a standard of living. Translating it as "well-being" or "good life" individualizes it.
- **Practice:** Indigenous communal economies. Since 2008 it has also been constitutional doctrine in Ecuador (and, as *suma qamaña*, in Bolivia's 2009 constitution [uncertain: not re-checked]).
- **Evidence (primary):**
  - **Constitution of Ecuador 2008** (Registro Oficial 449, 20 Oct 2008):
    - Art. 14 recognizes the right to a healthy, ecologically balanced environment that guarantees sustainability and *buen vivir, sumak kawsay*.
    - Art. 71 grants Nature (*Pacha Mama*) the right to integral respect for its existence and the maintenance and regeneration of its life cycles. Anyone can demand enforcement.
  - **Yasuní-ITT initiative:**
    - Proposed by President Correa in 2007: leave about 846 million barrels in the ground in exchange for US$3.6B over 12 years.
    - Ended on **15 Aug 2013** by Executive Decree 74, which liquidated the trust funds. Correa said "el mundo nos ha fallado" ("the world has failed us").
    - About US$13.3M had been deposited and about US$116M pledged (BBC Mundo, El Universo, El Telégrafo; Correa cited 0.37% of the target). PBS reported "$336 million raised," an inconsistency [uncertain; deposits vs. pledges].
  - **2023 referendum (20 Aug 2023; CNE final results):** 58.95% Sí (5,541,585) to 41.05% No (3,859,507), to keep Block 43-ITT oil underground indefinitely.
  - **Implementation:**
    - Petroecuador began closures in Aug 2024 (Reuters).
    - By late 2026 about 58–59 of 246 wells (about 24%) were closed, and Block 43 still produced about 40,000 barrels/day.
    - The government plan runs to Dec 2029 for wells and 2034 for decommissioning.
    - A compliance action is pending at the Constitutional Court.
    - Sources: Lupa Media, Primicias, GK (Aug 2026), Amnesty (Aug 2026) [secondary journalism; uncertain on exact counts].
- **Interpretation (descriptive):** a constitutionalized anti-extractive virtue was subordinated to oil revenue for a decade. Then a popular vote reasserted it, but implementation lags.
- **Cautions:**
  - State co-optation: "buen vivir" was used as branding for a development plan financed by extraction (Plan Nacional para el Buen Vivir) [uncertain: no primary document opened].
  - Indigenous scholars criticize romantic Western readings [qualitative].

### 2.11 Bhutan's Gross National Happiness
- **Native term:** Dzongkha *gyal yong ga kyid pel jor* (approximate romanization) [uncertain: transliteration and script not verified in this study].
- **Gloss:** Gross National Happiness, a development philosophy rooted in Vajrayāna Buddhism and formalized as a state index.
- **Doesn't translate because:** "happiness" in English suggests affect. GNH is a multidimensional sufficiency concept spanning psychological well-being, time use, community vitality, culture, governance, ecology, health, education, and living standards (nine domains).
- **Evidence (primary: Centre for Bhutan Studies / OPHI, *2022 GNH Survey Report*, released 22 May 2023):**
  - GNH Index: 0.743 (2010), 0.756 (2015), 0.781 (2022).
  - In 2022, 48.1% of people aged 15+ were "happy" (9.5% deeply, 38.6% extensively).
  - n = 11,052; response rate 96.6%.
  - **Living standards** (income, assets, housing) was the domain with the largest gain from 2010 to 2022.
- **Counter-evidence:** about 6,497 Australian visas went to Bhutanese in Jul–Dec 2022 (0.85% of 763,250 population), and about 15,552 education visas in FY2022–23, about 2.0% (*The Bhutanese*; secondary reporting of Australian data) [uncertain: visa grants ≠ departures].
- **Interpretation (descriptive):** GNH measures well-being, not restraint. Its rise is driven partly by *material* improvement.
- **Cautions:** "Bhutan, happiest country" is a common romanticization abroad. Bhutan's own index shows fewer than half classed as happy. The expulsion of Lhotshampa in the early 1990s is a frequently cited counterpoint [uncertain: no source opened].

### 2.12 Nordic modesty: Janteloven (Danish/Norwegian), lagom (Swedish)
- **Gloss:**
  - Janteloven: the "Law of Jante," roughly "don't think you are anything special";
  - lagom: "just the right amount."
- **Doesn't translate because:**
  - Janteloven is a *satirical* code of ten rules in Aksel Sandemose's novel *En flyktning krysser sitt spor* (1933), describing the coercive conformity of a small town.
  - Lagom denotes appropriateness and moderation, not anti-consumption.
- **Practice:** social norms against display. No vow, no cost marker.
- **Evidence on decline:** no series found [gap].
- **Cautions:**
  - **Janteloven is imposed leveling**, so it fails the "chosen" criterion.
  - **Lagom** was exported as a lifestyle trend around 2017 (several English-language lifestyle books) [uncertain: titles not verified]. It is commodified.

## 3. Global and US data

### 3.1 Inglehart's post-materialist value priorities

**Evidence: Inglehart's own trend publications**
- Inglehart, "Changing Values among Western Publics from 1970 to 2006," *West European Politics* 31(1–2), 2008:
  - In 1970, materialists outnumbered post-materialists **4:1** in six West European countries (Britain, France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, Netherlands), and 21:1 in the oldest cohort.
  - The US ratio was **3:1** materialist.
  - By 2006, post-materialists were "slightly more numerous than materialists in Western Europe" and **twice as numerous as materialists in the US**. Inglehart attributes the shift to cohort replacement and finds no life-cycle effect.
- In other writing (the WVS-based chapter "Changing Values in the Islamic World," U-Michigan repository; HSE LCSR lecture 2011), Inglehart reports:
  - materialist-to-post-materialist ratios of **55:1 in Pakistan, 28:1 in Russia, 1:2 in the US, 1:5 in Sweden**;
  - that the value shift had **stagnated in Western Europe**, with younger cohorts no longer substantially more post-materialist than the next older ones.
- Inglehart & Abramson (1994), *APSR* 88(2): controlling for inflation and unemployment, there is a significant trend toward post-materialism in all eight West European countries with data. Post-materialism increased in 18 of 20 societies over the preceding decade.
- Norris & Inglehart, "The Silent Revolution in Reverse" (*Perspectives on Politics*): an authoritarian-populist backlash. Not a materialism measure.
- **WVS waves 6 → 7:** the claim that post-materialists rose from 30.5% to 33.7% across 32 overlapping countries comes from a minor journal using a nonstandard definition **[uncertain]**. The WVS time-series file (doi:10.14281/18241.17) requires registration and was not downloaded [gap].

**Evidence: GSS (CAV computation)**
- Data: GSS cumulative 1972–2024 R3a, weight WTSSPS.
- Coding check: codes 1 = maintain order, 2 = more say in government, 3 = fight rising prices, 4 = protect free speech, confirmed against the SDA gss24rel3 codebook.
- Classification: materialist = both picks from {1, 3}; post-materialist = both from {2, 4}.

| Year | n | Materialist % | Mixed % | Post-materialist % |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1993 | 1,344 | 19.1 | 64.6 | 16.4 |
| 1994 | 1,198 | 21.1 | 62.3 | 16.5 |
| 2000 | 1,035 | 18.9 | 62.2 | 18.9 |
| 2010 | 1,183 | 19.7 | 62.0 | 18.3 |
| 2021 | 1,516 | 13.5 | 63.4 | 23.0 |

- **Caveats:**
  - The GSS shows roughly equal materialist and post-materialist shares in 1993–2010. That differs from Inglehart's "2:1 post-materialist in the US by 2006," which uses WVS samples and different wording or format. The discrepancy is unresolved [uncertain].
  - 2021 GSS fieldwork was mostly web-based. CAV's companion study, [Is American Virtue Declining, or Migrating?](/research/us-civic-virtue-decline), found post-2020 mode effects.
  - 2021 also preceded the 2022 inflation peak, which would raise "fight rising prices." This item is sensitive to period inflation, as Inglehart & Abramson note.

**Interpretation (descriptive)**
- In rich countries, value *priorities* moved toward post-materialism over 1970–2006, then stalled in Western Europe.
- In the US, the GSS suggests stability through 2010 and a mode-confounded rise in 2021.
- In poor and insecure countries, materialism dominates. Inglehart's theory predicts this.

**Interpretation (normative)**
- Post-materialism is the wrong yardstick for anti-materialism. Inglehart's own theory makes it a *product* of affluence, not a renunciation of it.
- Its rise is at most a *permissive condition* for anti-materialist conviction.

- Twenge & Kasser (2013), *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin* 39(7), analyzed 1976–2007.

- No comparable later Pew series was found [gap].

**Evidence: GSS RICHWORK (CAV computation)**
- Item: "If you were to get enough money to live as comfortably as you would like for the rest of your life, would you continue to work?" (employed respondents).
- Share who would continue: 70.3% (1973), peak 77.1% (1980), 72.6% (2018), 64.1% (2021), 66.0% (2024).

**Interpretation (descriptive)**
- Among young Americans, the 1970–1987 inversion of money and meaning is the single best-documented value shift in this brief.
- **"Being very well off" did not plateau after 1987.** It held at about 72–74% to 2004, then rose again to 84.3% (2019).
- "Meaningful philosophy" bottomed in 2003 (39.3%) and partly recovered (49.8% in 2019). Both rose together after 2003, which suggests students now endorse more goals overall (ceiling inflation) [interpretive; uncertain].

### 3.3 Materialism and well-being: Kasser, Dittmar et al.

**Evidence**
- **Dittmar, Bond, Hurst & Kasser (2014),** "The relationship between materialism and personal well-being: A meta-analysis," *JPSP* 107(5):879–924:
  - 753 effect sizes from 259 independent samples.
  - Mean r = −.15 (95% CI −.18 to −.13).
  - Stronger for materialist *values and beliefs* (r = −.19; ρ = −.24) and the *relative importance* of materialist goals (r = −.16; ρ = −.21). Weaker for money-focused measures alone (about −.08 to −.11).
  - The largest associations were with risky health and consumer behaviors and negative self-appraisals (about −.28 to −.44).
  - The paper reports moderators including age, gender, and cultural context [uncertain: moderator details not re-read].
- **Kasser & Ryan (1993, 1996)** established that relative emphasis on extrinsic aspirations (financial success) predicts lower well-being [standard citations; not re-opened]. **Kasser, *The High Price of Materialism* (MIT Press, 2002)** synthesizes the findings.
- **Twenge & Kasser (2013)** link generational rises in materialism to societal insecurity (see §3.2).

**Interpretation (descriptive)** The association is small to modest, consistent, and mostly correlational. It supports a *prudential* case for anti-materialism, not proof that anti-materialist virtue is causally beneficial.

**Interpretation (normative)** CAV can say anti-materialism has empirical support as a well-being strategy. But the virtue traditions in §2 ground it in non-prudential reasons: God, liberation, and community. Reducing it to well-being is itself a form of "migration": the virtue keeps its practice but changes its justification.

### 3.4 Richins Material Values Scale

**Evidence**
- Richins & Dawson (1992), *Journal of Consumer Research* 19(3):303–316: 18-item Material Values Scale (MVS) with success, centrality, and happiness subscales.
- Richins (2004), *JCR* 31(1):209–219: short forms (15, 9, and 3 items) [uncertain: page numbers not re-checked].
- **No population-level trend series using the MVS was found.** It is used in cross-sectional and experimental studies, and its use in Dittmar et al. contributes to the "values and beliefs" category.
- Trend claims about "rising materialism" therefore rest on HERI, MTF, and WVS, not the MVS [gap].

### 3.6 Minimalism, degrowth, FIRE, voluntary simplicity

**Evidence**
- **Degrowth:**
  - Krpan, Basso, Hickel & Kallis (2025), "Assessing public support for degrowth," *Lancet Planetary Health*: survey experiments in the UK and US found about **81–82% (UK) and 72–73% (US)** support for a full degrowth *policy package* when presented without the label.
  - Drews, Savin & van den Bergh (2019), *Ecological Economics* 157:141–155: opinion clusters (green growth, agrowth, degrowth) in a Spanish public sample (n = 1,004) and among researchers.
- **Minimalism:** a "17% of Americans identify as minimalists" figure appears on low-quality aggregator sites without a traceable poll **[uncertain; not used]**.
- **FIRE:** Intuit Credit Karma (company survey) reports that more than half of Gen Z respondents "consider themselves part of the FIRE movement," despite little or no savings **[uncertain: commercial poll, self-identification]**.
- **Voluntary simplicity:** no recent representative survey found [gap]. Academic literature dates back to Elgin's *Voluntary Simplicity* (1981) and Etzioni (1998) [standard citations].

**Interpretation (descriptive)**
- Policy sympathy for degrowth is broad but cheap: unlabelled, hypothetical, and costless to the respondent.
- FIRE self-identification is aspirational, and its goal is wealth.
- Minimalism's measurable footprint is largely commercial (books, services, design).
- None of these provides the conviction-plus-cost evidence the CAV evidence standard requires.

## 5. Hypothesis test: is the virtue migrating?

**Hypothesis (H-mig):** anti-materialism is not disappearing but migrating from traditional thick forms into new forms (minimalism, degrowth, FIRE, voluntary simplicity, post-materialist values).

**Confirm conditions**
- **C1:** New forms show growing adherence.
- **C2:** New-form adherents bear measurable cost.
- **C3:** Conviction, not constraint, drives the new restraint.
- **C4:** New forms are not chiefly commodified consumption.

**Refute conditions**
- **R1:** Thick forms decline without high-cost successors.
- **R2:** New forms are mainly products.
- **R3:** Wealth aspiration rises or stays high.
- **R4:** Apparent new restraint tracks economic constraint.

**Scoring (descriptive)**

| Condition | Result | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| C1 | Partial | Post-materialist priorities up (Inglehart; GSS 2021). Degrowth policy sympathy high. No adherent series for minimalism, FIRE, or simplicity |
| C2 | **Not met** | No cost data for new forms. FIRE's cost serves accumulation |
| C3 | Untested or negative | Tang ping is constraint-heavy. Degrowth support is hypothetical |
| C4 | **Not met** for wabi-sabi, lagom, KonMari-style minimalism | Qualitative |
| R1 | **Met** in Catholic West, Thailand, Korea, Shakers. **Not met** for Amish, Jains | CARA, NSO, Young Center |
| R2 | Partly met | Qualitative |
| R3 | **Met** (US) | HERI 84.3% (2019); MTF 62% (2005–07) |
| R4 | Partly met (China, Thai novices) | §2.1, §2.7 |

**Verdict on H-mig (descriptive):** **weakly supported in a diluted form.**
- The *value* travels: priorities, policy sympathy, a prudential well-being rationale.
- The *costly practice* does not travel into the new forms. Where it survives, it survives inside thick communities (Amish, Jains) that pass it on through family and institutions, not individual choice.
- Confidence: moderate (US, Catholic world); low (elsewhere).

## 9. CAV connections
- **Semantic Ethics:** semantic drift of lagom, wabi-sabi, mottainai, sumak kawsay, and GNH as they move from religious or communal vocabularies into marketing and state branding. Mottainai via Maathai is the positive case; wabi-sabi décor the negative.
- **Chronological Ethics:**
  - the 1970–1987 money/meaning inversion in HERI;
  - the 1965 peak and long decline of Western vowed poverty;
  - the 2013 → 2023 Yasuní reversal and re-reversal.
- **Sacred Axiology:** costly renunciation survives where it is sacralized and communal (Amish, Jains). Secular "migrated" forms lack sacral grounding and cost.
- **Ethno-Ethics:** each thick form is embedded in a particular community's structure. Export strips that embedding.
- **Catalog:**
  - [Amish forgiveness](/catalog/amish-forgiveness) is the closest live entry; Gelassenheit links forgiveness and plainness.

<h2 id="addendum-amish">Addendum: Has Commercialization Contradicted or Compromised Amish Plainness?</h2>

This Addendum extends §2.9 of the study above. It follows the same method: **Evidence**, **Interpretation (descriptive)**, and **Interpretation (normative)** are kept separate, with separate verdict columns. Behavior is kept apart from conviction, and chosen restraint from imposed restraint. Cost is measured, not just stated values. Every figure is cited. Figures CAV computed itself are labelled "CAV computation." Amish scholarship is mostly in books, so several book claims rest on publisher pages, reviews, the Young Center's free pages, or later scholars quoting them. Each is marked **[uncertain]**. Section numbers in the Addendum are prefixed with "A" (for example §A2.3).

### Addendum summary

1. **The question is open, and "commercialization" names two systems.** A is Amish-owned enterprise under each district's Ordnung. B is the non-Amish "Amish brand" market, which the Ordnung does not bind.
2. **System A is large and visibly regulated.** About 9,000 Amish-owned enterprises (range 8,600–12,000), and over half of households with non-farm primary income by 2009 (Kraybill, Nolt & Wesner 2010). Size caps, bans on litigation and owner-photo advertising, and costly technology workarounds are enforced, with excommunication as the backstop. The same authors report a three-tier class system, "Amish millionaires," and entrepreneurs who say contentment "means you lost it."
3. **System B is large, mostly outside Amish control, and acts on the Amish indirectly.** Lancaster County: 10.2 million visitors, US$2.7 billion direct spending in 2024. There is no "Amish" trademark, and the Amish do not sue. The documented harms are land prices and outmigration, media misrepresentation, and, in one 1992 Indiana dataset, higher defection near a tourist center.
4. **Persistence data show no collapse but cannot show plainness is intact.** Population 177,910 (2000) → 410,955 (2025) → 421,200 (2026); retention "85 percent or more" (Young Center), 84.46% at 40+ in the best peer-reviewed study. Growth is mostly fertility (TFR 6.1). Retention counts membership, not plainness, and groups that adopt cars leave the "Amish" count by definition.
5. **One pattern favors "compromise."** Retention is lowest among the New Order and in town-adjacent districts, and highest in the strictest affiliations. The occupation evidence is mixed (Meyers 1994: no father-occupation effect; an older study cited by Anderson & Thiehoff: farm fathers retained 97.2% versus 87.4%).
6. **Provisional verdict.** Plainness has been *reshaped*, not abolished: boundary markers persist, the egalitarian economic base has eroded. The external brand misrepresents and cheapens plainness but does not itself contradict it. This fits the main study's "partial migration with dilution."

### A1. Framing

#### A1.1 What "plainness" is, and what it is not
**Evidence**
- The Ordnung is a set of "district-specific regulations, usually unwritten." It applies "the biblical principle of separation from the world to issues such as clothing, use of mass media, technology, and leisure activities." Members affirm it twice a year before communion (Young Center, "Regulations").
- Old Order Amish forbid owning cars, drawing public-grid electricity, and owning televisions, radios and personal computers. They also forbid attending high school or college, military service, and initiating divorce (same page).
- The Young Center says limits "for the Amish, are a requirement for happiness. Without limits, they believe, individuals become arrogant, conceited, and self-destructive." It also says dress "signals submission to the collective order" and, unlike mainstream dress, is not an expression of personal preference.

**Interpretation (descriptive)**
- Plainness is *primarily a boundary-maintenance and humility discipline*. It limits display, technology, mobility and education. It does not, on the evidence here, set a ceiling on income or business success.
- That matters for the main study. "Anti-materialism" fits Amish practice only in part. The cost they bear is mostly communal submission (*Gelassenheit*) and restricted means, not renunciation of accumulation.

#### A1.2 What would count as "compromise" or "contradiction"
- **Contradiction** would be *behavior that the community's own stated norms prohibit*, practised without correction.
- **Compromise** would be *erosion of the practice's function*: humility, equality, separation.
- Using the main study's rule of cost over claims, each question has to be asked of observable things: firm size, technology rules and their drift, income and wealth spread, discipline, and who leaves.

#### A1.3 Two systems, kept apart
- **A** is owned by Amish members and bound by the district Ordnung, so restraint is visible and costly (size caps, technology detours). **B** is owned mostly by non-Amish and not bound by any Ordnung, so brand owners bear no restraint cost.

### A2. System A: community-internal enterprise

#### A2.1 Scale and the shift away from farming
**Evidence**
- **Number of enterprises.** Kraybill, Nolt & Wesner (2010), *Global Business and Economics Review* 12(1/2) (GBER), identified "approximately 9,000 Amish-owned enterprises in North America." Their note 1 calls this "a conservative estimate in a likely range of 8,600 to 12,000," derived from 1,725 districts × five to seven enterprises per district. No census of firms exists.
  - An earlier Young Center overview page (not re-fetched) said "some 12,000 Amish businesses," matching the top of that range. Its date is **[uncertain]**.
- **Non-farm share.** By 2009 "over half of the households received their primary income from non-farm sources" (GBER; note 8: derived from a sample of church directories).
- **Firm size and revenue.** Enterprises typically have fewer than a dozen employees, mostly Amish. Larger firms often exceed US$5 million in annual sales. Three to 15 businesses per district (GBER).
- **Local patterns.**
  - Elkhart-LaGrange, Indiana: farmers were 61% of household heads in 1970 and 37% in 1988; industry (largely RV and mobile-home factories) went from 26% to 43% (Meyers 1994).

**Interpretation (descriptive)**
- Commercial work is now the norm, not the exception. Olshan (1991, quoted by Moledina et al. 2014) put the issue sharply: the Amish's survival as a group is "much less problematic than their ability to perpetuate the values of an agrarian society once agriculture is no longer the foundation of economic life."
- The shift was driven substantially by economic constraint (land prices, mechanized competition). It is not simply an appetite for wealth. That keeps the chosen/imposed distinction open.

#### A2.2 Technology: negotiated exceptions for work
**Evidence**
- **The rule.** The Young Center says the Amish "use technology selectively," rejecting televisions, radios and personal computers outright. They believe technology "left untamed, will undermine worthy traditions and accelerate assimilation."
- **Workarounds in business (GBER §6.4).**
  - Electric motors are stripped from machinery and replaced with hydraulic or pneumatic motors, powered by diesel engines that pressurize oil or air.
  - The phone shanty outside a business is "the most widespread practice." Some change-minded groups allow office landlines and cell phones for contractors.
  - The authors say the detours "impede their efficiency and access to the outside world."
- **Cell phones and the Internet (Nolt 2015).**
  - Businesspeople argued phones had become "essential to their livelihood, especially during and after the 2009 recession," and that they were a variation on an older tradition.
  - After about 2007, Internet access changed the debate. The core objection to a personal device is that it undermines collective discernment ("You hold the whole world in your hand"). In 1910 Lancaster households had collectively disconnected home phones (Nolt 2015), so reversals are possible.
  - Business partners complicate the rule: if non-Amish partners hold 51%, the firm "might operate well outside the Ordnung," but the Amish partner "has had to give up significant control" (Nolt 2015).
- **Direction of travel.** "Of all the arenas of Amish life... enterprises are the first to employ or adapt new technology, which then gradually migrates to other sites," including homes and farms (GBER).
- **Variation.** The most traditional groups do not permit phones on Amish property. New Order groups permit more technology, and some regional "New New Order" districts permit smartphones (the New New Order detail comes from a search summary of an Amish studies page, not a page CAV read **[uncertain]**).

**Interpretation (descriptive)**
- The technology rules show *restraint with a price*: slower tools, outsourced services, a shared phone booth at the end of the lane. They also show a ratchet. Business use legitimizes a device first, and the legitimacy then spreads.
- Whether this is "compromise" depends on whether the function (separation from the world and from individualized media) is retained. The Internet-phone debate suggests the church treats that function as the line.

#### A2.3 Wealth held by Amish firms: what is actually published
**Evidence**
- **No aggregate figure exists.** CAV found no published estimate of total Amish business assets, net worth, or household wealth. The published numbers are revenue ranges ("often above US$5 million" for larger firms) and survival claims.
- **Survival.** GBER reports that the failure rate of Amish businesses "is less than 10% in the first five years," versus about 50% for US small businesses. Note 9 says the estimate rests on one settlement (Kraybill & Nolt 2004) plus interviews, and that church-appointed trustees "assume corrective control of struggling businesses," which "prevents some failures."
- **Income and land in one Holmes County tract.** Moledina, McConnell, Sugars & Connor (2014), *Journal of Amish and Plain Anabaptist Studies* 2(1), used 2006–2010 American Community Survey data, the Ohio Amish Directory, and county land records for one tract (about 80% "likely Amish," identified by language spoken at home).
  - An emergent two-peaked income distribution: 23% of tract households at about US$42,000, a dip to 10.6% at US$50,000–74,999, then a second peak of 15.9% at US$75,000–99,999. The authors say this "suggests two distinct income classes may be developing." They also note that the coefficient of variation is *not* significantly different from Ohio's, so their expectation of lower Amish dispersion was not confirmed.
  - Poverty rate 25.35% in the tract (Amish-identified population) versus 21.20% for all persons (US 13.21%). SNAP receipt was 1.6% in the tract versus 11.0% in Ohio. The authors caution that Census poverty lines may not fit barter, sharing and low-cost living.
  - Among 178 land-title holders, mean property value was US$269,270.50 and mean acreage 34.74.
  - Swartzentruber households (n = 55, found by a drive-through) held the most acreage (mean 60.69) and the most even distribution, versus 25.74 (Andy Weaver), 20.35 (Old Order) and 23.50 (New Order). Medians are far lower for the other three (4.00–7.94 acres), so those groups are bimodal: a few large holders and many small ones.
  - Limits: one tract near the tourist center of Berlin; about 20% non-Amish residents; land used as the wealth proxy.
- **Qualitative reports on wealth.** Church rank is positively associated with land wealth in the same tract (causation unclear).
  - GBER: "business owners have much more disposable income" under a "publicly uniform canopy." The term 'Amish millionaire' "is no longer an oxymoron." Entrepreneurs are "much more likely to hire non-Amish drivers, own rental property, take vacations, and own upscale homes (relatively plain by outside standards)."

**Interpretation (descriptive)**
- Published evidence supports *economic differentiation* inside Amish settlements. It does not support a claim of large aggregate wealth.
- The "millionaire" claim comes from Kraybill and colleagues' interview-based work and is not backed by a published count **[uncertain as to prevalence]**.

#### A2.4 Does commercial success strain the Ordnung?
**Evidence**
- **Constraints that bear costs (GBER §6).**
  - Business size: "five or six employees are the informal cap" in conservative groups; "more than 30 employees is considered too large" in change-minded ones. "Amish lore includes stories of business people who 'got too large' and were forced to sell by church leaders." An owner who refuses to downsize "may be excommunicated."
  - No litigation; no Sunday trade; no air travel; no alcohol, entertainment, electronic-communications, gambling or arts businesses; no TV or radio ads; no promotions featuring the owner's photograph.
- **Discipline.** Only baptized members can be excommunicated and shunned (Young Center, "Church Discipline"). GBER says "all violations of the cultural restrictions are serious," but "typically... church leaders seek compromises to avoid excommunicating entrepreneurs." **CAV found no published count of business-related excommunications [gap].**
- **Strain, per the authors.**
  - Enterprise brings "a rational worldview that accents planning, strategising, calculating ends versus means, and pursuing efficiency," which "undercut traditional values such as wisdom, patience, submission, and forgiveness." Individualism gains "some legitimacy" (GBER).
  - Owner-entrepreneurs "quietly bemoan the technological constraints that hold them back." The success of businesspeople "challenges, in subtle ways, the traditional sources of status, such as ordination to the ministry."
  - Kraybill & Nolt (2004) saw "the seeds of a three-class society consisting of farmers, entrepreneurs, and day laborers" and a growing tendency for ordained leaders in non-farming districts to be businessmen. Kraybill, Johnson-Weiner & Nolt (2013, p. 410) call owners "unbridled by church regulations" one of the most serious challenges, and a 2007 *Family Life* article, "The Big I," warned of rising individualism (all as quoted in Moledina et al.).
- **Two kinds of owner (GBER, citing Wesner 2010).** "Owner-managers" want steady income, stay small, and "mesh more comfortably with traditional virtues – humility, modesty, smallness, and informality." "Entrepreneurs" are "more competitive, take more risks... seek profits to reinvest." One said contentment "means 'you lost it, it's going downhill.'" Another: "We Amish try to think we're not competitive, but if you want to stay in business, you've got to be competitive!... [But] just because you're... competitive, doesn't mean you're not humble." The split between the two types is not quantified **[gap]**.
- **Variation by affiliation and place.**
  - The strictest groups are the least differentiated by land (Swartzentruber sample above). They refuse to appear in directories, so directory-based figures omit them.

**Interpretation (descriptive)**
- The Ordnung is acting as a *brake*, not an absent norm. Size caps, technology detours and ad limits are costly and enforced at least some of the time.
- The brake is leaky and unevenly applied. Evidence of strain is concentrated in (i) economic differentiation, (ii) growing legitimacy of individual initiative, and (iii) businessmen among ordained leaders.
- Several of these claims are the *authors' interpretations* of interviews. Only the Holmes County tract study (§A2.3) is quantitative, and it is a single place.

**Interpretation (normative)**
- The community's own internal critique ("The Big I," size caps, discipline) is the best evidence that the tension is *experienced as a contradiction by the Amish themselves*. CAV can treat that as stronger evidence than outsiders' impressions of hypocrisy.
- Not every commercial act contradicts plainness. A family firm that keeps work and children at home and stays small arguably serves *Gelassenheit*. A growth-seeking firm whose owner says contentment is failure arguably does not. The same evidence supports both readings.

### A3. System B: the non-Amish "Amish brand" market

#### A3.1 Tourism
**Evidence**
- **Lancaster County, 2024** (Discover Lancaster; Tourism Economics report, via FOX43, LancasterOnline, Central Penn Business Journal):
  - 10.2 million visitors;
  - US$2.7 billion direct spending;
  - 26,341 jobs supported;
  - US$3.5 billion total economic impact.
  - The report does not break out an Amish-specific share in the coverage CAV read **[uncertain]**. Lancaster's visitor economy includes outlets, restaurants and other attractions. A 2013 LA Review of Books essay (Weaver-Zercher) says "eleven million tourists" a year, an unreconciled difference.
- **Holmes County, Ohio.** Impact estimates conflict (about US$313 million versus about US$500 million), and both come from tourism-advocacy sources **[uncertain]**. Holmes County is 40.71% Amish, the highest share of any county (Donnermeyer, Anderson & Cooksey 2013, via Moledina et al.), and Berlin is its tourist center.
- **Shipshewana (Indiana) and Sarasota (Pinecraft).** Meyers (1994) describes Shipshewana's "thriving tourist industry." The Young Center's 2026 table lists Pinecraft with two districts and 100 residents. CAV could not verify visitor figures for either **[gap]**.
- **How much of Amish business depends on tourists.** In Kraybill & Nolt's Lancaster survey, 10% of Amish businesses reporting tourist sales said tourists account for more than half of their retail volume (Kraybill & Nolt 2004, p. 154, as quoted in Moledina et al. 2014). 

#### A3.2 Effects of tourism on Amish people
**Evidence**
- **Land and outmigration.** Lancaster's population "has risen by more than 100,000 since 1990," and a 60-acre farm "costs around $1.2 million, not including livestock or equipment" (Kraybill). The Young Center estimated Pennsylvania "lost hundreds of Amish families from 2008 to 2012" to western New York, Wisconsin, Kentucky and Tennessee. Kraybill: "The big danger is that the Amish here become so assimilated they end up not being Amish" (NPR The Salt, 2014). The same article credits development with generating the construction jobs many Amish men now hold. Samuel Lapp, a former Amish farmer near Intercourse, said he has "no animosity toward the non-Amish who are profiting from the Pennsylvania Dutch Country image" but that "They're not really representative of us Plain people."
- **Defection near the tourist center (Meyers 1994, Elkhart-LaGrange).**
  - Retention was 80% in town districts versus 86% rural. In Shipshewana-area congregations it was 66% versus 84% in other towns (Shipshewana n = 149; chi-square p < .001). A cluster table shows defection of 37% near Shipshewana versus 13% in the lowest cluster.
  - Shipshewana-area Amish "work in shops, restaurants, and the sale barn" or sell to tourists.
  - Meyers attributes the pattern to contact with outsiders and to a less restrictive Ordnung there, not to tourism alone. The data are 1980/1988 directories, so they are dated.
- **Other costs.** Kreps et al. (1997) on congestion and buggy accidents, and Testa (1992) on threats to "cultural authenticity," as summarized by Moledina et al. Not opened.
- **Photographs.** GBER lists owner-photo promotions as off-limits. Amish America says some owners use pictures of unbaptized children in marketing, which are "often seen as more acceptable" (blog; no study measures compliance **[gap]**).

**Interpretation (descriptive)**
- The best-documented effects are *structural*: land, traffic, proximity. Those push Amish households out of tourist areas, or push youth toward exit, more than they change Ordnung rules.
- The 1992 Shipshewana contrast is the only quantitative link between a tourist center and defection that CAV found, and it cannot separate tourism from town proximity or from district strictness.

#### A3.3 Labeling, trademark, and consumer protection
**Evidence**
- **No Amish trademark.** "There is no 'Amish' trademark" and "no regulatory body" for the label (Amish America, "Marketing the Amish"). CAV did not search the USPTO database directly, so the absence of any "Amish"-containing registered mark is unchecked **[gap]**. Amish America also says non-Amish dealers have sold software, refrigerators and organic cotton bedsheets "under the Amish moniker or by association with Amish images."
- **Amish attitudes.**
  - A common saying: "if it says Amish, then it ain't." An Amish acquaintance in Ohio knew of just one Amish-owned firm with "Amish" in the name, and it had since been sold to a non-Amish person (Amish America; blog journalism, anecdotal).
  - A woman running a food business, reported by Amish America: "Amish-made whatever!... We feel it's not right to use 'Amish' just so we can sell something. Not everybody feels that way, and that's okay."
  - Amish America: "The Amish find this slightly annoying. But they live with it. Suing is out of the question, and how could you do it, even if you wanted to? There is no 'Amish' trademark." Suing matches the church ban on litigation (GBER).
  - "Many Amish owners don't exactly mind if word gets out about their background. They know what sells." Some Amish catalogs and promotions imply or state the owner's Amishness (all Amish America).
- **Enforcement.** CAV found no Pennsylvania Attorney General or court action over false "Amish-made" claims. A search is not proof of absence **[uncertain]**.
- **Amish Country Popcorn (Indiana).** The company's own page says that in 1965, "in the Amish community of Berne, Indiana," Roy Lehman planted popcorn for his six-year-old son Brian. It now harvests "roughly 10 million pounds of popcorn per year and sells across the globe" (company About page). The page's tone is evangelical ("His life goal is to tell people everywhere how to get to heaven"). It never says the owners are Amish, and CAV could not verify whether they are **[uncertain]**. The brand name appears to refer to the place.

**Interpretation (descriptive)**
- "Amish" works as an *unprotected geographic and cultural signifier*. Non-Amish firms can use it freely. Amish owners mostly avoid it, so the brand's profits mostly do not go to the people it names.

**Interpretation (normative)**
- This is the main study's "rebranded consumption" case: the *prestige* of costly restraint sold without the *cost*. The absence of any legal protection that the Amish themselves could use (they decline to sue) makes the asymmetry sharper.
- It does not follow that Amish plainness is thereby contradicted. A counterfeit does not impeach the original.

#### A3.4 Amish-themed media
**Evidence**
- **Reality television.** *Amish Mafia* (Discovery, from 2012), *Breaking Amish* and spinoffs. Kraybill: "There's no Amish mafia. There never was. The whole thing is a fabrication in the minds of the producers" (NPR, 2014, WITF's Marie Cusick). NPR paraphrases him as saying many people on the shows "were raised in the community but never officially joined the religion." Dirk Eitzen's *Fooling with the Amish* (JHU Press 2022) treats the show as "entertaining fakery" (title and publisher listing; CAV did not read the book).
- **Pushback.** The 2014 "Respect Amish" campaign (Lancaster filmmaker Mary Haverstick) drew support from the Lancaster Chamber of Commerce, the tourism board, local churches and officials up to Pennsylvania's senators and governor. She reported an Amish elder saying the shows "make them look like garbage," and Dan Stoltzfus, an Amish baker, said: "It's all fake. It's not the real thing." NPR also reports that Lancaster police created a form explaining the shows are fake (WITF/NPR, 2014).
- **Romance fiction.** In 2012 a new Amish romance novel appeared about every four days; 60 more were published in 2012 than in 2009. The top three authors (Beverly Lewis, Wanda Brunstetter, Cindy Woodsmall) sold more than 24 million books combined; most of the books are written by evangelical Christians, not Amish authors, and published by evangelical houses (LA Review of Books, "Bonnet Rippers," 2013). Valerie Weaver-Zercher's *Thrill of the Chaste* (JHU Press) analyzes the genre; CAV did not open it.
- **Scholarship on the brand.** Trollinger, *Selling the Amish: The Tourism of Nostalgia* (2012); Walbert, *Garden Spot* (2002); Meyers (2003), "Amish Tourism: 'Visiting Shipshewana is Better than Going to the Mall'" (full record in §A11). None opened.

**Interpretation (descriptive)** The media market is mostly *about* the Amish, produced by outsiders. The evidence on Amish response is limited to a few quoted individuals, so it is thin.

**Interpretation (normative)** Misrepresentation (*Amish Mafia*) is a harm the community did not choose. It belongs in a CAV account of Semantic Ethics (the drift of "Amish" into a brand), not in an account of whether Amish practice has failed.

### A4. Testing the question against persistence data

#### A4.1 Retention
**Evidence**
- **Headline.** The Young Center states a retention rate of "85 percent or more" (2025 and 2026 profiles). Older figures near 90% appear in secondary sources CAV did not verify **[uncertain]**. The best peer-reviewed estimate is **84.46%** for those aged 40+ (below).
- **The best peer-reviewed estimate.** Anderson & Thiehoff (2025), *Population Studies* 80(2):257–280, analyzed 71 Amish directories (2009–2020, covering about 89.9% of North American Amish). Cumulative retention by age: 96.07% (18–23), 90.63% (24–29), 88.12% (30–39), **84.46% (40+)**. The naive cross-sectional figure (95.4%) understates attrition.
- **Time trend (Meyers 1994).** Elkhart-LaGrange cohorts remaining Amish: 75% (1920s), 68% (1930s), 75% (1940s), 83% (1950s), 92% (1960s, right-censored and likely overstated).
- **Affiliation differences.**
  - Anderson & Thiehoff summarize Holmes County studies (Friedrich 2001, Hurst & McConnell 2010, Choy 2020): New Order 53/67/64%; Old Order 85/>75/86%; Dan Church (Andy Weaver) 95/97/95%; Swartzentruber about 90%.
  - The New Order are commonly described as permitting more technology than the Old Order. CAV did not verify that against a source CAV opened **[uncertain]**.
- **Occupation: mixed evidence.** Meyers (1994) found no difference in defection by a father's non-farm occupation. Anderson & Thiehoff (2025) cite an older result (Ericksen et al. 1979): fathers in farming retained 97.2% of grown children, non-farmers 87.4% **[uncertain which holds today]**.
- **Ordnung and place.** Meyers links defection to the least restrictive Ordnung and to town proximity (Shipshewana and Clinton congregations).

**Interpretation (descriptive)**
- Retention is high and has not fallen. It rose across the cohorts Meyers observed, with the caveat above.
- Within the data, *stricter affiliations retain more*. If the New Order are in fact more permissive, as commonly described **[uncertain]**, "more permissive, lower retention" is consistent with the compromise hypothesis, but that reasoning cuts both ways. New Order groups may attract people with prior doubts about the Old Order, and some exits from the New Order may go to Beachy or Mennonite churches.

#### A4.2 Growth, doubling time, settlement spread
**Evidence** (Young Center profiles for 2025 and 2026; CAV computation in a re-runnable script)
- **Population.** 177,910 (2000) → 410,955 (2025): +233,045, +131%. June 2026: 421,200 (+10,245). CAV verified these figures against the profile pages before citing them.
- **Rates (CAV computation).**
  - 2000–2025: 3.41% a year, an implied doubling time of 20.7 years. This matches the Young Center's "about every 20 years."
  - The last two annual increments (+10,045, +10,245) are about 2.5% a year, implying about 28 years. Young Center estimates are rebuilt each year from directories and informants, so a single year's change is a weak signal **[uncertain]**.
- **Districts and settlements.** Districts: 1,335 (2000) → 3,114 (2025) → 3,285 (2026). The 2025 profile reports a net gain of 373 settlements since 2000. The 2025 profile says nine new settlements were founded that year and none dissolved. The 2026 profile says 43. The two profiles may count differently **[uncertain]**.
- **Spread.** 32 states and three provinces (2025), 33 states (2026); 54% of settlements have a single district (2026). The four largest settlements (Lancaster, Holmes, Elkhart-LaGrange, Geauga) hold 32.9% of the 2025 total (CAV computation from the Young Center county tables).
- **Stated reasons for new settlements** (Young Center 2025): cheap farmland, nonfarm work, rural isolation, kin proximity, and "a way to resolve church or leadership conflicts."
- **"Too commercialized."** Kraybill's remarks and the 2008–2012 Pennsylvania outflow (§A3.2) suggest tourism and suburbanization push families out. **CAV found no study quantifying how many settlements were founded explicitly to escape commercialization [gap].**
- **Drivers.** The Young Center credits "sizable nuclear families (five or more children on average)" and 85% retention. Anderson & Thiehoff report a TFR of 6.1 and 154 converts, 0.146% of baptized members alive in 2010.

**Interpretation (descriptive)**
- Growth is overwhelmingly demographic. It reflects *reproduction and retention*, not attraction. It says the community is reproducing itself. It does not show that its practices are unchanged.
- New settlements function as a *pressure valve*: dissenters and the land-squeezed move rather than split the home settlement or exit.

#### A4.3 Splits and counter-movements
**Evidence**
- **Strict reactions.** The Swartzentruber and Nebraska Amish are strict affiliations that arose from earlier splits over discipline and change. CAV did not verify their founding dates or causes against Kraybill, Johnson-Weiner & Nolt or Nolt **[uncertain]**. The Nebraska Amish are centered in central Pennsylvania (the Young Center's 2026 table lists Big Valley / Belleville in Mifflin and Huntingdon counties with 36 districts and 5,650 people).
- **Exits to car-driving Plain churches.** The Beachy Amish allow cars and public electricity. Their numbers in one search summary were 12,960 members in 201 congregations (2012) and 9,310 members in 154 churches (2017) **[uncertain: the two figures may count different areas, so they cannot show a trend]**. The Young Center excludes Beachy and Amish-Mennonites from its counts, so people who join them count as exits. **CAV found no published flow figure from Amish to Beachy or Mennonite churches [gap].**
- **Amish who reject cottage commercialization.** CAV found no organized movement. The visible rejections are affiliation-level (Swartzentruber, small settlements) and, in GBER's account, owner-managers who stay small on purpose.

**Interpretation (descriptive)**
- Splits show that the Ordnung is *contested and renegotiated*, which is evidence against a simple "compromised and unchanged" or "intact" reading.
- The strict affiliations and new small settlements show that exits from commercial pressure are available *inside* the Amish world. That is part of why measured retention stays high.

#### A4.4 Selection effects and survivorship
**Evidence and caveats**
1. **High exit costs could produce high retention.**
   - Only baptized members can be shunned, and expulsion "can lead to a lifetime of estrangement from family and friends" (Young Center). Formal schooling ends at eighth grade (GBER); CAV's inference is that this narrows outside options.
   - Yet the cost falls on those who are baptized. Those who leave before baptism "are not subject to church discipline," and the Young Center stresses an "open back door." Retention at 18–23 is 96.07%, which may reflect exit costs, satisfaction, or both (Anderson & Thiehoff).
   - Town proximity lowering retention (Meyers) suggests *outside options matter*, which supports the cost reading.
2. **Definitional survivorship.** "Amish" in these datasets excludes car-driving Plain groups. A family that adopts a car and electricity becomes Beachy or Mennonite and leaves the series. The count therefore tends to contain those who remain *within* the plain boundary, whatever the internal drift.
3. **Measurement.** Anderson & Thiehoff note that "families with children do leave as a whole family," which offspring-based retention cannot capture; directory coverage is about 89.9%. Retention counts membership, not plainness. Fertility (TFR 6.1) also masks conviction: growth continues under moderate attrition.

**Interpretation (descriptive)** The persistence data answer "does the Amish population survive?" (yes, strongly). They cannot answer "is plainness uncompromised?" Neither a high-retention nor a low-retention result would, by itself, distinguish satisfaction from lock-in or from selective departure of dissenters.

### A5. Mini-comparison: the Shakers (persistence logic only)

**Evidence**
- **Shakers (Bainbridge 1984, *Communal Societies* 4(1)).** 3,608 in 1840 and 3,842 in 1850 (the largest year with definite statistics), then decline to 92 members in 1936. The popular figure of 6,000 members is an error. Ten-year retention 1850–60 was 54.5% for females and 40.5% for males. A celibate sect can only replace members by recruitment. NPR (2025) reports three members at Sabbathday Lake.
- **Amish.** Growth by births; 154 converts in about a century; TFR 6.1; retention 84–90% (§A4).

**Interpretation (descriptive)**
- The *mechanism of persistence* differs. A celibate community needs a continuous flow of converts, and low retention then compounds to extinction. A family-reproducing community can absorb moderate attrition and still double.
- This contrast explains differing numbers. It says nothing here about whether commercial activity helped or hurt either group, and CAV draws no verdict on that.

### A7. What would confirm or refute "commercialization compromises plainness"

**Confirm if**
- Retention falls in the most business- and tourism-exposed districts after controlling for Ordnung strictness and affiliation.
- Technology exceptions granted for work spread to homes and are *not* reversed (unlike the 1910 telephone withdrawal).
- Income or wealth gaps widen, and visible spending (housing, rental property, hired transport, vacations) separates owners from laborers.
- Ordained leadership is increasingly drawn from business owners, and discipline of owners weakens (no excommunications for oversized firms).
- Members and church leaders report the tension in their own words (as in "The Big I"), and new settlements cite commercialization as a reason.

**Refute if**
- Retention is equal or higher in high-business settlements than in farm-based ones, once strictness is controlled.
- Size caps and technology rules are still enforced after a generation of business growth, with documented reversals.
- Wealth differences, however large, remain invisible in display and are redistributed (aid, benefit auctions, giving back).
- Amish-owned firms refuse tourist or brand markets at a cost.
- Non-Amish brand revenue has no measurable effect on Ordnung or exit rates.

### A9. Link back to the main study's thesis ("partial migration with dilution")

- The main study's thesis: anti-materialism persists in diluted form. The *value* spreads, while the *costly practice* survives mainly inside thick communities that pass it on through family and institutions.
- The Amish case supports the second half strongly: costly practice persists and grows because family and church institutions carry it. The qualification is that the cost is *regulated commerce, not renunciation of gain*. The Ordnung restricts display, technology and mobility and leaves room for income, so the cost is increasingly paid in internal economic differentiation.
- On the main study's conditions: R1 (thick forms decline without costly successors) is **not met** for the Amish. R2 (new forms are mainly products) is **met** for the external brand (B). R3 and R4 do not apply cleanly, because plainness is a communal discipline, not a stated goal.
- Overall: Amish plainness is a *persisting but economically reshaped thick form*. The external brand is a *dilution of its prestige*, which is the pattern the main study predicts.

<h2 id="appendix-wordsworth">Appendix: A Literary Touchstone, Wordsworth, "The World Is Too Much With Us"</h2>

<div class="poem">
<p>The world is too much with us; late and soon,<br>
Getting and spending, we lay waste our powers:<br>
Little we see in nature that is ours;<br>
We have given our hearts away, a sordid boon!<br>
This Sea that bares her bosom to the moon;<br>
The Winds that will be howling at all hours<br>
And are up-gathered now like sleeping flowers;<br>
For this, for every thing, we are out of tune;<br>
It moves us not— Great God! I'd rather be<br>
A Pagan suckled in a creed outworn;<br>
So might I, standing on this pleasant lea,<br>
Have glimpses that would make me less forlorn<br>
Have sight of Proteus coming from the sea;<br>
Or hear old Triton blow his wreathed horn.</p>
</div>

<p class="poem-source"><strong>Source:</strong> William Wordsworth, <em>Poems, in Two Volumes</em> (London: Longman, Hurst, Rees, and Orme, 1807), Vol. I, no. 18, p. 122 (untitled in the original, first line "The world is too much with us; late and soon"); public domain. The text follows the 1807 printing as transcribed on Wikisource and checked against the Internet Archive scan of Vol. I (<em>poemsintwovolume01word</em>), including the page image of p. 122. Modern anthology texts (Poetry Foundation, Academy of American Poets) were consulted only to note variants.</p>

**Note on the text.** The 1807 printing differs from the version most readers know. It reads "powers:" where later editions have "powers;", "coming from the sea" where later editions have "rising from the sea", and it has no punctuation after "forlorn" in line 12. It also has no comma after "hours", prints "nature" in lowercase, "every thing" as two words, and "Winds" with a capital. The dash after "It moves us not" is in the 1807 printing. The poem was untitled in 1807, and the title used today was added later.

**Why it is here.** The poem shows that the worry about getting and spending is old. It is illustrative, not evidence that anti-materialism is declining now. It is a touchstone for the question, and none of the study's findings rest on it.
