## About this study

The Center for the Study of Anomalous Virtues (CAV), a hybrid human-AI research organization, prepared this study with sources current as of 6 November 2026. First prepared 29 October 2026; revised 31 October 2026; revised 5 November 2026; revised 6 November 2026. This page is an unpublished draft. The 6 November 2026 date is the draft revision. The publication date is set only when the study is published. Figures were checked against the original reports, papers or their abstracts where these could be reached. Claims that could not be fully verified are tagged **[uncertain]** or **[unverified]**, and known holes in the evidence are tagged **[gap]**.

**Question framing.** This is an open question. The study assumes neither that politicians are unusually immoral nor that they are not.

**Reading of "anomalous."** "Anomalous" means departing from a norm. The main test is therefore relative (H5): do politicians show the *comparable* components of personal morality less often than matched comparison groups do? A secondary absolute reading reports the figure or proxy ceiling itself, with 50% as the only marked line (minority versus majority). Absolute cutoffs of 10%, 20% and 33% are not used.

**Scope.** The study covers national legislators in the United States (House of Representatives; Senate members appear only in the conviction count) and the United Kingdom (national MPs). Executives, state legislators and other countries are outside its scope.

## Executive summary

- **Provisional verdict:** the main question is relative (H5): on like-for-like financial-probity (M2) and private-conduct (M4) records, do national legislators show clean records less often than matched comparison groups? That question is answerable in principle, but not with the data compiled so far **[gap]**. Absolute M2 proxies describe detected financial records, not full M; the relative question remains open.
- **Behavior data, person level:**
  - US House, 1972 to 2012: 93 of 1,818 members (5.1%) were investigated for corruption by the Ethics Committee, and 75 (4.1%) suffered consequences. About 95.9% had no corruption consequence on that proxy (our own calculation: 1,743 of 1,818). That figure is an upper bound on the share with no detected Ethics Committee corruption consequence, not a measure of how many members meet M2, and not a measure of M.
  - UK 2009 expenses audit: 52% of 752 MPs and former MPs were recommended to repay some second-home allowance, and 48% had "no issues" (Legg 2010, paragraph 99); about 68% owed under £1,000 if the smallest band is grouped with "no issues." Those figures are alternative proxy classifications by how minor repayments are treated, not a statistical interval for true M2 or for M.
  - Repayment is not the same as dishonesty, and a clean detected record is not the same as morality.
- **Promise-keeping:** this is party-level, not person-level. Thomson et al. (2017) studied over 20,000 pledges from 57 election campaigns in 12 countries (published abstract). In the author manuscript, governing parties fulfilled 59% of 8,547 pledges at least partially (author-manuscript figure); the manuscript does not report the fully fulfilled share separately **[gap]**. H4 ("politicians break most of their promises") is therefore argued against (counting partial fulfilment), not refuted.
- **Perception data is separate:** in December 2025, 7% of US adults rated members of Congress "very high" or "high" on honesty and ethics (Gallup). This measures reputation, not behavior, and is not used to judge prevalence.
- **Relative method (one sentence):** H5 compares politicians with matched groups on M2 and M4 only, where genuinely like-for-like discipline, enforcement or conviction records can be defined, using a pre-specified significance rule (section 1); it does not claim that an M2/M4 gap identifies the full four-component conjunction M.

## 1. Working definitions (testable)

**M (personal morality).** A politician shows M over a period if their actual conduct meets all four components. Each component is defined by conduct; the measures listed are proxies for it, not the component itself.
- **M1 Honesty:** does not knowingly make false factual statements of consequence. *Proxy:* documented knowingly false statements.
- **M2 Financial probity:** does not misuse money or office for financial gain. *Proxy:* ethics-body findings, required repayments, or convictions involving money.
- **M3 Promise-keeping:** acts consistently with personal campaign pledges (votes, sponsorship, public advocacy). *Proxy:* coded pledge outcomes. Most individual legislators do not control whether a pledge is enacted, so the proxy must code their own actions, not enactment.
- **M4 Private conduct:** does not commit abuse, harassment or other serious personal misconduct. *Proxy:* adjudicated findings.

**Excluded:** policy positions, voting records (except as evidence for M3), party, ideology, and effectiveness. A politician can hold any policy and score high or low on M.

**Measurement rule.** Only behavior data (adjudicated findings, convictions, audits, coded pledge outcomes, documented statements) can test M. Perception polls test reputation. They never stand in for behavior.

**Inversion rule.** For each component, the share *without detected misconduct* is an upper bound on the share meeting that component, and therefore on M, *only when the proxy is a true misconduct screen*. The true share meeting the component is at most that figure, because some misconduct goes undetected and because clearing a misconduct bar is not the same as positive honesty. Where a proxy also counts non-misconduct (for example, rule-based repayments), the "clean" share is not a true upper bound on the share with no misconduct; such cases are flagged and must not be treated as ceilings on M2 or M.

**Conjunction rule.** Because M requires all four components, the M share is at most the lowest component share. If the components are independent, it equals their product; if they are positively correlated (the same people tend to pass all four), it is higher than the product but still no higher than the lowest share. How strongly they correlate is unmeasured **[gap]**. Example under independence only: if each component held for 80% of people independently, about 41% would meet all four (0.8^4 = 0.4096). That example does **not** establish that M is under 50% for almost any group. Applying the same four-component *definition* to a comparison group does **not** cancel differences in how strongly the components depend on each other, or differences in measurement exposure (how often conduct is observed and recorded).

**Test design.**
- **Main test (relative; H5).** "Anomalous" means departing from a norm. Compare politicians with matched groups on components that can be measured with genuinely like-for-like records.
  - **Confirmatory relative comparisons:** M2 (financial records) and M4 (private conduct), and only where the same kind of discipline, enforcement or conviction record can be defined for both populations over a matched observation window. Record visibility (press coverage, opposition research) is not the same thing as professional discipline or criminal enforcement; the two must not be treated as equivalent without an explicit mapping.
  - **M1 (honesty):** add to the relative test only when a comparable denominator and exposure can be defined (similar volume of public factual statements, or an exposure-adjusted rate). Politicians make far more public statements than most comparison groups, so raw falsehood counts are not like-for-like.
  - **M3 (promise-keeping):** politician-only on campaign pledges, pending a defensible equivalent such as keeping stated professional commitments. Non-politicians do not make campaign pledges, so M3 cannot be transferred as written.
  - Comparing M2 and/or M4 does **not** identify the full conjunction M. An M2 or M4 gap can support a relative claim about that component only.
  - Target groups: (a) professionals with similar education, income and age (lawyers, executives, senior civil servants); (b) other people under heavy public scrutiny (judges, CEOs of public companies).
- **Pre-specified H5 decision rule (chosen before further data collection).** For each confirmatory component comparison (M2, M4) with matched like-for-like populations and observation windows: test whether the politicians' clean-record share is lower than the comparison group's, two-sided alpha = 0.05, with the directional claim requiring the politicians' share to be the lower one. Adjust for two confirmatory tests with the Holm procedure. Report a confidence interval and an effect size (for example a risk difference or risk ratio) for each comparison. A census or a selected administrative list can still support a descriptive contrast of shares and differences. An inferential claim under this rule requires a justified target population and sampling model, or an explicit equivalent model. If that model is not justified, or if denominators and windows cannot be matched, label the inferential comparison **untested**, not mechanically "significant." Do not treat every census contrast as untestable in the descriptive sense. Statistical significance is a conventional threshold; it does not establish practical or moral importance. Do not use an undefined materiality threshold in place of this rule.
- **Secondary test (absolute).** Report the actual figure or proxy classification for each measured component. The only marked line is 50%: a share under 50% is a minority on that proxy; a share at or above 50% is a majority. Absolute results are descriptive. They do not settle H5.
- **Advantages of the relative design (when like-for-like records exist).** (1) No arbitrary prevalence cutoff: the comparison group sets the reference. (2) Relative to an absolute cutoff, it avoids treating definitional strictness as a finding about politicians alone; the same *named* components must still be measured with comparable exposure. (3) It matches the title's contrast with other people.

**Reference populations.** Each figure names its denominator, for example members who served in the US House from 1972 to 2012, or MPs and former MPs eligible for the UK allowance from 2004 to 2009.

## 2. Evidence: behavior

### 2.1 United States Congress

- **Ethics investigations (House, 1972 to 2012).** An article in *Public Integrity* reports that 93 of the 1,818 people who served in the House over that period were investigated for corruption by the Ethics Committee: 18 were acquitted and 75 "suffered consequences (reprimand/payback/resignation/conviction)." It also reports that longer tenure and more power were associated with a higher likelihood of corruption. Praino, R., and Graycar, A. (2018), "Does Corruption Follow Opportunity? A Study of the U.S. Congress," *Public Integrity* 20(5): <span class="nobr">478-496</span>, doi:10.1080/10999922.2017.1402169. Figures from the abstract; full text not read.
  - Shares: 5.1% investigated; 4.1% with consequences; about 95.9% without a corruption consequence (our own calculation: 1,743 of 1,818). Absolute reading: on this proxy, the share without an Ethics Committee corruption consequence is about 96%, which is a majority *on the proxy*. That 96% is an upper bound on the share with no such consequence under this screen; it is not a count of members who meet M2, and it is not a majority actually possessing M2.
  - **Limits of the measure (error in both directions; net direction unknown).** "Consequences" includes payback, which is not necessarily corruption, so 4.1% may overstate detected corruption and 95.9% understate the clean share. In the other direction, the Ethics Committee loses jurisdiction when a member leaves the House, and some members were prosecuted without a Committee investigation, so 95.9% overstates the clean share even as a proxy. The group also mixes members who served briefly with long-serving ones; given the tenure finding, short-serving members raise the clean share.
- **Criminal convictions.** Bump (2013), *The Atlantic*, using the Wikipedia "List of American federal politicians convicted of crimes," counted 68 members convicted of crimes while in office (64 House, 4 Senate). That 68 is the Atlantic's 2013 tally, not a verified census and not the current Wikipedia list's size. On the Wikipedia list as retrieved 6 November 2026 (last page revision 1 November 2026, 18:25 UTC), the <span class="nobr">1776-1897</span> section lists three members with convictions or guilty findings dated before 1900 (Matthew Lyon, 1798; Charles F. Mitchell, 1841; Robert Smalls, charged 1877 and found guilty). The current list's Legislative-branch bullet count is about 95. That figure is a count of bullets, not a verified count of unique people, so a "65 of 68" reading cannot be taken from the current list against the Atlantic's 2013 denominator. The Atlantic's decade breakdown could not be read **[unverified for any "almost all since 1900" share tied to the 68]**. A 1994 *Los Angeles Times* compilation counted 89 members indicted since 1798. No verified denominator of all members who have ever served was assembled, so no career conviction share is computed **[gap]**. Recent indictments span both parties (Roll Call, 2023). The Wikipedia list is a secondary compilation with its own inclusion rules; it is not a census of House or Senate members.
- **Office of Congressional Ethics referrals:** OCE (renamed the Office of Congressional Conduct by H.Res. 5, 119th Congress, section 4(d)(2)(A), agreed 3 January 2025, <span class="nobr">215-209</span>) publishes referrals. Counts per Congress were not compiled **[gap]**.

### 2.2 United Kingdom: the 2009 to 2010 expenses audit

Sir Thomas Legg reviewed Additional Costs Allowance claims from April 2004 to March 2009 for 752 MPs and former MPs (Legg 2010, House of Commons Members Estimate Committee, HC 348, report dated 4 February 2010; body paragraphs cited below). The direct Legg PDF was not read in this environment. Paragraph text below was checked against parliamentary publication excerpts.
- 48% had "no issues" and 52% were recommended to make repayments (paragraph 99; also executive-summary point 26). Absolute reading: the "no issues" share sits just under the 50% line *as a proxy classification*, not as a confirmed minority on M2 or on M.
- Before appeals, about £1.3m was recommended for repayment out of £55.5m paid (paragraphs <span class="nobr">96-97</span>; executive-summary point 25). The band counts sum to 390 MPs recommended to repay: three over £40,000, 56 between £5,000 and 40,000, 182 between £1,000 and 5,000, and 149 between £100 and 1,000 (paragraph 100; executive-summary point 27). These 390 match the 52% (about 391 of 752).
- Combining bands: 48% with no issues plus 149 (about 20%) owing under £1,000 means **about 68% owed less than £1,000**. Treat 48% and about 68% as alternative proxy classifications by how minor repayments are counted, not as statistical bounds on true M2 or on M.
- After appeals: paragraph 102 states that Sir Paul Kennedy remitted the recommended repayment in part or in full in 44 cases, reducing the total repayable by just under £185,000 (14%) to £1.12m, and bringing the highest single recommended repayment down to £42,458. Exact wording from paragraph 102: "The overall effect of Sir Paul's decisions will reduce the total amount repayable under my recommendations by just under £185,000 (14%) to £1.12m., and will bring the highest single recommended repayment down to £42,458." Kennedy's own conclusion likewise reports 44 appellants successful in whole or in part and a reduction of about £180,000. Paragraph 102 does **not** state how many MPs still owed after appeals. The BBC figures of about 373 (49.6%) or 372 therefore remain **[unverified]** against the primary report.
- Much of the repayment came from retrospective limits, such as caps on cleaning and gardening costs. A repayment recommendation is therefore not a finding of dishonesty. A smaller number of separate criminal prosecutions followed **[uncertain: count not verified]**.
- **Direction of the figure.** Because rule-based repayments are counted as "issues," 48% understates the share with no actual probity problem relative to a misconduct-only screen. Under the inversion rule, only a true misconduct screen yields an upper bound on the share meeting M2. The UK "no issues" share is therefore **not** a clean ceiling on M2 or on M.

The UK audit covers five years of claims; the US figure covers a 40-year membership group. The two shares are not directly comparable.

### 2.3 Honesty: fact-checks

Fact-checkers rate selected statements. Selection depends on newsworthiness, prominence and reader requests, so ratings describe the statements chosen, not a politician's overall truthfulness or a population share. No verified dataset gives a person-level share of politicians with a documented knowingly false statement **[gap]**. Relative comparison on M1 is not attempted here, because exposure (volume of public factual statements) is not matched to any comparison group.

### 2.4 Promise-keeping

Thomson et al. (2017), *American Journal of Political Science* 61(3): <span class="nobr">527-542</span>, doi:10.1111/ajps.12313, studied over 20,000 pledges made in 57 election campaigns in 12 countries (published abstract). The author manuscript reports 18,743 pledges from 54 campaigns; the figures below are author-manuscript figures:
- Governing parties fulfilled 59% of their pledges at least partially (5,044 of 8,547; author-manuscript figure).
- UK single-party majority governments fulfilled 86% (494 of 575; author-manuscript figure).
- US presidential parties fulfilled more than most coalition executives but less than single-party parliamentary governments.
- The unit is the party pledge, not the individual politician, and non-fulfilment often reflects power-sharing rather than a choice not to act on the pledge. This is party-level evidence and cannot give a person-level M3 share **[gap]**. M3 remains politician-only pending a defensible equivalent for non-politicians.

### 2.5 Private conduct

No systematic, person-level dataset of adjudicated private misconduct among legislators was verified **[gap]**. Relative comparison on M4 therefore remains untested.

### 2.6 Relative test: comparison groups and data needed (H5)

The main test asks whether politicians show *lower* clean-record shares than matched comparison groups on confirmatory components M2 and M4, using genuinely like-for-like discipline, enforcement or conviction records, matched populations and observation windows, and the pre-specified significance rule in section 1. M1 is eligible only with comparable exposure; M3 is politician-only for now. An M2 or M4 comparison does not identify full M.

H5 is therefore untested; the data have not yet been compiled.

## 3. Evidence: perception (reported separately)

Gallup's December 1 to 15, 2025 poll (n = 1,016 US adults) found:
- 7% rated members of Congress "very high" or "high" on honesty and ethical standards, and 71% rated them "low" or "very low". Only members of Congress and telemarketers drew a majority-negative rating.
- The December 2024 poll rated state officeholders at 14% and local officeholders at 20% high or very high.
- The members-of-Congress rating was 25% in November 2001, its highest since 1976 in Gallup's trend table.

These figures describe public opinion about a group. They are not evidence of the share of politicians who behave morally.

## 4. Selection and detection

- **Selection (who runs).** Candidates are self-selected and vetted by parties, donors and the press. Selection could raise or lower component shares relative to comparison groups. No verified study measuring the direction was compiled **[gap]**.
- **Detection.** Legislators face audits, disclosure rules, opposition research and press scrutiny far beyond most citizens. Higher scrutiny inflates *detected* misconduct relative to equally behaved groups, while closed processes (self-regulating ethics committees) may deflate it. Both biases operate, and their net size is unknown **[gap]**. Matched comparisons should use groups that face comparable formal enforcement, or adjust for detection. Record visibility is not the same as professional discipline.
- **Inversion caveat, restated.** Low detected misconduct on a true misconduct screen means a clean record is common on that proxy. It does not show that morality is common, only that it is not ruled out on that screen.
- **One-way test on absolute proxies.** Where a proxy is a true misconduct screen, the clean share is a ceiling on the share meeting that component. A ceiling under 50% would show a minority clean record on that proxy; a ceiling above 50% is consistent with the component share being either a minority or a majority. The UK 48% "no issues" share is not such a ceiling (section 2.2). No proxy here answers H5. Only matched like-for-like component data under the section 1 rule could.
- **Conjunction.** Even if every component were measured and each were common, M could still be a minority, because a person must pass all four. Dependence among components and measurement exposure can differ across groups, so applying the same definition does not by itself make absolute conjunction rates comparable.
- **What the absolute proxies bound.** For House members, about 96% is the most that could meet M2 *on the Ethics Committee corruption-consequence screen* (and therefore an upper bound on M only through that component and that screen). For UK MPs, the comparable figure on the Legg audit lies somewhere between 48% and about 68% as alternative proxy classifications by how minor repayments are treated; neither end is a true ceiling on M2 or on M. The data say nothing about how far below those figures M falls.

## 5. Interpretation (descriptive)

1. On the one US person-level behavior dataset compiled here, about 96% of House members from 1972 to 2012 had no corruption consequence from the Ethics Committee (a majority *on that proxy*). That is an upper bound under the screen, not a count of members who meet M2. The measure has error in both directions (section 2.1).
2. In the UK audit, 48% had no repayment issue at all, and about 68% owed less than £1,000. These are alternative proxy classifications, not bounds on true M2 or on M. The 48% does not confirm that M2 (or M) is a minority among UK MPs.
3. Absolute M2 proxies therefore describe detected financial records: about 96% clean on the US Ethics screen; 48% to about 68% on UK Legg classifications. They do not measure M.
4. Positive M (all four components) is unmeasured at the person level for politicians. Relative comparisons on M2 and M4 are untested. The relative question (H5) is therefore open.
5. The public's rating of Congress is low (7% high or very high) while the clean-record proxies are high on some screens (about 96% US; 48% to about 68% UK). These are different quantities, one an opinion share among the public and the other a record share among politicians, so they are reported side by side rather than compared. The data compiled cannot say how they relate.

## 6. Interpretation (normative)

These are judgments, not findings.
- A low public rating is not a moral verdict on individual politicians. Treating it as one would misjudge the many with clean records and ignore detection effects.
- A clean record is a minimal standard. If the Center for the Study of Anomalous Virtues (CAV) wants to recognise morality as a virtue in politics, the relevant trait is positive (honesty under pressure, keeping costly promises), not mere absence of findings. That trait is unmeasured.
- Examples should be drawn evenly across parties, or not named at all.
