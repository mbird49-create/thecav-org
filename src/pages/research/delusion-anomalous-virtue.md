> **A note on mental health.** This study discusses delusion and psychosis as research topics. People who live with psychosis are not morally lesser, and nothing here treats psychosis as a gift, an insight, or something to seek out. Delusions are usually involuntary and often distressing, and the people who have them deserve care and respect. If unusual beliefs or experiences are causing you or someone close to you distress, please talk to a doctor or <span class="nobr">mental-health</span> professional.

## Abstract

Can being delusional be an anomalous virtue? This study treats that as an open question. It does not assume that any form of "delusion" is a virtue, or that none is. Because "delusional" covers several distinct things, the study splits it into five testable meanings: clinical delusion, positive illusions, self-deception, faith or conviction, and the "delusional" confidence praised in founders and athletes. It tests each against prevalence data and against four interpretive criteria for virtue, and then asks whether the philosophical idea of *epistemic innocence* changes the picture. The evidence suggests that how rare "delusion" looks depends on the cut-off, that the chosen forms are common where measured, and that verdicts depend on the reference population. The one open candidate, chosen and accountable conviction against the odds, has never been measured.

**Working definition.** In this study a virtue counts as *anomalous* in a reference population when fewer than 20% of that population hold it, on the best available measure; a share of 20-25% is treated as borderline. A trait reads as *virtue-like* only if it is (a) at least partly chosen or cultivated, (b) aimed at a good beyond self-flattery, (c) open to correction when the stakes for others rise, and (d) costly in some way to the holder. These four criteria are interpretive, not findings.

**Keywords:** delusion; psychosis; positive illusions; optimism bias; depressive realism; self-deception; overconfidence; faith; voice-hearing; epistemic innocence; Semantic Ethics; Ethno-Ethics; Sacred Axiology

## Executive summary

### Findings

1. **"Delusional" is five different things.** Clinical delusion, positive illusions, self-deception, faith or conviction, and "delusional" founder or athlete belief have different definitions, different data and different evidence bases. Popular praise of "being delusional" appears to refer mainly to the last two **[gap]**: no usage or attitude data.
2. **Clinical delusion is a symptom, and its rarity depends on the cut-off.** It is usually involuntary and usually distressing. Survey data show self-reported delusional experiences in about 1.3% of adults over a lifetime across 18 countries (McGrath et al. 2015), but these are experiences reported to lay interviewers, not clinical diagnoses, and broader questionnaires find delusion-like ideas far more often (Peters, Joseph and Garety 1999).
3. **Mild positive bias about the self is typical on average** (Taylor and Brown 1988; Moore and Fresco 2012), and in some samples most people rate themselves above their peers (Svenson 1981). No representative, person-level share of self-enhancers was found, so no cutoff (10%, 20% or 33%) can be applied **[gap]**. A large meta-analysis links self-enhancement to better personal adjustment but mixed interpersonal effects (Dufner et al. 2019).
4. **"Sadder but wiser" is weak.** The pooled depressive realism effect is small (d = -0.07 across 75 studies), and a preregistered replication found no effect (Dev et al. 2022). The data do not support the claim that accuracy requires sadness or that optimism requires error.
5. **Founder optimism is common among new owners.** 81% of new US business owners in 1985 rated their odds of success at 7 in 10 or better (Cooper, Woo and Dunkelberg 1988), so within that group it is not anomalous. Measured against all adults, the same founders would be a minority, so the verdict depends on the reference population. Related research points the same way only indirectly: laboratory subjects over-enter skill-based markets (Camerer and Lovallo 1999), hubris theory predicts higher venture failure (Hayward et al. 2006), and overconfident CEOs overpay for acquisitions (Hayward and Hambrick 1997). None of these tracks founders' outcomes **[gap]**.
6. **Culture shapes how voices and visions are read and lived** (Luhrmann et al. 2015a; Luhrmann 2012). This is evidence about meaning and experience, not prevalence. The <span class="nobr">DSM-5</span> excludes culturally shared beliefs from "delusion."
7. **Epistemic innocence is an epistemic assessment, not a prevalence measure.** Bortolotti (2015, 2020) proposes that some delusional beliefs bring an epistemic benefit that no available alternative belief would give. Its No Alternatives condition implies that innocent beliefs are largely unchosen, and it adds no prevalence data.
8. **No form examined has measured prevalence under 20% combined with the virtue criteria.** The one candidate that might, chosen and accountable conviction against the odds, has never been measured.

### Our reading

- Clinical delusion is not a candidate virtue: rarity of a symptom is not rarity of a virtue.
- Reading some visionary conviction as faith rather than pathology is supported by the cultural evidence, but this does not make pathology a virtue.
- Epistemic innocence is an epistemic excuse, not a moral credit, so it does not change the verdict.
- The most defensible candidate for an anomalous virtue here is better named hope or courage than delusion.

## 1. Definitions, and what the data measure

Each definition names what would count as a measurement. The study treats them as separate constructs and does not let data about one stand in for another.

**D1. Clinical delusion.** The <span class="nobr">DSM-5</span> (American Psychiatric Association 2013, p. 87) defines delusions as "fixed beliefs that are not amenable to change in light of conflicting evidence." Its glossary adds: "The belief is not one ordinarily accepted by other members of the person's culture or subculture (e.g., it is not an article of religious faith)" (as quoted in Antrobus and Bortolotti 2016, p. 192). Both passages were confirmed through published quotations; the manual itself was not read. Whether the <span class="nobr">DSM-5-TR</span> (2022) keeps both passages unchanged, and their page numbers there, are **[uncertain]**. Measurement: structured clinical interview, or survey items on delusional experiences (as in the WHO World Mental Health Surveys). Key feature: not chosen.

**D2. Positive illusions and optimism bias.** Taylor and Brown (1988) describe three normal tendencies: unrealistically positive self-evaluation, an exaggerated sense of control, and unrealistic optimism. Measurement: self-ratings against peer or objective benchmarks, control judgments in tasks where the person has no control, comparative-optimism ratings. Key feature: mild, widespread, and usually not experienced as belief against evidence.

**D3. Self-deception.** Von Hippel and Trivers (2011) treat it as favoring welcome over unwelcome information in ways that serve goals, possibly to persuade others more convincingly. Measurement: biased information search, interpretation and memory, with some evidence that the truth is registered. Key feature: motivated, and partly hidden from the self.

**D4. Faith or conviction.** Commitment to a belief or path that outruns available proof, held within a tradition or community that recognizes it, and open to that community's tests. Measurement: survey items on belief, certainty and religious commitment; ethnography. Key feature: socially shared and usually chosen or cultivated. Under D1's cultural exclusion, it is not clinical delusion.

**D5. "Delusional" founder or athlete belief.** Confidence in one's own odds that clearly exceeds base rates, or exceeds one's own estimate for comparable others. Measurement: self-rated odds compared with ratings for similar businesses (Cooper, Woo and Dunkelberg 1988), or set beside base rates such as BLS survival data; overconfidence in survey tasks (Koellinger, Minniti and Schade 2007). Key feature: often deliberate, with costs that fall partly on the person and partly on investors, staff and family.

**Choosing the reference population.** A prevalence verdict is only as good as its denominator. Confident founders were 81% of new US business owners in Cooper, Woo and Dunkelberg's 1985 sample, but new business owners are themselves a minority of adults. In the 2023 GEM US adult survey, adults starting or running a new business (Total early-stage Entrepreneurial Activity) were a minority of 18-to-64-year-olds **[uncertain: exact US TEA figure for 2023 not read from the GEM report]**. If a similar share held today, founders with that level of self-rated confidence would be a small fraction of all adults, plausibly under 20% **[uncertain: combines a 1985 member sample with an unread 2023 GEM rate]**. The choice of denominator can flip the D5 verdict and the H1 test, so each claim below names its population.

## 2. Evidence

### 2.1 Clinical delusion and psychotic experiences

McGrath et al. (2015), using WHO World Mental Health Surveys from 18 countries (31,261 adults, 2001-2009), found lifetime prevalence of any psychotic experience of 5.8%, of hallucinatory experiences 5.2%, and of delusional experiences 1.3%. Twelve-month prevalence of any psychotic experience was 2.0%. Lifetime prevalence was higher in middle- and high-income countries (7.2% and 6.8%) than in low-income countries (3.2%), and higher among women (6.6%) than men (5.0%). Most people with any experience reported only one type, and about a third reported a single occurrence.

Peters, Joseph and Garety (1999) found that individual items of the Peters et al. Delusions Inventory were endorsed by about one in four adults on average, and that patient and non-patient score ranges overlapped; distress, preoccupation and conviction separated the groups. Estimates of delusion-like ideas in the general population vary widely by instrument and threshold (reviewed by Heilskov, Urfer-Parnas and Nordgaard 2020). No range is quoted here from that review, because its full text was not read.

*Data match:* McGrath gives real prevalence of a symptom-type experience. It does not measure whether anyone holds the belief as chosen or good, and the pooled figure should not be applied to a single country.

### 2.2 Positive illusions and the <span class="nobr">mental-health</span> debate

Taylor and Brown (1988) argued that the three illusions are widespread in normal adults and go with markers of mental health, such as contentment, caring for others and productive work. Colvin and Block (1994) replied that the evidence did not show illusions *foster* mental health, and that the definitions of both illusion and health were problematic. Taylor and Brown answered in the same journal in 1994.

Dufner, Gebauer, Sedikides and Denissen (2019), a meta-analysis of 299 samples (N = 126,916), found self-enhancement positively related to personal adjustment (life satisfaction, affect, depression), robust across sex, age, cohort and culture, with a positive longitudinal effect. Interpersonal effects were mixed: positive at first acquaintance but not at long acquaintance, and agentic self-enhancement was linked negatively to informant-rated communion. The authors call self-enhancement "beneficial for personal adjustment but a mixed blessing for interpersonal adjustment." This partly answers Colvin and Block on personal adjustment, while supporting their caution about social costs.

**How common is self-enhancement, person by person?** Three kinds of data exist, none of them a representative prevalence estimate:
- Svenson (1981): of 41 US students asked about driving skill, 93% placed themselves above the group median; of 40 asked about safety, 88% did. These are small student samples from Oregon; Swedish students showed lower shares.
- College Board Student Descriptive Questionnaire (1976-1977), as reported in later literature: 70% of college-bound students rated their leadership above average and 2% below **[uncertain: primary College Board report not read; figure from secondary sources]**.
- Kwan, John, Kenny, Bond and Robins (2004) treat self-enhancement as a continuous individual difference and do not report a share of "self-enhancers."

These show that majorities rate themselves above average *in specific domains and samples*. They do not give the share of adults who hold positive illusions in general **[gap]**.

Moore and Fresco (2012) report that across 75 studies nondepressed participants showed a positive bias (d = 0.29), and depressed or dysphoric participants also showed one (d = 0.14). These are standardized group mean differences, not prevalence: they show that the average participant leans positive, not what share of people do.

### 2.3 Depressive realism

- Alloy and Abramson (1979) found that depressed students judged their control in a contingency task more accurately than nondepressed students, who overestimated control when outcomes were favorable. This launched the "sadder but wiser" hypothesis.
- Moore and Fresco (2012) meta-analyzed 75 studies (7,305 participants). The overall effect was small (d = -0.07), and larger in studies without an objective standard of reality and in studies that measured depression by self-report rather than structured interview.
- Dev, Moore, Johnson and Garrett (2022), a preregistered study of 246 online workers and 134 undergraduates, found no evidence that depressive symptoms related to illusory control or overconfidence.

The data do not show that depression improves accuracy.

### 2.4 Self-deception

Von Hippel and Trivers (2011) argue that self-deception evolved partly to deceive others more effectively, by reducing cues of deceit and mental load. This is a theoretical target article with peer commentary, not a prevalence study. It gives no share of people who self-deceive, so no cutoff can be applied to it at any level.

### 2.5 Founders and athletes

Cooper, Woo and Dunkelberg (1988) surveyed 2,994 US entrepreneurs who had recently become owners (NFIB members, 1985). 81% rated their own odds of success at 7 in 10 or better, and 33% at 10 in 10, while rating similar businesses as less likely to succeed. Poorly prepared founders were as optimistic as well-prepared ones. The sample should not be generalized to all founders, to venture-backed startups, or to other countries.

For comparison, the US Bureau of Labor Statistics Business Employment Dynamics program tracks how long new private sector establishments survive (BLS, Table 7, data through March 2025):
- **First year:** 77.9% of establishments that opened in the year ended March 2024 were still open in March 2025. Across cohorts from 1994 to 2024, first-year survival ranges from 75.2% to 80.9%.
- **Five years:** 51.4% of the cohort that opened in the year ended March 2020 survived five years. Across cohorts from 1994 to 2020, the range is 45.4% to 51.9%.
- **Longer term:** of the 1994 cohort, 25.5% survived 15 years and 12.6% survived 31 years.

*Caveats:* these figures count establishments, including new branches of existing firms, not only founder-led startups. The ranges are CAV's own calculation from the table. Setting them beside the 81% of founders who rated their odds at 7 in 10 or better is suggestive but not a direct test: Cooper measured self-rated odds of "success" with no time frame, among 1985 NFIB members and their firms, while BLS counts survival of establishments, including branches, in cohorts from 1994 on. The populations, the outcome and the time period all differ.

Koellinger, Minniti and Schade (2007), using Global Entrepreneurship Monitor survey data across many countries, examine whether overconfident perceptions of one's own skills relate to starting a business. The specific findings are paraphrased from secondary summaries **[uncertain]**.

Athletes: no population prevalence study of "delusional" athletic belief was located. Popular claims that elite athletes are unusually self-deceiving or overconfident are **[uncertain]** and are not used.

#### Overconfidence and outcomes

- Camerer and Lovallo (1999): in laboratory market-entry games, subjects entered more when payoffs depended on their own skill rank than on chance, and industry profits turned negative in skill rounds, more so when subjects self-selected knowing success would depend on skill. The authors call this "reference group neglect." These are student laboratory data, not field founders.
- Hayward, Shepherd and Griffin (2006): a theoretical model proposing that founder overconfidence leads to under-resourced ventures and higher failure, while possibly helping founders bounce back and start again. Theory, not a test.
- Hayward and Hambrick (1997): CEO hubris indicators were associated with larger premiums paid in large acquisitions, and higher premiums with losses to acquiring shareholders **[uncertain: volume, issue and pages from standard citation; abstract not read]**.
- Malmendier and Tate (2005): CEOs classed as overconfident (by holding in-the-money options too long) showed investment more sensitive to cash flow, consistent with overestimating returns **[uncertain: volume, issue and pages from standard citation; abstract not read]**.

These are studies of established-firm CEOs, laboratory subjects and models, not panel data on whether overconfident founders themselves earn more or survive longer. The CEO studies should not be generalized to founders.

*Data match:* these are belief-about-odds data, not attitude data about whether such optimism is admirable. No survey was located that asks the public whether "delusional" confidence is a virtue, so no claim about public approval is made **[gap]**.

### 2.6 Faith, visions and culture

- Luhrmann, Padmavati, Tharoor and Osei (2015a) interviewed 20 people meeting schizophrenia inclusion criteria in each of San Mateo (USA), Chennai (India) and Accra (Ghana). US participants more often used diagnostic labels and reported violent commands; Indian and Ghanaian participants more often described relationships with their voices (family, spirits, God) and less often described them as an intrusion on the mind. The samples are small and clinical; this is evidence about how culture shapes experience within illness, not about prevalence.
- Luhrmann et al. (2015b) propose a "social kindling" hypothesis, that cultural attention shapes which experiences become voices. It draws on the same three-site sample, so it is not independent corroboration.
- Luhrmann (2012) is an ethnography of US evangelical prayer, describing how congregants learn to experience God's voice and how they and their communities test those experiences. It concerns one group.
- Pew Research Center, 2023-24 Religious Landscape Study (published 26 February 2025): 83% of US adults believe in God or a universal spirit, and 54% say they are absolutely certain of it (down from 71% in 2007). Fewer than half say religion is very important in their lives **[uncertain: exact overall percentage not read]**. This is belief and certainty data for US adults. It does not measure commitment within a tradition, which is how D4 is defined, so it is only a rough upper-bound proxy for D4. It also does not measure visions or attitudes toward visionaries.

## 3. Epistemic innocence

This is a philosophical framework, not a dataset. It contributes no prevalence figures.

**The idea.** Lisa Bortolotti (2015) argues that some delusional beliefs, though irrational, can be *epistemically innocent*. The paper appeared in a special issue of *Consciousness and Cognition* on the costs and benefits of imperfect cognition (Bortolotti and Sullivan-Bissett 2015). A belief is innocent when it meets two conditions, quoted from the paper (p. 494 **[uncertain: page within 490-499 not confirmed]**):

- "Epistemic Benefit: The delusional belief confers a significant epistemic benefit to an agent at the time of its adoption."
- "No Alternatives: Other beliefs that would confer the same benefit are not available to that agent at that time."

**What "benefit" means.** Bortolotti argues that psychological benefits, such as managing overwhelming emotion or protecting self-esteem after trauma or loss, can preserve a person's capacity to keep forming true beliefs, and so count as epistemic.

**Not justification.** Her book (Bortolotti 2020) extends the idea to distorted memories, confabulation, elaborated delusions and optimistically biased beliefs, which overlaps with D2. Its abstract states that epistemic innocence "is a weaker notion than epistemic justification, as it does not imply that the epistemic benefits of the irrational belief outweigh its epistemic costs." Antrobus and Bortolotti (2016) argue that depressive delusions can preserve "overall coherence and narrative identity at a critical time."

#### Critiques

- *The No Alternatives condition may be too easy to meet.* McKenna (2022) argues that if a psychological need to think well of oneself can make a more accurate belief "unavailable," the condition may be satisfied by the very bias under evaluation.
- *It may excuse harmful beliefs.* Białek (2024) argues that most elaborated delusions in schizophrenia bring "an intense type of epistemic harm" and that the current definition "does not exclude them," and proposes modified conditions.
- *The benefit may be merely psychological.* Fatić argues that coherence, narrative identity and motivational engagement are psychological benefits, and that calling them epistemic presupposes a theory of epistemic value the program does not defend ("The Limits of Epistemic Mercy" **[uncertain: venue and year not confirmed]**).

## 4. Interpretation (descriptive)

1. **Apparent rarity depends on the cut-off.** Self-reported delusional experiences in the WHO surveys were 1.3% lifetime, but on the Peters et al. Delusions Inventory individual items were endorsed by one in four adults on average, and patient and non-patient score ranges overlapped. Neither figure is a rate of clinical delusion. Whatever the cut-off, the experience is usually involuntary.
2. **The chosen forms are common where measured, but their share is mostly unmeasured.** Positive bias is typical on average, and majorities rate themselves above average in particular samples and domains; the population share of self-enhancers is a **[gap]**. Among new US owners in 1985, 81% rated their odds at 7 in 10 or better, and 33% at 10 in 10, within a narrow group.
3. **Religious belief and certainty are common among US adults.** 83% believe in God or a universal spirit and 54% are absolutely certain (Pew 2025). D4's narrower construct, commitment held within a tradition, was not measured directly; even so, a share above 20% seems likely. Under the <span class="nobr">DSM-5</span> definition, culturally shared belief is excluded from delusion.
4. **What might be rare is untested.** Conviction against the odds that is chosen, other-directed, open to correction and costly may be rare, but no dataset measures it.
5. **Epistemic innocence answers a different question.** It asks whether a belief, given the person's situation, earns a softer *epistemic* judgment. It does not ask whether the belief is rare, chosen or morally good. Under No Alternatives, the stronger the case for innocence, the weaker the case that the belief was chosen.
6. **Independent corroboration is limited.** The positive-illusion and depressive realism studies share methods (laboratory tasks, student samples), and the cross-cultural voice work rests largely on one research program.

## 5. Interpretation (normative)

These are judgments, not findings, and readers may weigh them differently.

- Because delusional experience is usually involuntary, it fails virtue criterion (a), and often (d), because its cost is suffering rather than sacrifice.
- Calling clinical delusion a virtue would romanticize suffering and risks discouraging people from seeking care **[uncertain]**. This study does not do that. Equally, people with psychosis are not morally lesser, and cultural framings that let people relate to their voices with less fear (§2.6) seem worth learning from on grounds of care.
- Nothing in the depressive realism evidence (§2.3) means that depressed people are "right" or that their suffering is insight, or that cheerfulness is a moral achievement.
- In popular praise, "delusional" appears to refer mainly to unreasonable hope plus effort **[gap]**: no usage or attitude data. The older vocabulary for that is hope, magnanimity or courage. A virtue reading is more defensible when the belief is about one's own effort rather than other people's money or safety, when it can be revised, and when the holder is honest about the odds with those who share the risk. Overconfidence that shifts costs onto others reads more like a vice.
- Self-deception of the kind von Hippel and Trivers describe, deceiving oneself to persuade others, sits poorly with truthfulness, even when it works.
- **Epistemic innocence supports compassion, not praise.** It gives a principled reason not to blame or belittle people for beliefs adopted under trauma or illness, and a reason for clinicians to understand what a belief is doing before trying to remove it. That is a strong point against stigma. It does not make the belief a virtue: innocence is the absence of a fault, while a virtue is a cultivated excellence.
- Could a belief be both innocent and a chosen virtue? Only in a narrow band, such as a grieving person or a founder who knowingly keeps a hopeful belief in order to keep functioning. But then the strict No Alternatives condition fails, and in its motivational form it describes inability rather than choice. Where the two labels meet, the better name is hope, not delusion.
- Faith and prophetic conviction are better judged by their own traditions' tests (discernment, fruits, community accountability) than by the label "delusion."

## 8. Open questions and limits

- **No measure [gap]:** nothing measures the population share of people who self-enhance, athletic overconfidence, public admiration for "delusional" confidence, the narrow chosen-conviction form, or how common epistemically innocent beliefs are. No founder-level outcome panels for overconfidence were located.
