## Abstract

When one cardinal virtue is markedly stronger or weaker in a society than in comparable societies, is that society more likely to decline or collapse? This study does not assume the answer. Person-level, behavior-based shares of the classical virtues, tied to collapse indicators, were not found, so the question is split into a conceptual part, an imbalance part, and a comparative-history part. Section 4 then runs a desk pilot on ten polities. Both coding passes were made by AI from sources opened for the pilot, so this tests the method; it is not a finding at publication standard.

The working answer is unchanged: the evidence does not show that one virtue out of balance threatens collapse, and it does not rule it out. The pilot adds one concrete result, that Sparta's imbalance, coded this way, is an atrophy of justice followed by decline, not collapse. Most cells could not be coded (21 of 40). Quadratic-weighted kappa is 0.98 on 19 cells, but both passes came from one AI system, so agreement is inflated. The binary justice items saturate and do not reproduce Blanton and Fargher's ranking. The coders could not be blind to well-known outcomes. The caveats in section 6 stand as written.

**Keywords:** cardinal virtues; prudence; justice; fortitude; temperance; hypertrophy; atrophy; societal collapse; societal decline; pilot coding; Chronological Ethics; Sacred Axiology

**Question:** When one cardinal virtue is markedly stronger or weaker in a society than in comparable societies, is that society more likely to decline or collapse? This study does not assume the answer.

## Bottom line

As first framed, the question cannot be answered with data. It needs person-level, behavior-based shares of the classical virtues in a named society, tied to collapse indicators. None were found.

Split into three parts, the question is more tractable:

- **A. Conceptual.** Under the classical doctrine that the virtues are connected, one of them cannot be fully present while the others are missing. A virtue that runs ahead of the rest becomes, on that view, a vice or an imperfect (natural) virtue. On that reading the question is partly settled by definition.
- **B. Imbalance.** Historical cases exist where one virtue was cultivated far past the others. They point both ways. Sparta's military system lasted for centuries and then declined after its defeat at Leuctra in 371 BCE; the Shakers declined gradually over decades; the Old Order Amish are growing. None has yet been coded against the collapse criteria.
- **C. Comparative history.** One opened study, Blanton et al. (2020), codes the quality of government in 30 premodern states and narrates rulers' conduct in four of them. Read through Plato's city-level account of justice, it is the closest real evidence on any one virtue. It is suggestive, not a test, and it bears on justice only.

**Pilot coding (section 4).** The next step was run as a desk pilot on ten polities: five cases (Sparta and the four Blanton cases) and five matches. Both coding passes were made by AI from sources opened for the pilot, so this tests the method; it is not a finding at publication standard. What it shows:

- **Sparta's anomaly is in justice, not fortitude.** Justice scores 0.5 of 4 against Athens's 3 and sits 2.8 below Sparta's other virtues: *atrophy* on both tests. Fortitude scores 4, but prudence and temperance score 3, so it misses the 2-point rule (1.8). Aristotle's "one part of virtue only" reading is not reproduced.
- **No polity met every collapse condition.** Eight declined, one faced a threat, one showed none. Eastern Han came closest, lacking only documented capital losses. With no collapse, the pilot cannot link anomaly to collapse.
- **Most cells could not be coded.** 21 of 40 polity-virtue cells are missing or unresolved, reflecting the sources opened, not the polities.
- **Agreement is high but inflated.** Quadratic-weighted kappa 0.98 (19 cells), but both passes came from one AI system.

The working answer is unchanged: the evidence does not show that one virtue out of balance threatens collapse, and it does not rule it out. The pilot adds one concrete result (Sparta's imbalance, coded this way, is an atrophy of justice followed by decline, not collapse) and a clear list of what a full study needs (section 5).

## The question, in three parts

"Any one" points to one virtue out of step with the other three, not to how common virtue is overall.

| Part | Question | Kind of evidence | Can it be answered now? |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Conceptual (normative) | Can one cardinal virtue be anomalous while the others stay normal? | Classical texts on how the virtues are connected | Largely yes, on the classical account |
| B. Imbalance (descriptive) | When a society cultivated one virtue far past the others, did collapse or a threat of it follow? | Named historical cases coded against the collapse criteria | Partly: Sparta pilot-coded (section 4); Shakers and Amish not coded |
| C. Comparative history (descriptive) | Does a measured change in one virtue, at the level of the polity, track collapse across matched cases? | Coded institutions and rulers' conduct across states | Pilot only (section 4): five matched pairs, much missing data, no collapse in the sample |

The first draft's person-level test (what share of individuals have the virtue, against a 50% line) is set aside. Section 2 explains why.

## 1. Definitions

The definitions of the virtues and of collapse are kept from the first draft. The definition of "anomalous" changes.

**The four virtues.** Each is defined by conduct, following Aristotle (*Nicomachean Ethics* II.6-7, 1106b36-1107b8) and Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* I-II q. 61 a. 3). In Aquinas, prudence "commands," justice "is about due actions between equals," temperance "suppresses desires for the pleasures of touch," and fortitude "strengthens against dangers of death."

| Virtue | Working definition | Named excess | Named deficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prudence | Choosing actions by deliberation about what is good in the circumstances | Cunning: cleverness aimed at a base end (Aristotle's "knavery," VI.12, 1144a) | Imprudence, rashness in judgment |
| Justice | Rendering what is due to others, in exchange and distribution | Rigorism, harshness | Injustice, taking more than one's share |
| Fortitude | Holding to a reasoned good in the face of fear | Rashness | Cowardice |
| Temperance | Restraining appetite for bodily pleasure to a reasoned mean | Insensibility, austerity past reason | Profligacy |

The columns read as too much or too little of the virtue itself. For temperance that reverses Aristotle's axis, which measures pleasure: his excess is "Profligacy," and the deficient character, who "scarcely occur[s]," he calls "Insensible" (1107b). The excesses for prudence and justice are not in his table at 1107a-b; they are working labels.

**Anomalous.** One virtue is anomalous when, in a named society and period, it is markedly stronger or weaker than in a matched society, or than the society's own other three virtues. Two directions:

- *Hypertrophy*: one virtue is cultivated far past the others (a warrior society built on fortitude).
- *Atrophy*: one virtue is markedly absent while the others hold (an orderly, sober society whose rulers are unjust).

**How "markedly" would be tested.** This is CAV's proposed rule, stated before any coding; the thresholds are not from a published scheme.

1. *Coding.* Each virtue is coded for the polity on a 0-4 scale from documented institutions, laws, and the conduct of rulers and office-holders over a stated period. Indicators are fixed in advance: for justice, equitable taxation, a fair judiciary, and sanctions on officials who profit privately (items Blanton and Fargher 2008 code); for fortitude, military training and conduct under risk; for temperance, enforced limits on consumption, dress, or sex; for prudence, deliberative institutions.
2. *Two coders.* Two coders score each case independently from the same sources and report agreement (weighted kappa). Cases below kappa 0.6 are reported as unresolved.
3. *Main test (relative, per the CAV site-wide standard).* A virtue is markedly anomalous if the polity's code on it differs by 2 or more points from a matched polity's code on the same virtue. The match is chosen before outcomes are examined: similar period, region, scale, and fiscal base.
4. *Secondary check (within society).* The same virtue also differs by 2 or more points from the polity's own mean on the other three.

Higher codes mean hypertrophy; lower codes mean atrophy.

**Collapse** has two parts, both from the first draft:

- Tainter (1988, p. 4 **[uncertain: publisher page not obtained; standard quotation]**): "A society has collapsed when it displays a rapid, significant loss of an established level of sociopolitical complexity." Slower losses are decline, not collapse.
- Cumming and Peterson (2017), four necessary conditions: (1) the identity of the system is lost; (2) the loss is fast relative to regeneration times, for people typically under 25 years; (3) there are substantial losses of social-ecological capital; (4) the consequences last longer than a single generation.

**Threatening collapse** means a documented trend toward those conditions, in a named system, that has not yet met them all. Cumming and Peterson say exact thresholds "will vary with identity criteria and context," so no single risk percentage is set.

## 2. Why the person-level 50% test is set aside

The CAV standard for individuals includes an absolute check: does a specified form of a virtue appear in fewer than 50% of people in a justified population? That test does not apply here. The question concerns polities and the balance between virtues inside one polity, not how many individuals hold a virtue. A society with a minority of brave individuals can still be organized around fortitude. At the polity level the 50% line has no meaning, so the coded comparison in section 1 replaces it. No person-level shares were found in any case.

## A. Conceptual: can one virtue stand alone? (normative)

This part reports what a tradition holds, not what was observed. On the classical account, the answer is no. A virtue that grows while the others are missing stops being that virtue. That settles the "extreme form" reading of the question by definition, not by evidence.

**The argument.**

1. Each moral virtue is a mean, and the mean is set by prudence. Aristotle: virtue is a mean "determined by principle, that is, as the prudent man would determine it" (*NE* II.6, 1107a1-2).
2. Prudence and the moral virtues require each other. Aristotle draws the conclusion in *NE* VI.13 (1144b30-1145a2): "it is not possible to be good in the true sense without Prudence, nor to be prudent without Moral Virtue," and "if a man have the one virtue of Prudence he will also have all the Moral Virtues together with it." Only the *natural* virtues can exist in isolation.
3. Aquinas makes the same move (*ST* I-II q. 65 a. 1). An imperfect moral virtue "is nothing but an inclination in us to do some kind of good deed," and such inclinations "are not connected." But "the perfect moral virtue is a habit that inclines us to do a good deed well," and since there is no prudence without the moral virtues, "it follows clearly that the moral virtues are connected with one another." He adds that "strength of mind is not commended as virtuous, if it be without moderation or rectitude or discretion."
4. So an anomalous virtue (one far stronger than the rest) is, on this account, an *imperfect* virtue or a natural disposition: a temperament for boldness or restraint, not fortitude or temperance proper.

**What follows for the question.**

- The question becomes: can a society tolerate one *imperfect* virtue, a strong disposition running ahead of prudence and the rest? That is empirical again, and Parts B and C address it.
- The tradition predicts that such a society would show the named vices: rashness, harshness, austerity past reason. Part B looks for that.
- Plato's city-level account (*Republic* IV, 427e-434c) applies the same idea to a polity. A rightly founded city "will be wise, brave, sober, and just" (427e), and justice is each class "doing its own work in the state" (434c). Interference among the classes "is the greatest injury to a state" (434c). That a city strong in courage but unjust would be disordered is CAV's inference from this passage, not Plato's wording.

**Limits.** This is an argument from a tradition, not a finding. Modern virtue ethicists dispute the strong unity thesis, and that debate was not opened for this draft.

## B. Imbalance: named cases (descriptive)

Each case is a candidate for one virtue, or one discipline, cultivated far past the others; that framing is CAV's, except where the ancient authors make it for Sparta. Cases that declined and cases that lasted are both included, to avoid survivorship bias. Only Sparta has been coded under section 1 (in the section 4 pilot); the Shakers and the Amish are communities, not polities, and remain uncoded.

**Sparta (fortitude).** Britannica describes a "military oligarchy that ruled the Spartan city-state from the 6th to the 2nd century bce," whose ruling class "devoted itself to war and diplomacy." Sparta became the leading Greek state after 404 BCE, lost to Thebes at Leuctra in 371, lost Messenia, and "a century-long decline followed." Two classical authors already read Sparta as one-virtue imbalance. Plato's *Laws* (I, 630d-e) says the lawgiver should legislate "with an eye not to some one fraction, and that the most paltry, of goodness, but to goodness as a whole." Aristotle (*Politics* II.9, 1271b) writes that "the entire system of the laws is directed towards one part of virtue only, military valor," so the Spartans "remained secure while at war, but began to decline when they had won an empire." He also gives a second cause: land tenure left fewer than a thousand citizen soldiers, so "the state did not succeed in enduring a single blow, but perished owing to the smallness of its population" (1270a). These are ancient judgments naming more than one cause, not coded evidence.

*Status: pilot-coded (section 4). On the CAV indicators the anomaly is atrophy of justice, not hypertrophy of fortitude; the post-371 outcome is classified as decline, not collapse, because the polity's identity was not lost.*

**The Shakers (temperance, as celibacy and communal property).** Bainbridge (1984), from US census enumerations: 3,842 Shakers in 1850, the peak year with definite statistics, then annual decline of 0.9% (1850-1860), 3.1% (1860-1880), and 3.8% (1880-1900). Celibacy may be an extreme form of temperance or a different religious rule; that is not settled. The unit is a voluntary community, not a society, and the decline was gradual. Bainbridge discusses defection and recruitment, not a single cause.

*Status: named; long community decline, not societal collapse; not treated as a cause.*

**The Old Order Amish (temperance, as plainness and limits on technology).** The Young Center for Anabaptist and Pietist Studies estimates 421,200 Amish in North America in June 2026, up from about 318,390 in 2017 (32%), and says the population "doubles about every 20 years." It credits large families and "an average retention rate ... of 85 percent or more." This too is a community within a state, and growth does not show the discipline is unchanged.

*Status: named; growing; not coded against the collapse criteria.*

The cases point both ways. Communities built on strong restraint have declined (the Shakers, dependent on converts) and grown (the Amish, growing through families). A polity built on fortitude lasted centuries before declining. None of this shows that the imbalance caused the outcome.

## C. Comparative history: Blanton et al. (2020) (descriptive)

**What the paper is.** Blanton, Feinman, Kowalewski and Fargher, "Moral collapse and state failure: a view from the past," *Frontiers in Political Science* 2 (2020), 568704, doi:10.3389/fpos.2020.568704. The journal classes it as a conceptual analysis. It draws on the authors' coding of 30 premodern states (Blanton and Fargher 2008).

**What it codes.** Good government, as numerical scales: Public Goods; Bureaucratization (hearing citizen voice and controlling officials); Modes of Control over Governing Principals; and a Good Government Summary. It also codes each regime's period of continuity and its decline pattern: "Major" (system-wide demographic, economic, and agricultural decline), patchy, or "Minor."

**What it finds.**

- Mean regime continuity: 152.4 years for the more authoritarian states (n = 14), 166.1 for good-government states, "not a statistically significant difference."
- Most higher-scoring states declined in the major pattern, and most authoritarian states in the minor pattern (p = 0.04, n = 19).
- In four high-scoring cases (Ming China, the Mughal empire, the Roman High Empire, and Venice), narrative summaries trace decline to leaders who abandoned founding principles and their role as "moral exemplars": the Wan-li emperor's illegal extraction, Aurangzeb's abandonment of religious ecumenism, Commodus's neglect of governing, and the Council of Ten's failure to act against a Doge's family.
- The authors call this "a possible initial causal factor" and write that they "cannot fully explain why this kind of failure would necessarily precipitate a major collapse."

**A correction to the review.** The paper codes institutions of good government across 30 states; rulers' conduct appears only in narratives of four cases and is not a coded variable.

**How CAV reads it.** Reading the good-government scales and the four rulers' lapses as a measure of Platonic justice in the city is CAV's interpretation, not what the study measured. On that reading the four cases look like *atrophy* of justice in the rulers, followed by major decline. The study does not use the CAV definition, compares good-government with autocratic states rather than matched societies on the same virtue, and offers its causal claim as a possibility. It is therefore **suggestive, not a test**, and it says nothing about prudence, fortitude, or temperance.

## 4. Pilot coding (AI coders, n = 10)

This section runs the previous draft's next step as a **pilot of the method**. Codebook, matches, evidence extracts, both coding files, results, dated outcome evidence and sources are in `cav-research/cardinal-pilot/`.

**Method.** (1) Codebook first: four indicators per virtue, each present (1), absent (0), or not found in the opened sources (NA); the score is the count on 0 to 4, rescaled if one indicator is NA, missing if two or more are. Justice items follow Blanton and Fargher's dimensions (public goods, bureaucratization with a fair judiciary, control over principals) plus rule-based taxation; the other indicators are CAV's proposals. (2) Polities, matches and windows fixed before coding. (3) Two passes from the same evidence extracts, the second written without opening the first. (4) Section 1 rule applied to consensus scores (agreement, or the mean when passes differ by one point; two or more is unresolved). (5) Outcomes classified only after both passes were saved.

**Polities and matches** (each coding window ends where the decline under study begins; reasons in `matches.md`): Sparta with Athens (both 480 to 371 BCE, rival Greek poleis); Ming (1368 to 1572) with Qing (1644 to 1795), same region and civil-service state; Mughal (1556 to 1658) with Ottoman (1453 to 1566), contemporaneous Islamic land empires; Roman High Empire (69 to 180 CE) with Eastern Han (25 to 168 CE), contemporaneous agrarian empires; Venice with Genoa (both 1290 to 1600), maritime republics.

**Scores (consensus of the two passes, 0 to 4).** "Missing": two or more indicators without opened evidence. "Unres.": scored in one pass only.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sparta | 0.5 | 3 | 4 | 3 |
| Athens | 3 | 3 | 3 | missing |
| Ming | 4 | 4 | missing | missing |
| Qing | missing | unres. | missing | missing |
| Mughal | 4 | missing | missing | 1 |
| Ottoman | missing | missing | missing | missing |
| Roman High Empire | 4 | 4 | 4 | 3 |
| Eastern Han | missing | 4 | missing | missing |
| Venice | 4 | unres. | missing | 4 |
| Genoa | missing | 1 | missing | missing |
| *Quadratic-weighted kappa (cells)* | *0.95 (6)* | *1.00 (6)* | *1.00 (3)* | *1.00 (4)* |

Overall quadratic-weighted kappa is 0.98 (linear 0.95) on 19 cells scored in both passes, with 18 exact agreements. No virtue falls below 0.6, so none is unresolved on kappa. With 3 to 6 cells per virtue and many scores at the ceiling, these values are unstable, and because one AI system made both passes they overstate reliability.

**Justice anchor.** Blanton and Fargher's scores were obtained (Blanton and Fargher 2009, Table 1). Rescaled to 0 to 4 they give Athens 3.08, Venice 2.97, Ming 2.88, Rome 2.39, Mughal 2.08, Ottoman 1.24. The pilot scores Ming, Mughal, Rome and Venice at 4 and Athens at 3, so the binary justice items saturate and do not reproduce the anchor's ranking. On the anchor, Mughal against Ottoman differs by 0.84, under the 2-point rule.

**Anomaly calls.**

- **Sparta, justice: atrophy (both tests).** 2.5 below Athens; 2.8 below its own mean on the other three. Evidence: no state treasury and broad tax exemptions, ephors "easily bought" and judging without written rules, no sanction on principals shown as applied (Aristotle, *Politics* 1270b, 1271b; Xenophon, *Lac.* 15).
- **Athens, justice: hypertrophy against its match only.** The same gap seen from the other side, not a second finding; within its own profile justice is level.
- **Sparta, fortitude: none.** It scores 4, 1.0 above Athens and 1.8 above its own profile, short of the 2-point rule.
- **Roman High Empire: none** on any virtue. **Eastern Han: none** on prudence, the only virtue scored on both sides.
- **Not assessable:** Ming, Qing, Mughal, Ottoman, Venice and Genoa, for lack of a scored match or of two other scored virtues. The Mughal profile (justice 4, temperance 1) is the largest gap in the sample but cannot be tested with only two virtues scored.

**What the pattern shows and does not show.** It shows that the section 1 rule can be applied end to end and that it gives a definite, checkable call on one well-documented pair. On these indicators Sparta's imbalance is low justice beside high fortitude, temperance and prudence. It does not show any link between anomaly and collapse: nothing in the sample collapsed, decline is the usual outcome for cases and matches alike, and 6 of 10 polities could not be assessed. No significance test is possible or attempted.

## 5. Next step: a full human-coded study

1. Two trained historians per region code from specialist histories (for example the Cambridge histories of China, Islam, and Greece and Rome), blind to each other, with the codebook revised so that justice does not saturate (graded items rather than binary ones, checked against Blanton and Fargher's scores).
2. Fill the missing cells first (fortitude and temperance for the empires; all of Genoa and the Ottoman Empire), and add a second match per case.
3. Add cases that collapsed on Tainter's and Cumming and Peterson's tests (Eastern Han is the nearest candidate once population losses are documented), so that the outcome column varies.

## 6. Caveats, in one place

- Tainter (1988, p. 4) is used from the standard quotation; the publisher page was not obtained **[uncertain]**.
- Shaker figures come from the first draft's full reading of Bainbridge (1984); the PDF did not load on 13 November.
- Britannica is tertiary, used only for Spartan chronology. No specialist history of Spartan decline was opened.
- Blanton et al. (2020) is one conceptual study. Its coding is reported from its own summary of Blanton and Fargher (2008), which was not opened.
- The coding thresholds (0-4 scale, 2-point gap, kappa 0.6) are CAV proposals, not validated instruments.
- Modern strength inventories (Peterson and Seligman 2004) do not measure the classical four without remainder, so they cannot fill the coding.
- The modern unity-of-virtues debate was not opened. Part B makes no causal claim.
- **Pilot: coders.** Both coding passes were made by one AI system working from secondary sources and some primary texts, not by trained human historians. No separate coding agent was available, so the kappa values are test-retest agreement and an upper bound.
- **Pilot: indicators and sample.** The prudence, fortitude and temperance indicators, the binary reduction of the justice items, and the scoring rule are CAV proposals. Ten polities were coded; only three on three or more virtues. Many cells are missing because the opened sources did not cover them (several Britannica pages loaded only in part).
- **Pilot: blinding.** The coders could not be blind to well-known outcomes, and window end points sit at recognized turning points.
- **Pilot: Blanton scores.** Obtained from Blanton and Fargher (2009), Table 1, not from the 2008 book's appendix. Their focal periods differ from the CAV windows for Athens and the Ottoman Empire. Their text gives a public-goods range of 10 to 30, their Table 2 gives 11 to 33; the pilot uses Table 2.
- Blanton et al. (2020) date the Wan-li emperor to 1567 to 1572; the Wanli reign ran from 1572 to 1620.
