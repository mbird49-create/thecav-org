## Abstract

Are social restraint, civic virtue, self-sacrifice, and common ethical norms declining in the United States? This study treats that as an open question rather than a premise, and tests it against long-run US data on crime, youth risk behavior, turnout, volunteering, giving, caregiving, trust, polarization, and stated obligations. It adds two further questions: how social media shapes the acquisition of virtue, and whether Americans are becoming less virtuous or less willing to accept obligations to people beyond themselves. Throughout, it separates declining harm from rising virtue, distinguishes three kinds of restraint, measures sacrifice by its cost rather than by participation alone, and keeps descriptive findings apart from normative conclusions. The organizing hypothesis is that virtue is migrating rather than simply declining. The evidence supports a qualified form of that hypothesis: the expressions of American virtue are migrating, while whether the underlying commitments are migrating with them is not yet known.

**Keywords:** civic virtue; social restraint; self-sacrifice; moral norms; personal obligation; affective polarization; social media; virtue migration; Chronological Ethics; Semantic Ethics

### About this study

The Center for the Study of Anomalous Virtues (CAV), a hybrid human-AI research organization, prepared this study with data current as of 9 October 2026. Figures were checked against primary data, official releases, or the original papers where these could be reached. Figures computed by CAV directly from public microdata are marked "CAV computation." Claims that could not be fully verified are tagged **[uncertain]**.

**Labels used throughout.**
- **Evidence**: documented findings and data.
- **Interpretation (descriptive)**: what the data show, as an empirical claim about behavior, attitudes, or trends.
- **Interpretation (normative)**: what those findings would mean for *virtue*. Normative claims depend on a view of what virtue is, and the data alone cannot settle them.

The verdict table (§13) gives descriptive and normative verdicts in separate columns.

## Executive summary

**Organizing thesis (stated as a hypothesis, not a finding).** *Virtue is migrating.* The forms, channels, and objects of American virtue are moving: from institutions to private and informal settings, from obligations owed to strangers and the polity toward obligations owed to kin and chosen causes, and from restraint grounded in community norms toward restraint produced by changed circumstances. §1.1 sets out what would confirm and what would refute this hypothesis. §11 scores it.

**Framing principle.** Virtue may be changing how it is expressed. The evidence does not yet show whether those changes preserve, strengthen, or weaken the underlying moral commitments.

**Headline findings, descriptive and normative kept apart.**

- **Social restraint.**
  - *Descriptive:* harmful behavior fell sharply over the long run. Violent victimization fell from 79.8 per 1,000 (1993) to 23.3 (2024) (BJS). The FBI's 2025 murder rate of 4.1 per 100,000 ties 1955–56 for the lowest on record, now confirmed against the FBI's own report. Teen births, binge drinking, and teen sex are at or near record lows.
  - *Descriptive:* most of these declines fit *behavioral restraint* (not doing harm, for whatever reason) and *restricted opportunity* (less unsupervised time, more surveillance and security, more time at home online) better than *moral restraint* (refraining because one believes the act is wrong). Among 12th graders, binge drinking fell from 32% (1998) to 8.8% (2024), but disapproval of weekend binge drinking rose only from 63.8% to 73.0%. Disapproval of trying marijuana *fell* from 54.8% (2009) to 39.7% (2024) while overall abstention rose (Monitoring the Future).
  - *Normative:* declining harmful behavior is related to, but not the same as, increasing moral virtue. On present evidence, America has become *safer* far more clearly than it has become *more restrained in the moral sense*.
- **Civic virtue.**
  - *Descriptive:* Americans are joining and participating at stable or higher rates. Turnout reached 66.4% in 2020, formal volunteering was 28.3% in 2023, and informal helping was 54.2%. They are measurably worse at regarding opponents as fellow citizens. In CAV's computation from the ANES cumulative file, partisans' ratings of the other party fell from about 48 (1978) to about 20–21 (2024) on a 0–100 scale, while ratings of their own party held near 70. Majorities of both parties now call the other party "more immoral" than other Americans (72% of Republicans and 63% of Democrats in 2022, up from 47% and 35% in 2016; Pew). Stated support for *compromise* has not collapsed (Gallup: 47–54% since 2010).
  - *Normative:* the hypothesis "better at joining causes, worse at cooperating with people who disagree" is **supported for attitudes toward opponents** and **not tested for behavior**. No national series measures actual cooperation across party lines in local governance.
- **Self-sacrifice.**
  - *Descriptive:* measured by **cost**, not just participation, the picture is uneven:
    - Individual giving was 1.7% of disposable personal income in 2025, tied for its 40-year low, against a high of 2.4% in 2005 (Giving USA, via secondary summary).
    - Hours per volunteer fell from 96.5 (2017) to 70 (2023).
    - Family caregiving is large and intense: 63 million caregivers averaging 27 hours a week. But 56% say they had *no choice* (AARP/NAC 2025).
    - Living kidney donors accept small but real risks: 3.1 deaths per 10,000 within 90 days in 1994–2009, and lifetime end-stage kidney disease risk of 90 per 10,000 against 14 for matched non-donors.
  - *Normative:* much of today's heaviest sacrifice is *imposed by circumstance* rather than chosen. That complicates reading it as rising virtue.
- **Common ethical norms (now a central question).** Can a society keep high individual ethical behavior while losing agreement about what ethical behavior is?
  - *Descriptive:* the US is currently doing both at once. Harm is at or near record lows, and cooperation among strangers in lab dilemmas did not decline. Meanwhile, agreement on contested norms is thin, and partisans increasingly *moralize* each other.
  - *Normative:* whether this combination is stable is unknown. The study identifies the mechanisms by which disagreement could erode behavior and finds no evidence yet that it has.
- **Personal obligation.**
  - *Descriptive:* duty items are mixed.
    - In the GSS/ISSP citizenship module, the share calling voting "very important" (7 on a 7-point scale) fell from 67.4% (2004) to 59.8% (2014), and helping worse-off Americans from 49.7% to 41.9% (CAV computation).
    - In ANES, 51.2% (2016) to 54.8% (2024) call voting "mainly a duty" (unweighted codebook figures).
    - Approval of older parents living with grown children *rose* from 33.0% (1973) to 55–60% (2016–22).
    - Agreement that adult children have a duty to look after elderly parents was 74.4% (2002) and 72.2% (2018).
    - In books, "my responsibility" became about four times as frequent from 1950 to 2022, while "obligations" and "entitled to" fell.
  - *Normative:* the evidence does **not** support a simple shift from "responsibility" to "rights." It better fits a narrowing of the *radius* of accepted obligation, toward kin and chosen causes and away from strangers and the state, with weak and partly mode-confounded data.
- **Social media.**
  - *Descriptive:* there is strong evidence that platforms reward moral-emotional and out-group-hostile expression (Brady et al. 2017, 2021; Rathje et al. 2021), and good evidence that they enable rapid prosocial mobilization.
  - *Descriptive:* there is only weak and contested causal evidence that social media changed adolescents' moral development or overall well-being at the population level (Orben & Przybylski 2019; Odgers 2024; National Academies 2024, against Haidt 2024 and Twenge). No study found here measures virtue acquisition directly.
- **Data verification (§15).** FBI 2025: **confirmed** against the FBI's own report, with one correction (the final violent-crime *rate* decline is 9.7%; the 9.3% in the May release is an earlier figure). FY2025 Army recruiting: **confirmed** (army.mil). FY2023 Army: **confirmed** (army.mil transcript). FY2022 Army: **confirmed in substance** by several outlets, with the official DoD table still **[uncertain]** because the source could not be accessed. FY2025 all-service totals: **partly unverified**, because the Department of War release could not be accessed.

## 1. Framing, organizing thesis, and method

### 1.1 Organizing thesis: "virtue is migrating" (a hypothesis)

**The hypothesis.** American virtue is not simply declining or rising. It is *migrating* along four dimensions:

| Dimension of migration | From | To |
|---|---|---|
| **Channel** | Formal institutions (congregations, fraternal federations, enlistment, party organizations) | Informal, private, episodic, and networked channels (informal helping, family caregiving, crowdfunding, online mobilization) |
| **Object** | Strangers, the polity, "the public" | Kin, chosen causes, and like-minded networks |
| **Ground of restraint** | Shared moral norms enforced by community | Changed circumstances: less unsupervised time, surveillance, security technology, risk-aversion |
| **Vocabulary** | Obligation-words ("duty," "obligations," "entitled to") | Capacity- and responsibility-words ("resilience," "self-care," "my responsibility") |

**What would confirm it.** Each condition must hold on *behavioral* data, not only attitudes:
1. Declines in institutional channels are matched by rises in non-institutional channels of comparable *cost* (hours, money, risk).
2. Total costly prosocial effort is roughly stable, while its distribution across channels and objects shifts.
3. Behavior remains stable or improves where community-norm enforcement has weakened, while moral attitudes shift. That pattern is what one would see if the *grounds* of good conduct moved rather than disappeared.
4. Within-person or within-cohort data show the same people expressing virtue through new forms, rather than different people dropping out and joining.

**What would refute it.**
1. **Simple decline:** institutional channels fall and nothing of comparable cost replaces them, so total costly prosocial effort per person falls.
2. **Pure substitution of cheap for costly:** new channels are mostly low-cost (likes, shares, one-off donations) while costly forms fall. That would be *dilution*, not migration.
3. **Circumstance only:** the "new" forms (caregiving, restraint) are explained entirely by demand or constraint (aging, surveillance), with no change in motive. That would be *displacement by circumstance*, not migration of virtue.
4. **No movement:** the old forms are stable, and apparent change is measurement drift.

**Interpretation (descriptive).** The hypothesis is stated so that it can fail. §11 scores each condition. In brief: condition 1 is partly met (informal helping and caregiving up, formal channels down), condition 2 cannot be tested with current data, condition 3 is met for restraint, and condition 4 is untested. Refutation 2 (dilution) and refutation 3 (circumstance) both have real support. The thesis survives as a working hypothesis only in a qualified form.

### 1.2 Framing principle

**Virtue may be changing how it is expressed, but the evidence does not yet show whether those changes preserve, strengthen, or weaken the underlying moral commitments.**

Every section below applies this principle. A change in *form* (where, how, toward whom) is an empirical question that data can partly answer. A change in *commitment* (whether people hold, and act from, the same moral reasons) is mostly unmeasured. Most of the available series capture form. This is why many verdicts in §13 are firmer descriptively than normatively.

### 1.3 Declining harm is not the same as rising virtue

**Declining harmful behavior is related to, but not the same as, increasing moral virtue.** The two can diverge in four ways:
1. **Opportunity.** Harm can fall because opportunities fell (fewer cars to steal without keys, fewer unsupervised evenings), with no change in character.
2. **Deterrence.** Harm can fall because detection rose (cameras, phones, digital records). Classical virtue ethics would call this continence under supervision, not virtue.
3. **Withdrawal.** Harm can fall because people withdraw from shared life. A teenager who never goes out neither commits nor resists vandalism.
4. **Motive.** Conversely, virtue can rise without measured harm falling, for example where virtue shows up as courage, generosity, or justice rather than abstention.

This study therefore treats crime, risk-behavior, and safety series as evidence about **behavioral outcomes**, and only indirectly as evidence about **virtue**.

### 1.4 Three kinds of restraint

| Kind | Definition | Example | Typical measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **(a) Moral restraint** | Refraining because one believes the act is wrong | A student declines to drink because she thinks binge drinking is wrong | Attitudes of personal disapproval *paired with* behavior; self-reported reasons for abstaining |
| **(b) Behavioral restraint** | Not doing harm, whatever the reason | Crime, births, and risk-behavior rates | Most outcome series (FBI, NCVS, NCHS, YRBS, MTF prevalence) |
| **(c) Restricted opportunity** | No harm because the opportunity or temptation is absent | Teens at home online on Friday night; immobilizers that prevent car theft; cameras | Time-use data; security and surveillance indicators; routine-activity measures |

*Routine-activity theory* (Cohen & Felson 1979) holds that crime requires a motivated offender, a suitable target, and the absence of a capable guardian, converging in time and space. Changes in daily routines can therefore change crime with no change in offenders' motivation. Osgood et al. (1996) extended this to individual deviance: unstructured socializing with peers, away from authority figures, predicts deviant behavior. These theories predict that less unsupervised peer time will reduce teen risk behavior independently of moral beliefs.

Outcome series capture (b) by construction. They are consistent with (a) or (c) and cannot distinguish between them on their own. Distinguishing them requires attitude data, time-use data, or both (§3.5).

### 1.5 Other distinctions used throughout

- **Behavior vs. attitude.** Turnout, volunteering, crime, and giving are behaviors. Trust, confidence, and moral ratings are attitudes. They often move in different directions.
- **Level vs. perception of change.** "How honest are people?" and "Are people less honest than before?" are different questions with different answers (Mastroianni & Gilbert 2023).
- **Period vs. cohort.** A trend can come from everyone changing at once (period) or from older generations being replaced by younger ones (cohort). Separating these is statistically hard (Fosse & Winship 2019).
- **Participation vs. cost.** "How many people gave?" differs from "How much did giving cost them?" (§5).
- **Chosen vs. imposed sacrifice.** A cost accepted freely differs morally from a cost circumstances force on someone (§5.4).

## 3. Social restraint

### 3.1 Crime

**Evidence.**
- The NCVS, a household survey that captures crimes not reported to police, estimated **79.8 violent victimizations per 1,000 persons age 12+ in 1993, 21.0 in 2019, and 23.3 in 2024** (BJS, *Criminal Victimization, 2024*). BJS describes 2024 as similar to 2023 (22.5).
- The FBI's reported violent-crime rate peaked at **758.2 per 100,000 in 1991** (FBI UCR, as compiled by CRS). The FBI's own 2025 report gives **327.6 per 100,000 in 2025**, down from an updated **362.9 in 2024**, a **9.7% decline in the rate**. This is the largest year-to-year decline in violent-crime rates since FBI estimates began in 1936 (FBI, *UCR Summary of Reported Crimes in the Nation, 2025*, pp. 3–4; FBI press release, 14 August 2026). *Checked against the FBI's own report; see §15.*
- **Murder.** Murder rose **29.4% in 2020**. The murder rate reached a 20-year high of **6.6 per 100,000 in 2020 and 2022**. Murder and nonnegligent manslaughter *offenses* fell an estimated **18.1%** in 2025, and the *rate* fell **18.5%**, to **4.1 per 100,000**, tied with 1955 and 1956 for the lowest since FBI estimates began (FBI 2025).
- In 30 cities, homicides in January–June 2026 were **18% below** the same period of 2025 (Council on Criminal Justice, mid-year 2026). City samples are not national estimates.

**Which kind of restraint this captures.** Crime rates capture **(b) behavioral restraint** only. The criminological literature attributes much of the post-1990s crime drop to factors that fit **(c) restricted opportunity** and changes in guardianship. Examples include the "security hypothesis," which links declines in vehicle and property crime to immobilizers, central locking, and other target hardening (Farrell, Tilley & Tseloni 2014). Policing, incarceration, demographics, and economic conditions also feature in that literature. No national data measure whether would-be offenders came to believe crime was *more wrong*.

### 3.2 Traffic

**Evidence.** Traffic deaths per 100 million vehicle miles fell to **1.08 in 2014**, rose to **1.34 in 2020 and 1.38 in 2021**, and fell back to an estimated **1.10 in 2025** (NHTSA, DOT HS 813 800).

**Which kind.** Mostly **(b)**, heavily mixed with vehicle safety and medicine, which concern neither the driver's restraint nor their opportunity. The 2020–21 spike, widely attributed to riskier driving on emptier roads, is one of the few recent series where a *loss* of (b) is visible and then reverses.

### 3.3 Youth risk behavior

**Evidence: outcomes (kind b).**
- The teen birth rate (ages 15–19) fell from **61.8 per 1,000 in 1991** to **11.7 in 2025** (provisional) (CDC NCHS, VSRR No. 43).
- High school students who had *ever had sexual intercourse* fell from **54.1% in 1991** to **38.4% in 2019**, **30.0% in 2021**, and **31.6% in 2023** (CDC YRBS, via childstats.gov table BEH4.A).
- Among 12th graders, **66%** abstained from alcohol, cannabis, and nicotine in the past 30 days in 2025, compared with **24% in 1976** (MTF 2026, vol. 1).
- Binge drinking (five or more drinks in a row in the past two weeks) among 12th graders peaked at **41% in 1981**, fell to **28% in 1993**, rose to **32% in 1998**, and fell to **8.8% in 2024** (MTF 2025, detailed report, ch. 8).

**Evidence: attitudes (a proxy for the belief component of kind a).** MTF asks 12th graders whether they *personally disapprove* of people 18 or older doing various things. The table below is from MTF 2025, Table 8-6:

| 12th graders who personally disapprove of… | Earlier | Peak/other | 2024 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Five or more drinks once or twice each weekend | 55.6% (1980) | 70.7% (1992); 63.8% (1998); 75.8% (2018) | 73.0% |
| Trying marijuana once or twice | 54.8% (2009) | n/a | 39.7% |
| Using marijuana regularly | 80.3% (2009) | n/a | 68.2% |
| Taking one or two drinks nearly every day | 70.5% (2009) | 67.4% (2021, low) | 79.0% |

MTF notes a mode effect: disapproval of weekend binge drinking reads about **5 points lower** on electronic devices than on paper. On a paper basis, the 2024 figure would be about 78% (MTF 2025, ch. 8). MTF also reports that the share of 12th graders who perceive *peers* as disapproving of weekend binge drinking reached a record **77% in 2024**.

**Evidence: opportunity (kind c).**
- Among 12th graders, the share who got together with friends "almost every day" fell from **52% in the late 1970s to 28% in 2017** (Twenge, Spitzberg & Campbell 2019, using MTF).
- Twenge and Park (2019) document declines across 1976–2016 in U.S. adolescents' "adult activities": dating, going out without parents, driving, working for pay, drinking, and sex. They interpret this as a "slow life strategy," not as moral change.
- In the American Time Use Survey, time spent with friends among 15–24-year-olds fell by an estimated **377 hours per year between 2003 and 2019**, the steepest drop of any age group (Kannan & Veazie 2023).

**Interpretation (descriptive).** For youth risk behavior, the evidence points mainly to **(b) and (c)**:
1. **Alcohol.** Binge drinking fell by roughly three-quarters from 1998 to 2024 (32% to 8.8%). Personal disapproval rose about 9 points (63.8% to 73.0%), or about 14 points with the paper-equivalent adjustment. A shift in moral belief of that size *could* contribute, but it is small relative to the behavioral change. Disapproval was also *higher* in 1992 (70.7%) than in 1998 (63.8%), while binge drinking was 28% in 1993 and 32% in 1998. So the link between disapproval and behavior is loose over time.
2. **Marijuana.** Personal disapproval of trying marijuana fell 15 points (2009–2024) while overall abstention rose. Here behavior became *more* restrained while moral disapproval *weakened*. This pattern is hard to reconcile with (a) as the main driver.
3. **Sex.** Ever-had-sex fell 22.5 points (1991–2023), with the sharpest fall between 2019 and 2021, the pandemic years. That timing points to opportunity (school closures, less in-person contact) more than to a sudden moral shift. No comparable long series on teens' moral views of teen sex was found.
4. **Time.** Unsupervised peer time fell sharply. Routine-activity theory predicts reduced risk behavior from this alone (Osgood et al. 1996).

The proposition that "most of the declines in crime and teen risk behavior reflect (b) and (c) rather than (a)" is therefore **supported for teen risk behavior** (medium confidence) and **plausible but not directly testable for adult crime** (low confidence, because no attitude series is paired with offending).

### 3.4 Delay of gratification and incivility

**Evidence.**
- Carlson et al. (2018) reanalyzed marshmallow-test data for 840 U.S. children aged 3–5. Children tested in the 2000s waited **about 2 minutes longer** (out of 10) than children in the 1960s.
- Protzko (2020) found waiting times rising by about **a fifth of a standard deviation per decade**. Most of 260 polled experts (84%) expected the same or worse.
- Caveats: small, non-representative samples (Watts, Duncan & Quan 2018).
- FAA unruly-passenger reports spiked to **5,973 in 2021** and fell to **2,076 in 2023** (FAA). No national behavioral series on everyday incivility was found.

**Which kind.** The marshmallow test measures a *capacity* for behavioral restraint (b) under controlled opportunity. It says nothing about moral motive. It is, however, the one series here that holds opportunity constant by design, and it rose.

**Interpretation (descriptive).** Harmful behavior has fallen greatly. The best-measured *driver* candidates are opportunity and routine changes (c). The one direct test of moral belief (a), MTF disapproval, moved much less than behavior on alcohol and in the *opposite* direction on marijuana.

**Interpretation (normative).** In line with §1.3, a society can become much safer without becoming more virtuous, and the US data are at least consistent with that. Two cautions apply against the deflationary reading:
- Aristotle's continent person, who refrains despite temptation, and the virtuous person, who is not tempted, both count as behaving well. A generation with less temptation may have less *occasion* for virtue without having less virtue.
- Restraint learned under restricted opportunity may become habit (*hexis*), which classical virtue ethics treats as the beginning of virtue. The data cannot tell whether that is happening.

## 4. Civic virtue: better at joining, worse at cooperating?

**The test.** Are Americans getting better at joining causes while getting worse at cooperating with people who disagree? Answering requires evidence on both halves: *joining* (participation) and *cooperating across disagreement*. The second half breaks down into affective polarization, cross-partisan ties, attitudes toward compromise, and local cooperation.

### 4.1 Joining: participation

**Evidence.**
- **Voting.** Turnout of the voting-eligible population in presidential elections was **63.8% in 1960**, **51.7% in 1996**, **66.4% in 2020** (the highest since 1900), and **64.3% in 2024**. Midterm turnout was **36.7% (2014)**, **50.0% (2018)**, and **46.1% (2022)** (McDonald, UF Election Lab).
- **Volunteering.** Formal volunteering was **30.0% (2019)**, **23.2% (2021)**, and **28.3% (2023)**. Informal helping of neighbors rose to **54.2% in 2023**, compared with **51.7% in 2019** (Census/AmeriCorps CEV, November 2024).
- **Protest and online mobilization.** These are covered in §9.3. Large episodic mobilizations are well documented, but no consistent long-run national series on protest participation was located for this study **[uncertain]**.
- **Associations.** Congregation membership fell from **70% (1999)** to **47% (2020)** (Gallup 2021).

### 4.2 Cooperating with people who disagree

**Evidence: affective polarization (ANES feeling thermometers, values verified).** CAV computed party thermometer means by year from the ANES Cumulative Data File 1948–2024, using UC Berkeley's SDA server. Settings: variables VCF0218 (Democratic Party) and VCF0224 (Republican Party); partisans including leaners (VCF0303); weight VCF0009z. Ratings run from 0 (cold) to 100 (warm).

**Evidence: moralizing the other side.** The share of Republicans saying Democrats are "more immoral" than other Americans rose from **47% (2016) to 72% (2022)**. The share of Democrats saying the same of Republicans rose from **35% to 63%** (Pew, August 2022).

**Evidence: cross-partisan friendship and marriage.**
- In 2017, **55% of Republicans and 64% of Democrats** said they had few or no friends in the other party (Pew, October 2017). No more recent comparable Pew figure was retrieved **[uncertain]**.
- Spousal party agreement rose from **73.2% (1965) to 81.5% (2015)**, and spousal disagreement fell from **13.0% to 5.8%** (Iyengar, Konitzer & Tedin 2018).
- The share who would be displeased or upset if a child married someone of the other party was **5% of Republicans and 4% of Democrats in 1960** (Almond & Verba). It was **49% and 33% in 2010**, in a YouGov online survey (Iyengar, Sood & Lelkes 2012). *Caution:* the 1960 and 2010 surveys differ in wording, mode, and sample, so the size of the change is **[uncertain]**.

**Evidence: attitudes toward compromise.**
- Gallup has asked since 2010 whether it is more important for political leaders to compromise to get things done or to stick to their beliefs even if little gets done. The share preferring compromise has ranged from **47% to 54%**: 47% (2010), 54% (2017), 50% (2018), and **47% (September 2025)**. The share preferring "stick to beliefs" was **24%** in 2025.
- In 2025, **58% of Democrats and 38% of Republicans** preferred compromise. Only **26%** said their own member of Congress tends to compromise, against **40%** who said their member sticks to their beliefs (Gallup, 29 September 2025).
- Pew (2017) found **58%** preferring leaders who compromise with people they disagree with, against **39%** preferring leaders who stick to their positions.

**Evidence: local governance.**
- Trust in local government was **67%** ("great deal" or "fair amount") in September 2024, against **55%** for state government (Gallup 2024, as reported). Local government remains the most trusted level.
- Hopkins (2018) documents the *nationalization* of state and local voting: local elections increasingly follow national party lines, partly because local news has declined.
- Newspaper closures reduced split-ticket voting by about **1.9 percentage points** in matched counties (Darr, Hitt & Dunaway 2018). This is a behavioral sign of more party-line voting where local information thins.
- **No national, repeated, behavioral series** on cross-partisan cooperation in local governance was found (e.g., share of unanimous council votes, bipartisan local coalitions, or conflict at school-board meetings). Reports of rising conflict at local meetings after 2020 are widespread but anecdotal or non-representative **[uncertain]**.

### 4.3 Trust and confidence

**Evidence.**
- GSS "most people can be trusted" (CAV computation, interviewer-administered): **46.6% (1972)**, **33.8% (2018)**, **24.8% (2024)**. Web versions in 2024 range from **15.7%** to **35.4%** depending on whether "depends" is shown (§10.6).
- Gallup's average confidence in institutions: **48% (1979)**, **27% (2026)**.
- Trust in the federal government: **73% (1958)**, **17% (2025)** (Pew).

This can coexist with a **civic-virtue surplus in participation**. Whether the participation is virtuous depends partly on its motive. Joining a cause chiefly to defeat a despised out-group is participation, but it is not obviously civic friendship. The data do not measure motive.

## 5. Self-sacrifice: measuring cost, not just participation

**The issue.** Many common measures of sacrifice capture only *participation*: how many gave, volunteered, enlisted, or donated. Sacrifice is a matter of **cost**. This section measures cost where data allow, along four dimensions: **hours**, **share of income**, **risk**, and **opportunity forgone**. It then separates **chosen** from **imposed** sacrifice.

### 5.1 Money: giving as a share of income and GDP

**Evidence.**
- Total giving was **$617.20 billion in 2025**, and individuals gave **$394.2 billion** (Giving USA 2026 press release).
- **Total giving as a share of GDP** was **2.0% in 2025**. It dipped to **1.9% in 2009–2011**, has been at or above 2.0% every year since 2012, and peaked at **2.3% in 2021** over the past 40 years.
- **Individual giving as a share of disposable personal income** was **1.7% in 2025**, unchanged from 2024. Over 40 years it ranged from a high of **2.4% in 2005** to a low of **1.7%, first reached in 2023**.
- These share-of-income figures come from Foundation Source's summary of *Giving USA 2026* (19 August 2026). The underlying Giving USA data tables were not accessed **[uncertain: secondary source]**.
- **Participation:** the share of households giving fell from **66.2% (2000)** to **46.9% (2020)**, while the average gift among donor households rose (PPS; Lilly Family School 2021, 2024).
- The share of all giving coming from individuals fell from **80% (1986–90) to 65% (2021–25)**, while the foundation share rose from **7% to 18%** (Foundation Source summary of Giving USA).

**Interpretation (descriptive).** Measured by *cost to the individual giver*, household giving has **declined** relative to income: from 2.4% of disposable income (2005) to 1.7% (2023–25). The aggregate is propped up by bequests, foundations, and large donors. This is a sharper finding than a reading of the totals alone ("stable aggregate, narrower donor base") would suggest. The typical household now gives a smaller share of its income, and fewer households give at all.

### 5.2 Time: hours and intensity

**Evidence.**
- Formal volunteers served **4.99 billion hours** in 2022–23.
- Average hours per volunteer fell from **96.5 (2017)** to **70 (2023)**, and the median from **40 to 24**. The Census Bureau calls these "ongoing declines" consistent with growth in *episodic* volunteering (Census/AmeriCorps, November 2024).
- Family caregivers average **27 hours per week**. **24%** provide 40 or more hours a week, **35%** more than 20 hours, and **40%** provide high-intensity care. **30%** have provided care for five or more years, up significantly from 2020 (AARP/NAC, *Caregiving in the US 2025*).
- **70%** of working-age caregivers were employed while caregiving (AARP/NAC 2025).

**Interpretation (descriptive).** Volunteering has become **broader but thinner**: about the same share participates, for fewer hours each. Caregiving has become **broader and deeper**: more people, longer durations, more help with activities of daily living. Most of the time Americans give to others now flows through the family, not through organizations.

### 5.3 Risk and opportunity forgone

**Evidence: living organ donation.**
- In 2025 there were **7,237 living donors**, up 3% (UNOS, January 2026).
- Risks to living kidney donors include perioperative mortality of **3.1 per 10,000 donors** within 90 days, for donations from 1994 to 2009 (Segev et al. 2010, *JAMA*).
- Estimated lifetime risk of end-stage renal disease is **90 per 10,000 donors**, against **14 per 10,000** for matched healthy non-donors (Muzaale et al. 2014, *JAMA*). The absolute risk is low, but it is several times the counterfactual.
- A more recent registry study reports **0.9 deaths per 10,000** within 90 days for 2013–2022 (*JAMA* 2024, as summarized in secondary sources; not read in full **[uncertain]**).

**Evidence: military service.**
- Only **23%** of Americans aged 17–24 were qualified to serve without a waiver in 2020 (DoD QMA study).
- Youth propensity to serve was **11% in spring 2025** (JAMRS).
- Earlier JAMRS readings appear somewhat higher on the agency's chart **[uncertain: values read from a chart]**.

**Evidence: financial strain and forgone work from caregiving.** **18%** of caregivers report high financial strain from caregiving (4 or 5 on a 5-point scale) (AARP/NAC 2025). The report also documents work disruption among employed caregivers. No national series measures foregone *lifetime* earnings from caregiving **[gap]**.

**Interpretation (descriptive).** The costliest forms of sacrifice for which data exist are now concentrated in **family caregiving** (time, health, money) and **living donation** (bodily risk). Both are rising in volume. Military service, the classic civic form of risk-bearing, shows low propensity and constrained eligibility, with recruiting goals met in FY2025 after shortfalls in FY2022–23 (§15).

### 5.4 Chosen versus imposed sacrifice

**Evidence.**
- **56%** of family caregivers say they **had no choice** in taking on caregiving (AARP/NAC 2025, Q39).
- Caregivers without a choice report more poor physical-health days (**5.8 vs. 4.6**) and more poor mental-health days (**8 vs. 4.5**). They are less likely to find purpose in the role (**42% vs. 62%**) and nearly three times as likely to feel isolated (**34% vs. 12%**).
- Black caregivers were the most likely to say they had a choice (**52%**).
- The number of caregivers rose **45% since 2015**, to **63 million** (AARP/NAC 2025). Population aging drives much of this.
- Living donation is by design voluntary, screened for coercion, and often directed to a relative. Military service has been voluntary since 1973.

**Interpretation (descriptive).**
- Sacrifice that is both **chosen and costly** (living donation, enlistment, sustained formal volunteering, giving a high share of income) is small in volume and mixed in trend.
- Sacrifice that is **costly but often imposed** (family caregiving) is large and rising.
- Sacrifice that is **chosen but low-cost** (episodic volunteering, one-off donations, crowdfunding contributions) appears to be growing, though no consistent series measures it.

**Interpretation (normative).** Whether imposed sacrifice counts as virtue is a classic question. On one view, virtue lies in *how* one bears a burden one did not choose: fidelity, patience, care. On that view, 63 million caregivers represent a vast, largely invisible exercise of virtue. On another view, sacrifice needs choice to be morally creditable, and rising caregiving mostly reflects a *gap in social provision* rather than rising virtue. The data are compatible with both views. They do show that the "no choice" group bears worse costs and finds less meaning, which bears on whether such sacrifice is *sustainable* as a social practice.

## 6. Common ethical norms: can behavior stay high while agreement erodes?

**The central question.** Can a society keep high individual ethical behavior while losing agreement about what ethical behavior is?

This question matters because two familiar views predict opposite answers:
- **The "moral consensus" view**, associated with Durkheim and with Devlin's position in the Hart–Devlin debate, holds that shared moral beliefs are part of what holds a society together. On this view, eroding agreement should eventually erode conduct.
- **The "overlapping consensus" view** (Rawls, *Political Liberalism*, 1993) holds that a pluralist society can be stable if citizens who disagree about comprehensive moral doctrines still endorse common terms of cooperation for their own different reasons. On this view, behavior can stay high so long as a *thin* core of agreement (do not harm, keep promises, follow fair procedures) survives beneath *thick* disagreement.

### 6.1 Evidence: individual ethical behavior

- Harmful behavior is at or near record lows (§3).
- Cooperation among strangers in social-dilemma experiments did **not** decline from 1956 to 2017. A meta-analysis of 511 studies with 63,342 U.S. participants found a slight increase (Yuan et al. 2022).
- In 107 survey items asking people to describe *current* conduct (being treated with respect, being helped by a stranger), responses were stable from 1965 to 2020 (Mastroianni & Gilbert 2023, study 4).
- The IRS projects a voluntary tax-compliance rate of **85.0%** for tax year 2022, similar to its 2014–16 estimate. This is not a usable trend, because projections assume unchanged behavior.
- *Partial counter-evidence (CAV computation, GSS).* The share saying people "try to be fair" fell from about **58–63% in the 1970s–80s** to about **49–54% in the 1990s–2010s**, and to **41.6% in 2024** (interviewer-administered). Web formats read higher (**48.6%** in 2024).

### 6.2 Evidence: agreement about what is ethical

- **Contested behaviors.** Gallup's 2026 moral-acceptability data show partisan gaps of **55 points** on abortion and on changing one's gender, **46** on gay or lesbian relations, and **43** on the death penalty.
- **Stable core norms.** Extramarital affairs remain the least accepted behavior (**7%** acceptable in 2026; Gallup).
- **Moralization of the other side.** Majorities of both parties call the other party more immoral than other Americans (§4.2; Pew 2022).
- **Moral foundations.** Liberals and conservatives weight moral foundations differently. Liberals emphasize care and fairness; conservatives give more equal weight to loyalty, authority, and sanctity (Graham, Haidt & Nosek 2009). This is cross-sectional evidence of *structured* disagreement, not a trend.
- **Citizenship norms.** Partisans agree closely on most good-citizenship items: voting, paying taxes, jury duty, respecting opponents' opinions. They diverge on following the law (Republicans 79% "very important" vs. Democrats 61%), knowing the Pledge (71% vs. 34%), displaying the flag (50% vs. 25%), and protesting wrong government actions (35% vs. 52%) (Pew, April 2018).
- **Obeying the law vs. conscience.** The share saying people should "obey the law without exception" rather than "follow their consciences" was **43.0% (1985)**, **43.4% (1990)**, **42.8% (1996)**, **54.1% (2006)**, and **39.8% (2016)** (CAV computation, GSS variable OBEYLAW, ISSP Role of Government module).

### 6.3 Interpretation

**Interpretation (descriptive).** The US currently combines **high and stable-to-improving behavior on core interpersonal norms** with **low agreement on contested norms and rising mutual moralization between partisans**. Agreement on *thin civic norms* (vote, pay taxes, serve on juries) is still broad across parties (Pew 2018). Disagreement is concentrated in *thick* norms about sex, life, gender, and authority.

So the answer to the central question, for now, is **yes, empirically, at least in the short run**. Behavior has stayed high while thick agreement has thinned. This fits the overlapping-consensus picture better than the moral-consensus picture.

Three mechanisms could still link disagreement to future behavioral decline. None is yet visible in the national behavioral data:
1. **Moralized out-groups lower the bar for harming them.** If opponents are seen as immoral, norms against cheating, lying to, or harming them may weaken *selectively*. Political violence and threats against officials are the relevant indicators. No consistent national series was retrieved **[uncertain]**.
2. **Loss of shared standards for judging conduct.** If people disagree about what counts as honesty or fairness, enforcement through reputation and shame weakens. The GSS "fair" decline (1970s–1990s) is consistent with this but predates the recent polarization surge.
3. **Institutional legitimacy.** Partisan swings in confidence (§10.7) may weaken the shared institutions that make cooperation among disagreeing people possible.

**Interpretation (normative).** A society whose members behave well toward each other while disagreeing deeply about thick moral questions is not obviously *less* virtuous. Liberal political philosophy treats that condition as the normal, even desirable, state of a free society. What would be normatively troubling is erosion of the **thin core**: the shared commitment to treat opponents as fellow citizens owed honesty and fair dealing. The rise in partisan moralization (§4.2) is the clearest warning sign on that front. The data show attitudes moving toward erosion; they do not yet show behavior following.

## 7. Virtue and personal obligation

**The question.** Has the American moral orientation shifted from "What responsibility do I have toward others?" toward "I have a right to do this"?

Rights and responsibilities are not opposites, and both are necessary. A right claimed by one person implies duties for others. A healthy moral culture holds both: the confidence to claim what is owed and the willingness to give what is owed. The empirical question is whether the *balance* has shifted, and toward whom obligations are felt.

### 7.1 Evidence: civic obligations (GSS 1984 and the GSS/ISSP Citizenship modules)

**GSS 1984 (CAV computation, weighted, n ≈ 1,440).** Respondents were reminded that citizens "have certain rights" and asked whether each item is a *very important obligation*, a *somewhat important obligation*, or *not an obligation* that a citizen owes to the country. Share saying "very important obligation":
- Reporting a crime one witnessed: **89.7%**
- Men serving in the military in wartime: **83.4%**
- Voting in elections: **79.0%**
- Serving on a jury if called: **64.7%**
- Keeping fully informed: **53.7%**
- Men serving in the military in peacetime: **32.3%**
- Community service: **28.7%**

The item set was asked only in 1984 in the GSS, so it provides a baseline but no trend.

- Vote in elections: **74%**
- Pay all the taxes you owe: **71%**
- Always follow the law: **69%**
- Serve on a jury if called: **61%**

Younger adults rate most duties lower: **56%** of those under 30 call voting very important, against **92%** of those 65 and older. Jury duty shows **47%** against **81%** (Pew, April 2018). Age gaps in one cross-section can reflect life stage rather than generational change.

### 7.4 Interpretation

**Interpretation (descriptive).**

2. **Civic duties toward the polity softened in 2004–2014 on two items.** In comparable interviewer-administered surveys, "always vote" fell from 67.4% to 59.8% and "help worse-off Americans" from 49.7% to 41.9%. Tax and law obligations held steady. After 2014, mode changes prevent a clean reading. 2024 in-person respondents look *more* duty-minded than 2014 respondents on several items, and web respondents look less.
3. **Voting-as-duty in ANES held at a narrow majority (51–56%) from 2016 to 2024.** This is a short series.
4. **Family obligation shows no collapse, and co-residence approval rose strongly.** The rise in approval of multigenerational living (33% to 55–60%) may reflect housing costs and immigration as much as obligation, so it is not a clean measure of duty. Agreement with filial duty was roughly stable in 2002–2018, with some softening at the "strongly agree" end.
5. **"Self and family first" is near-universal and slightly declining.** That is the opposite of what a rising-entitlement story predicts.

**Interpretation (normative).** The evidence fits a **narrowing radius of obligation** better than a shift from responsibility to rights. Obligations to kin are stable or stronger. Obligations to the polity and to unknown fellow citizens (voting, helping worse-off Americans, peacetime service) show softening in the one clean comparison available (2004–2014). Moral philosophers disagree about how to weigh "special obligations" to kin against "general obligations" to strangers. A narrowing radius is therefore not, by itself, a loss of virtue. It is a change in the *shape* of moral commitment that matters most for civic virtue.

## 8. Are Americans becoming less virtuous, or less willing to accept personal obligations to people beyond themselves?

**The question.** These are distinct possibilities:
- **Less virtuous:** a decline in character traits and conduct (honesty, restraint, generosity, courage) across the board.
- **Less obligated beyond the self:** character and conduct toward people one *deals with* are intact, but fewer people accept *standing obligations* to those outside their circle: strangers, the polity, future generations.

The two have different signatures in the data.

| Signature | "Less virtuous" predicts | "Less obligated beyond self" predicts | What the data show |
|---|---|---|---|
| Harm to others (crime, violence) | Rising | Unchanged or falling | **Falling** (§3) |
| Cooperation with strangers in direct interaction (lab dilemmas) | Falling | Unchanged | **Unchanged to slightly up** (Yuan et al. 2022) |
| Reports of contemporaries' conduct | Worsening | Unchanged | **Stable** 1965–2020 (Mastroianni & Gilbert 2023) |
| Obligations to kin | Falling | Unchanged | **Stable**; co-residence approval up (§7.2) |
| Informal help to neighbors | Falling | Unchanged or up | **Up** to 54.2% (2023) |
| Giving to strangers as a share of income | Falling | Falling | **Falling** (2.4% → 1.7% of disposable income; household participation 66% → 47%) |
| Felt civic duties (vote, help worse-off Americans) | Falling | Falling | **Softened 2004–2014** (GSS/ISSP); ANES duty stable 2016–24 |
| Institutional channels (congregations, enlistment propensity) | Falling | Falling | **Falling or low** |
| Regard for opponents as fellow citizens | Falling | Falling | **Falling sharply** (§4.2) |
| Hours per volunteer | Falling | Falling | **Falling** (96.5 → 70) |

**Interpretation (descriptive).** The pattern fits **"less willing to accept obligations beyond oneself (and one's kin and neighbors)"** much better than **"less virtuous."**
- Conduct toward people one interacts with (strangers in direct exchange, neighbors, family) is stable or improving.
- What has weakened is the *standing, impersonal* layer of obligation: proportional giving to strangers, felt civic duties, institutional membership, and regard for political opponents.

**Interpretation (normative).** Several moral traditions treat the impersonal layer as precisely where civic virtue lives: the willingness to bear costs for people one will never meet, and to treat adversaries as partners. On those views, the US shows a **real but specific** weakening. It is not a general moral decline. It is a contraction of the circle within which obligation is felt as binding. Other traditions, especially those centered on care and particular relationships, would read the same data as a *reallocation* of moral effort toward the people most directly in one's care. The data cannot choose between these readings. They can only show that the contraction, not general decline, is the phenomenon to explain.

## 9. Social media and the acquisition of virtue

**The question.** How does social media influence the way people, especially adolescents, acquire and develop virtue?

Virtue ethics since Aristotle holds that virtues are acquired by **habituation** (repeated practice), **imitation** of exemplars, and **social feedback** (praise and blame). Social media changes all three: what behavior is practiced, which exemplars are visible, and what earns praise. This section reviews the evidence on four channels and states plainly where causal evidence is weak.

### 9.1 Moral learning: what the feedback teaches

**Evidence.**
- **Moral contagion.** In 563,312 tweets on three polarizing issues, each additional moral-emotional word increased retweets by about **20%** (mean incidence-rate ratio 1.20). Diffusion was stronger *within* ideological networks than between them (Brady et al. 2017, *PNAS*).
- **Outrage is learned through reinforcement.** In two preregistered observational studies (7,331 Twitter users, 12.7 million tweets) and two experiments (N = 240), users who received more likes and retweets for outrage expressed more outrage later. Users also conformed to their network's outrage norms. In ideologically extreme networks, norm learning outweighed feedback (Brady et al. 2021, *Science Advances*).
- **Out-group animosity drives engagement.** In 2.7 million posts from news media and members of Congress, each additional word about the political out-group raised the odds of sharing by about **67%**, a larger effect than for negative or moral-emotional language (Rathje, Van Bavel & van der Linden 2021, *PNAS*).
- **Theory.** Crockett (2017) argues that digital media lower the costs and raise the personal rewards of expressing outrage, while weakening the feedback that would otherwise check it, such as seeing the target's distress.

**Interpretation (descriptive).** The evidence that platforms **reward and teach** moral-emotional, outrage-laden, and out-group-hostile expression is **strong** as regards on-platform *expression*. It includes experimental evidence for the learning mechanism. Evidence that this learning *transfers* to offline conduct or to lasting traits is **weak**. The studies measure what people post, not how they act or who they become.

### 9.2 Moral grandstanding

**Evidence.**
- Tosi and Warmke (2016; book 2020) define *moral grandstanding* as the use of moral talk for self-promotion, to seek status, rather than to advance moral understanding.
- Grubbs, Warmke, Tosi, James, and Campbell (2019, *PLOS ONE*) developed a grandstanding-motivation measure across six studies, including two U.S. samples matched to national demographics (N = 1,063 and N = 2,000) and two short longitudinal studies.
- Grandstanding motivation had *prestige* and *dominance* dimensions. It was associated with status-seeking and narcissistic traits, and it predicted more political and moral conflict in daily life, including on social media. Both dimensions explained **10.8%** of unique variance in conflict beyond personality in one study.
- Grandstanding was found across the ideological spectrum.

**Interpretation (descriptive).** Grandstanding is a validated construct, and it correlates with conflict. The research **does not show** that social media *increases* grandstanding over time, or that grandstanding has risen. No trend data exist. The causal direction (status-seeking leads to moral talk, or platform incentives produce status-seeking) is not established.

**Interpretation (normative).** For virtue ethics the concern is clear. If moral speech is rewarded for display rather than truth, people practice *seeming* virtuous. Aristotle and Kant both distinguish acting from virtue from acting for reputation. Habituated display could crowd out the inner motive. This is a plausible mechanism, not a demonstrated trend.

### 9.3 Prosocial mobilization

**Evidence.**
- The 2014 ALS Ice Bucket Challenge raised about **$115 million** for the ALS Association (ALS Association).
- GoFundMe reported more than **$30 billion** raised on its platforms since 2010, including its nonprofit platform Classy (AP; publication date not recorded **[uncertain]**).
- Jost et al. (2018) review evidence that social media help protest movements by spreading information, emotional and motivational messages, and network ties.
- Among U.S. teens in 2022, **80%** said social media makes them feel more connected to what is going on in their friends' lives, and **67%** said it makes them feel they have people who can support them through tough times (Pew, November 2022).

**Interpretation (descriptive).** Social media clearly lowers the cost of mobilizing generosity and collective action. This fits the "migration" thesis: prosocial effort moves into networked, episodic channels. It also fits the "dilution" refutation (§1.1): much online prosociality is low-cost, and no series measures whether it substitutes for, or adds to, costlier forms.

### 9.4 Adolescent development: Haidt and Twenge versus their critics

**Evidence: the case for harm.**
- Haidt (*The Anxious Generation*, 2024) argues that a "phone-based childhood," from about 2010–2015, displaced free play, in-person time, and sleep, and caused a rise in adolescent anxiety and depression.
- Twenge et al. (2018) linked rising adolescent depressive symptoms and suicide-related outcomes after 2010 to new-media screen time.
- *Generations* (Twenge 2023) emphasizes slower development and less in-person socializing.
- Supporting causal-design evidence includes the following:
  - The staggered rollout of Facebook across U.S. colleges worsened student mental health by about **0.085 standard deviations**, with effects strongest among students prone to unfavorable social comparison (Braghieri, Levy & Makarin 2022, *AER*).
  - Randomly deactivating Facebook for four weeks before the 2018 midterms raised subjective well-being by about **0.09 SD** and reduced political polarization by about **0.16 SD**, but also reduced news knowledge (Allcott et al. 2020, *AER*).

**Evidence: the critics.**
- Orben and Przybylski (2019) used specification-curve analysis of three large datasets (355,358 adolescents). They found the association between digital technology use and adolescent well-being negative but tiny, explaining **at most 0.4%** of variation. They compared it to the association with eating potatoes.
- Odgers (2024, *Nature*), reviewing *The Anxious Generation*, argues that most of the evidence is correlational, that effects are small or mixed, and that reverse causation (struggling teens use more social media) is plausible. She does not dispute that platform reform is warranted.
- The National Academies' 2024 consensus report concluded that the evidence **does not support** the claim that social media causes changes in adolescent health at the *population* level. It identified specific harms (online abuse, harmful content, unhealthy social comparison) and benefits for some users, and recommended targeted protections and better causal research.
- In large platform experiments during the 2020 election, switching users to a chronological feed or reducing exposure to like-minded sources for about three months did not measurably change polarization or political attitudes (Guess et al. 2023, *Science*; Nyhan et al. 2023, *Nature*). These were adults and short exposures.

**Interpretation (descriptive).** Where the causal evidence is weak, plainly:
1. **On virtue as such: no direct evidence.** No study found here measures the *acquisition* of honesty, courage, generosity, justice, or temperance as a function of social-media use. All claims linking social media to virtue are inferences from well-being, expression, or time-use studies.
2. **On adolescent mental health: contested.** Quasi-experimental studies of college students and adult deactivation experiments show modest causal effects. Population-level causal claims about adolescents remain unproven by consensus review (National Academies 2024).
3. **On displacement of in-person life: strong descriptively, weak causally.** Teens' in-person peer time fell (§3.3), but the decline began before smartphones (Twenge & Park 2019 trace it from the 1970s), so smartphones cannot be the whole cause.
4. **On outrage and out-group hostility: strong for on-platform expression, weak for offline character.**
5. **On prosocial mobilization: strong for capacity, unknown for durable commitment.**

**Interpretation (normative).** If virtue is acquired by habituation, imitation, and feedback, then a medium that rewards display, outrage, and hostility to out-groups is a *poor school* for justice, temperance, and civic friendship. A medium that makes generosity easy and visible may be a *good school* for some forms of beneficence. Both statements concern the *incentive environment*. Whether they describe what young Americans are actually becoming is not yet known. On restraint, social media may have contributed to the fall in teen risk behavior by keeping teens home (kind c, §3). That would make it a cause of *safer* behavior without being a cause of *virtue*.

## 10. Interpretations and counter-evidence

### 10.1 Putnam, *Bowling Alone* (2000), and its critics

**Evidence (argument).** Putnam argued that American social capital fell from the 1960s, driven mainly by generational replacement.
- Paxton (1999) found declining trust in individuals but not in associations.
- Ladd (1999) argued people joined *different* groups.
- Fischer (2005) questioned the coherence of "social capital."
- Skocpol (2003) described a shift from membership federations to professionally managed advocacy.

**Interpretation (descriptive).** The critics' "change of form" argument is an early version of this study's migration thesis.

### 10.2 Putnam & Garrett, *The Upswing* (2020)

**Evidence (argument).** An "I-we-I" curve, with communitarianism peaking in the mid-1960s.

**Interpretation (descriptive).** The curve treats decline as a reversible phase. It smooths over series that rose after the 1990s (crime, teen behavior, turnout).

### 10.3 Mastroianni & Gilbert (2023), "The illusion of moral decline"

**Evidence.** In 177 U.S. survey items (1949–2019), majorities reported moral decline on 84% of items, with no trend by survey year. Reports of *current* conduct were stable.

**Limits.** The archival items were not designed for this purpose. CAV's GSS "fair" series does show long-run erosion **[uncertain how the authors aggregated it]**.

### 10.4 Pinker and declining violence

**Interpretation (descriptive).** U.S. crime data since the 1990s support the long-run decline in violence. §3 adds that the decline is better explained by restraint of kinds (b) and (c) than by moral restraint (a), which Pinker's "expanding empathy" account would emphasize.

### 10.5 Generational vs. period effects (CAV computation, GSS trust by birth cohort, interviewer-administered)

| Birth cohort | Surveyed 1972–89 | 1990–2006 | 2008–18 | 2022–24 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1940–59 | 40.3 | 40.7 | 39.7 | 31.0 |
| 1960–79 | 31.2 | 28.0 | 31.0 | 29.3 |
| 1980–99 | n/a | 21.5 | 23.1 | 20.3 |

**Interpretation (descriptive).** Through 2018 the pattern fits cohort replacement. The 2022–24 drop in the oldest cohort may be a period effect or a mode artifact. This is not a causal decomposition (Fosse & Winship 2019).

### 10.6 Measurement drift and survey mode

**Evidence (CAV computation).** GSS 2024 "most people can be trusted":
- **24.8%** interviewer-administered
- **35.4%** web without "depends"
- **15.7%** web with "depends"

Two further mode problems apply:
- **GSS/ISSP citizenship items (§7.1).** In 2024, web respondents gave lower "very important" ratings than in-person respondents on every item, by about 5 to 15 points.
- **MTF.** Disapproval of weekend binge drinking reads about 5 points lower on electronic devices than on paper (MTF 2025).

Other drift: the NCVS redesign (2024), the FBI's NIBRS transition (2021), Pew's move to an online panel (2020), and the CPS volunteering redesign (2017).

**Interpretation (descriptive).** Mode is a first-order problem for every *attitude* series that crosses 2020. Several "post-2020 declines" in attitudes may be partly artifacts.

### 10.7 Partisan asymmetries

**Evidence.** Gallup institutional confidence by party diverges sharply in 2026, with gaps of 70 points on the presidency and 46 on the military. Moral-values ratings flip with the presidency (Gallup 2026). Trust in government follows party control (Pew 2025).

**Interpretation (descriptive).** Much measured "decline" in confidence is thermostatic. This connects to §6: what has declined is *shared* confidence.

### 10.8 Post-2020 crime and disorder

**Evidence.** Murder rose 29.4% in 2020, and traffic fatality rates and unruly-passenger reports spiked. All have since fallen. The murder rate reached a record-tying low in 2025.

**Interpretation (descriptive).** A shock produced a real, broad, short-run loss of behavioral restraint (b), which largely reverted within about four years. That pattern looks more like disruption than a decaying culture.

## 11. Testing the organizing thesis: is virtue migrating?

| Test (from §1.1) | Evidence | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **C1.** Institutional declines matched by non-institutional rises of comparable *cost* | Congregations down; informal helping up (54.2%); caregiving up (63M; 27 h/week); volunteering hours per person down; giving as a share of income down | **Partly met.** Caregiving and informal help rose. Volunteering became thinner and giving cheaper relative to income |
| **C2.** Total costly prosocial effort roughly stable | No series aggregates money, time, and risk across channels | **Untestable with current data** |
| **C3.** Behavior stable or improved while the grounds of restraint shift | Harm down sharply; moral disapproval flat or down on marijuana; opportunity sharply reduced | **Met for restraint**, though "grounds shifting from norms to circumstance" is also what refutation R3 describes |
| **C4.** The same people expressing virtue through new forms | No within-person panel links old and new forms | **Untested** |
| **R1.** Simple decline: costly effort per person falls with no replacement | Giving as % of income down; hours per volunteer down; household giving participation down; but caregiving up | **Partly supported** for money and organized time |
| **R2.** Dilution: new channels mostly low-cost | Episodic volunteering; crowdfunding; online mobilization | **Plausible, unmeasured** |
| **R3.** Circumstance only: new forms explained by demand or constraint | 56% of caregivers report no choice; teen restraint tracks opportunity | **Substantially supported** |
| **R4.** No movement; apparent change is measurement drift | Mode effects are large in attitude series; behavioral series are less affected | **Rejected for behavior; live for some attitudes** |

**Interpretation (descriptive).** "Virtue is migrating" survives as the best *organizing* hypothesis, because it accommodates more of the evidence than "decline" or "stability." But the strongest version, in which virtue moves intact into new forms, is **not supported**. The data fit a weaker version: **the forms and objects of costly good conduct have moved (toward family, neighbors, and episodic and networked action), while part of what looks like virtue's new home is better described as circumstance (imposed caregiving, restricted opportunity) or dilution (cheaper forms).** Restated with this qualification, the thesis is: *virtue's expressions are migrating; whether its commitments are migrating with them is unknown* (the framing principle, §1.2).

**Interpretation (normative).** If virtue requires choice and motive, then circumstance-driven and diluted forms count for less. The migration then carries a real *net loss* in the impersonal, chosen, costly layer. If virtue can be formed through burdens borne well, then imposed caregiving is a major site of contemporary American virtue, and the normative picture is more hopeful.

## 14. Open questions and CAV connections

### 14.1 Semantic Ethics

**Evidence.** "Duty" fell about 70% in American books from 1900 to 2022, while "resilience" and "self-care" rose steeply (CAV Ngram query, 7 October 2026). In the same corpus (§7.3), "my responsibility" rose about fourfold from 1950 to 2022, while "obligations" and "entitled to" fell.

**Interpretation (descriptive).** The vocabulary is moving from *impersonal obligation* ("duty," "obligations") toward *first-person responsibility* ("my responsibility") and *self-capacity* ("resilience," "self-care"). This parallels the narrowing radius in §7.4: responsibility is increasingly framed as something *I* take on, rather than something *owed* by position or status. This is hypothesis-generating material for Semantic Ethics, not evidence of practice.

### 14.2 Chronological Ethics

**Interpretation (descriptive).** Perceptions of moral decline have been constant for 70 years (Mastroianni & Gilbert 2023). This study suggests a second recurring pattern: **decline narratives conflate falling harm with rising or falling virtue**. The proposed CAV "declension audit" should record, for each era, *which kind* of restraint its data actually measured.

### 14.3 Open questions

1. Is there any national dataset that pairs risk behavior with the *reasons* for abstaining? A "reasons for not using" module in a survey such as MTF would directly test kind (a).
2. When will the GSS release VOTEELEC and HELPWRLD for 2024, if collected? And will NORC publish mode-adjustment guidance for ISSP items?
3. Can a behavioral measure of cross-partisan cooperation in local governance be built? Candidates include unanimous-vote shares on councils and school boards, and bipartisan co-sponsorship in state legislatures.
4. Can giving and volunteering be combined into a single *cost-weighted* series (dollars plus hours times wage plus risk) to test C2 in §11?
5. Is caregiving "no choice" rising? The 2025 AARP/NAC report says the finding "remains true" but does not give a trend figure in the text read.
6. Does any longitudinal study link adolescent social-media use to later *prosocial behavior or moral reasoning*, rather than well-being?
7. **For CAV's catalog:** is family caregiving under constraint a candidate *anomalous virtue*? It is practiced at enormous scale, praised in private, rarely named publicly, and morally ambiguous because it is so often unchosen.
