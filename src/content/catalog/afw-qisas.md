## 1. Entry details

- **Term:** ʿafw (pardon) within qiṣāṣ (retaliation in kind), usually with diya (blood money)
- **Native script:** Arabic عفو (ʿafw); قصاص (qiṣāṣ); دية (diya)
- **Language:** Arabic (Qurʾanic and juristic); the terms are used in Persian, Urdu and other languages of Muslim legal systems
- **Region:** Islamic legal tradition; applied today in, among others, Iran, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia
- **CAV pillars:** Sacred Axiology; Semantic Ethics; Ethno-Ethics

## 2. One-line gloss

In intentional homicide and wounding, the right to retaliation belongs to the victim or the victim's heirs, not the state, and so does the right to waive it, freely or in exchange for compensation, an act the Qurʾan praises but does not compel.

## 3. Etymology and semantic field

- **Evidence: Qurʾanic usage** (Sahih International translation).
  - Q 2:178 prescribes *qiṣāṣ* for the killed, then: "whoever overlooks from his brother anything [*fa-man ʿufiya lahu min akhīhi shayʾun*], then there should be a suitable follow-up and payment to him with good conduct. This is an alleviation from your Lord and a mercy."
  - Q 2:179: "there is for you in legal retribution [saving of] life."
  - Q 5:45: "whoever gives [up his right as] charity, it is an expiation for him."
  - Q 42:40: "the retribution for an evil act is an evil one like it, but whoever pardons and makes reconciliation [*ʿafā wa-aṣlaḥa*], his reward is [due] from Allah."
  - The same root means "surplus" in Q 2:219 ("the excess [beyond needs]").
- **Evidence: neighbouring terms.**
  - *Maghfira* (from *gh-f-r*) is forgiveness, in the Qurʾan mostly divine; Q 42:43 pairs human patience with forgiving (*ṣabara wa-ghafara*).
  - *Ṣafḥ* means "overlooking," as in Q 24:22: "let them pardon and overlook" (*wal-yaʿfū wal-yaṣfaḥū*).
  - *Ṣulḥ* is settlement.
- **Interpretation.** *ʿAfw* carries a sense of effacing or forgoing what one is owed. Q 2:178 calls the killer the heirs' "brother," framing pardon as a restoration of kinship within the community rather than a private feeling.

## 4. How it is practiced

- **Evidence: classical law** (Peters 2005).
  - Homicide and wounding are claims of the victim or the heirs.
  - The heirs may demand retaliation, pardon in return for diya, or pardon without payment. In the classical schools, pardon by any one heir generally bars retaliation **[uncertain: school-by-school variation not checked in detail]**.
  - The authorities may still impose discretionary (*taʿzīr*) punishment.
- **Evidence: modern codifications.**
  - Iran's 2013 Islamic Penal Code keeps qiṣāṣ and diya. Osanloo (2020), drawing on observation of more than 80 murder trials, describes a whole field of "forgiveness work": lawyers, judges, social workers, relatives and artists lobby victims' families to forgo execution, often up to the gallows.
  - Pakistan's Penal Code, as rewritten by the Qisas and Diyat law of the 1990s **[uncertain: exact ordinance and act dates]**, lets the legal heirs (*wali*) waive or compound qiṣāṣ. Courts may then impose taʿzīr sentences (PPC ss. 309–311).

## 5. History and change over time

- **Interpretation.** The Qurʾanic rules are commonly read as reforming Arabian blood-feud custom by requiring equivalence and favouring compensation and pardon. **Evidence:** classical jurists then systematized them (Peters 2005).
- **Evidence.** In the 20th century, many states folded homicide into state criminal law. From the 1970s to the 1990s, Pakistan, Iran and others reintroduced qiṣāṣ and diya rules as part of Islamization programmes (Peters 2005; Warraich 2005).
- **Evidence.** Reform has come through limits on the heirs' power, not its abolition. Pakistan's Criminal Law (Amendment) (Offences in the name or pretext of Honour) Act 2016 subjects waiver and compounding to s. 311, under which a court may still punish offences amounting to *fasad-fil-arz*, now defined to include honour killings. For killings "in the name or on the pretext of honour," it makes life imprisonment mandatory even after pardon.
- **Interpretation (Chronological Ethics).** The same text supports two different modern grammars: Iran builds an apparatus around persuading heirs to show mercy, while Pakistani legislators restrict pardon to protect women. Osanloo (2006) shows Iranian officials presenting Islamic mercy as an answer to human rights critique **[uncertain: paraphrase from the article's framing]**.

## 6. Why the generic English word fails

- **Interpretation.**
  - "Forgiveness" is an emotion or a moral choice. *ʿAfw* is a legal act with immediate effect on punishment.
  - "Pardon" in English law is a sovereign prerogative. *ʿAfw* is held by victims' families and can be refused even to the state.
  - "Blood money" sounds like bribery to English ears. Diya is a recognized substitute that Q 2:178 calls a "mercy."
  - The concept fuses what CAV distinguishes as forgiveness, pardon and settlement.

## 7. Evidence on outcomes or social effects

- **Evidence.** Osanloo's (2020) ethnography shows that the heirs' right creates sustained social and institutional pressure toward mercy in Iran, and that families' decisions involve grief, money, religion, reputation and state influence together.
- **Evidence.** Warraich (2005) documents how, in Pakistan, waiver and compounding allowed killers in "honour" cases to escape punishment, because the heirs who forgave were often the killer's own relatives. That record led to the 2016 amendment.
- **Gap.** No systematic cross-national data on rates of pardon, diya amounts, or effects on later violence were found.

## 8. Diplomatic and mediation relevance

- **Evidence.** In 2011 a Lahore court freed CIA contractor Raymond Davis, who had shot two men, after the victims' heirs told the court they had accepted blood money (reported at about US$2.3 million in total) and pardoned him. The heirs also received help with resettlement. A Pakistani official called the legal process "a fig leaf" for a deal between the CIA and Pakistan's intelligence service (Walsh & MacAskill 2011).
- **Interpretation.**
  - Where qiṣāṣ applies, a foreign government cannot pardon or obtain pardon by state-to-state means. It must work with the victims' families, which gives those families real diplomatic leverage.
  - The Davis case also shows the risk: when states are the real negotiators, the family's "forgiveness" can become a formality.

## 9. Cautions

- **Coercion and inequality.**
  - Poor families may face strong pressure to accept money.
  - Pardon can be bought or extracted by powerful parties.
  - In honour killings, the heirs who pardon may be complicit (Warraich 2005).
  - Diya rates have been unequal by gender. Iran's 2013 Code sets a woman's diya at half a man's (art. 550) and has a state fund pay the difference (art. 551), a fix the Center for Human Rights in Iran describes as leaving the discriminatory rule intact (CHRI 2019).
- **Not the whole tradition.** The heirs' pardon is one piece of Islamic ethics of forgiveness. Divine *maghfira*, everyday *ʿafw* and *ṣafḥ*, and *ṣulḥ* are separate entries, and none of them should be reduced to state penal practice.
- **Voice.** Muslim jurists and communities disagree about qiṣāṣ today. This entry describes; it does not endorse retaliation or any state's practice.
