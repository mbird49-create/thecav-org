- **Native script:** Pashto ننواتې / ننواته **[uncertain: orthography and the singular/plural form to be checked by a Pashto speaker]**
- **Language:** Pashto
- **Region:** Pashtun areas of southern and eastern Afghanistan and north-western Pakistan
- **CAV pillars:** Ethno-Ethics; Semantic Ethics

## 2. One-line gloss

A ritual in which an offender, or his kin, "enters" the house of the injured family in visible submission to ask forgiveness, which honour then obliges that family to grant, ending the duty of revenge.

## 3. Etymology and semantic field

- **Evidence.** The noun is formed from the Pashto verb *nənawatəl*, "to enter"; Rzehak (2011) glosses *nənawāta* as "entering [the house of the offended party]" and calls it "a ritual of forgiveness."
- **Evidence: the surrounding field** (Rzehak 2011; Landinfo 2011):
  - *badal*: "exchange," covering reciprocity for good deeds as well as revenge;
  - *poar* / *khunbahā*: blood money;
  - *jirga*: a council of elders that hears disputes;
  - *baramta*: taking hostages or property as security during talks;
  - *baad*: giving girls in marriage as compensation;
  - *melmastiyā*: hospitality; *panāh*: refuge or asylum.
- **Evidence: competing scholarly glosses.** Rzehak separates the forgiveness ritual from asylum. Barfield (2003) and Ginsburg (2011) present *nanawati* as a single principle covering both "submission" and "asylum," and Ginsburg calls it "a formal apology that consists of symbolic submission of the wrongdoer to the victim's family." Landinfo (2011) reads it as covering "both asking for forgiveness and forgiving."
- **Interpretation.** The "entering" metaphor ties forgiveness to hospitality. Once the offender is under the injured family's roof, the host's duty to protect a guest and the victim's duty to forgive converge. That convergence is why outsiders slide from "forgiveness ritual" to "sanctuary."

## 4. How it is practiced

- **Evidence: who and how** (Rzehak 2011):
  - The offender goes to the injured family's house to admit guilt, sometimes with a mullah, elders, or women of his family.
  - In older forms he came with a cord around his neck and grass in his mouth, as signs of submission.
  - Blood money is paid, and once the ritual is accepted the feud must end.
  - "A Pashtun is obliged to accept" it.
- **Evidence: variants** from the International Legal Foundation's survey of customary law (ILF 2004, quoted in Landinfo 2011, 13–14):
  - After a killing, the murderer's relatives may help carry the victim's body to the grave. A member of the murderer's family may lie in the grave dug for the victim, signalling surrender to death.
  - The aggressor may come with elders, learned men and "some old ladies" to slaughter a sheep at the victim's gate (for a dispute between families) or at the mosque (for one between tribes).
  - Refusing nanawati exposes the victim's family to accusations of defying Pashtun norms, "an allegation that no one can afford."
- **Evidence: institutional setting.** Nənawāta often runs through a jirga or shura. Analogous apology rites exist among other Afghan groups (e.g., *uzr* in Nuristan, *ozrana* among Hazaras, per Landinfo 2011).

## 5. History and change over time

- **Evidence.** Pashtunwali was transmitted orally. Written accounts are recent and were often produced by outsiders (Rzehak 2011). Steul's (1981) study, based on fieldwork in the Khost area **[uncertain: fieldwork site per secondary description]**, is a key pre-war German-language account of Pashtunwali as law.
- **Evidence.** The Taliban banned *baad* by decree in 1998 (Rzehak 2011). Rzehak stresses that three decades of war changed the formal side of Pashtunwali (elders, decision-making, tribal cohesion) more visibly than its values.
- **Evidence.** After 2001, Western military "human terrain" writing turned Pashtunwali into lists of countable tenets, a reduction Rzehak criticizes.
- **Interpretation.** Under Chronological Ethics, the entry's history is mostly a history of *description*: who wrote the code down, for what purpose, and with which terms fused or split.

## 6. Why the generic English word fails

- **Interpretation.**
  - *Forgiveness* is something a victim may give. Nənawāta is *requested by an act* and then *owed*: the plea creates a duty in the victim's family.
  - The units are lineages, not individuals. What is restored is the honour balance between groups.
  - It is bodily and spatial (entering, kneeling, carrying the body, lying in the grave), not an inner state.
  - It sits beside compensation and sometimes marriage exchange. English "forgiveness" hides that economy, and "apology," "surrender" and "sanctuary" each capture only one side.

## 7. Evidence on outcomes or social effects

- **Evidence.** No study isolates nənawāta's effects. The closest data concern jirga and shura outcomes. In the nationwide survey for the *Afghanistan Human Development Report 2007*, three-quarters of respondents said *solh* (peace or compromise) was always or sometimes the final outcome; half said compensation was. Most said *baad* and social boycott were "never or sometimes" the outcome (CPHD 2007, 93, quoted in Landinfo 2011).
- **Interpretation.** Ginsburg (2011) reads nənawāta and the jirga as devices that "minimize the endless spiral of violence." He also notes a perverse effect: because a known exit caps retaliation, these institutions may make initial violence less costly. That is a model-based argument, not a measured finding.

## 8. Diplomatic and mediation relevance

- **Interpretation.**
  - In Pashtun settings, an approach that stages humility in the injured party's space, through recognized intermediaries and with compensation, may carry obligations that a written apology cannot.
  - Practitioners should know that accepting the plea can bind the injured side. Using the form tactically, or without consent, risks humiliating the people it is meant to honour.
  - Hybrid justice proposals in Afghanistan (CPHD 2007) treated these customary forms as partners of state courts, subject to human-rights limits.

## 9. Cautions

- **Patriarchy and coercion.**
  - The parties are male-headed lineages. Women appear mainly as intermediaries or as compensation: the ILF describes a nanawati requiring "four copies of the Holy Koran, four women, and a fat sheep" for murder combined with abduction of a married woman (quoted in Landinfo 2011).
  - Landinfo notes "broad agreement that *baad* is a tradition that often results in violations of fundamental human rights."
  - A *duty* to accept can override a grieving family's unreadiness.
- **Romanticization and militarization.** Western popular culture has made "nanawatai" a byword for sanctuary. Examples include the 1988 film *The Beast* (written by William Mastrosimone from his own stage play named after the term) and widespread retellings of the 2005 rescue of Navy SEAL Marcus Luttrell, whose own memoir names the hospitality principle *lokhay warkawal* (Luttrell & Robinson 2007). These accounts blend asylum with the forgiveness ritual and should be treated cautiously **[uncertain: as flagged in the forgiveness study; popular usage not systematically surveyed]**.
- **Voice.** Nearly all English-language sources are by outsiders. Pashto-language scholarship and Pashtun women's perspectives (Rzehak notes older women's role in feud resolution) are underrepresented here.
