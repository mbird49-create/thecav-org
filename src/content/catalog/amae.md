## 2. One-line gloss

Presuming on someone's goodwill in a close relationship, typically by making a request that would be out of place elsewhere, together with the warm acceptance that can greet it.

## 3. Etymology and semantic field

**Evidence.** *Amae* is the noun form of the everyday verb *amaeru*. The kanji 甘 is the character for *amai*, "sweet," a link Doi himself draws on **[uncertain: a historical etymology beyond this graphic and semantic link was not verified]**. Doi defined amae as the ability "to depend and presume upon another's love or bask in another's indulgence" (Doi 1992, p. 8, as quoted in Niiya, Ellsworth, and Yamaguchi 2006).

**Evidence.** English renderings collected in the literature include "whining," "sulking," "coaxing," "pouting," "wheedling," and "being spoiled or pampered." Niiya et al. note that almost all carry negative connotations in English, whereas amae "does not ordinarily elicit disapproval in Japan."

**Evidence.** Kumagai (1981, cited in Niiya et al. 2006) analyzed amae as one pole of a pair: *amaeru* (seeking indulgence) and *amayakasu* (granting it). The field is therefore two-sided. It names both the presuming and the indulging.

## 4. How it is practiced

**Evidence.** The prototype is the parent–child relationship, but amae also occurs between adults: friends, couples, colleagues, and teacher and student (Niiya et al. 2006; Behrens 2004). Yamaguchi's behavioral definition, which Niiya et al. tested, identifies amae when (a) a request or behavior would be inappropriate for the person's age or status, and (b) the person expects it to be accepted because of the closeness of the relationship. A grown student asking a parent for an airport pickup, when a bus is easy, is a typical example.

**Evidence.** Behrens (2004) argues that amae differs across contexts and across three developmental phases. Treating it as a single trait, as many non-Japanese accounts do, has "often led to confusions and erroneous conclusions."

**Interpretation.** Amae is not a ceremony. It is a pattern in relationships: when to ask for what one is not strictly owed, and how to grant it so that the asking confirms closeness.

## 5. History and change over time

**Evidence.** The psychiatrist Takeo Doi was already writing about amae in English in 1962, in an edited volume on Japanese culture (Doi 1962). His Japanese book *Amae no kōzō* (1971) became a bestseller, and John Bester's English translation, *The Anatomy of Dependence*, followed in 1973. Doi called amae "a key concept for the understanding not only of the psychological makeup of the individual Japanese but of the structure of Japanese society as a whole" (Doi 1973, p. 28, as quoted in Niiya et al. 2006).

**Evidence.** Doi's work became central to *nihonjinron*, the genre of writing about Japanese uniqueness. Peter Dale's *The Myth of Japanese Uniqueness* (1986) attacked this genre, including Doi's use of Freudian terms and his claim that amae is uniquely Japanese. In a review of Dale, Ian Buruma (1987) agreed that the uniqueness claims were overdrawn but defended amae as a useful concept for describing Japanese social relations.

**Evidence.** Later empirical work recast the debate. Rothbaum et al. (2000) argued that attachment theory's measures of "security" build in Western values of autonomy and exploration, and used amae to illustrate a Japanese pathway to secure relationships through dependence. Yamaguchi (2004) separated amae from both dependence and attachment.

**Interpretation.** The concept moved from a clinical insight (1950s–60s), to a national self-description (1970s–80s), to a target of anti-essentialist critique (1980s), to a testable construct in cultural psychology (2000s). That arc is a Chronological Ethics case in its own right.

## 6. Why the generic English word fails

**Interpretation.**
- *Dependence* frames amae as a deficit. In Japanese usage it is often warm, and accepting it can be a gift.
- *Spoiling* puts all the responsibility on the indulger and treats the result as damage.
- *Trust* captures the expectation but not the deliberately inappropriate request that makes amae visible.
- *Healthy attachment* is close, but Rothbaum et al. (2000) argue that attachment theory's own measures carry autonomy-centered assumptions.

**Correction to the earlier catalog sketch.** The earlier sketch called amae "the art of being received," a competence. Niiya et al. (2006) found that the person making an amae request was judged to have as much control over the situation as the person asked. Japanese researchers they cite describe two motives: seeking affection, and getting what one wants. Amae can be a skill, a manipulation, or both.

## 7. Evidence on outcomes or social effects

- **Evidence.** In vignette experiments, Japanese students reported more positive and less negative emotion, and inferred a closer relationship, when a friend made an inappropriate request of them than when the friend asked someone else or no one (Niiya et al. 2006, Study 1, N = 119).
- **Evidence.** In Study 2 (58 U.S. and 66 Japanese students), Americans reacted in the same direction, and Japanese raters recognized amae in favors the Americans described from their own lives. One difference stood out: Americans felt *more in control* when asked, while the Japanese participants did not show this consistently.
- **Limits.** These are small student samples and hypothetical vignettes. The authors themselves call them "a starting point." No field study of amae's effects on cooperation or organizational outcomes was found **[uncertain]**.
- **Interpretation.** The evidence weakens the uniqueness claim. Americans have the experience but no word for it, which fits Levy's notion of "hypocognized" emotions (cited by Niiya et al.).

## 8. Diplomatic and mediation relevance

**Interpretation.**
- An amae-literate negotiator can tell a request for indulgence from a demand based on rights. Answering one as if it were the other can damage trust.
- Niiya et al.'s control finding suggests a cross-cultural trap. A party from an autonomy-centered culture may grant a favor as an exercise of power, while the requesting party experiences it as confirmation of closeness. The two sides may then read the same exchange differently.
- No study applying amae to diplomatic negotiation was verified for this entry. Claims that amae explains Japan's foreign policy should be treated as speculative **[uncertain]**.

## 9. Cautions

- **Nationalism and essentialism.** Amae was a pillar of *nihonjinron* (Dale 1986). Presenting it as the key to "the Japanese mind" repeats that error. Niiya et al. (2006) show that the experience is not unique to Japan.
- **Patriarchal and power features.** Classic examples, such as a wife coaxing a gift from her husband or a mother indulging a son (Niiya et al. 2006), sit inside gendered family roles. Indulgence also flows along hierarchies. Who may presume, and who is expected to indulge, is a question about power **[uncertain: not examined directly in the sources reviewed]**.
- **Clinical framing.** Western clinical readings may pathologize amae as dependency. Japanese clinical readings may underweight its manipulative side. Both are partial.
- **Commercialization.** Amae has not been branded the way sisu has, but the "untranslatable word" genre invites it.
- **Insider vs. outsider voice.** Doi wrote as a Japanese psychiatrist addressing both Japanese and Western readers. All sources here are in English. Japanese-language scholarship (for example Yamaguchi 1999 and Masamura 1998, both cited by Niiya et al.) was not reviewed **[gap]**.
