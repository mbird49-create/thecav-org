## 1. Entry details

- **Term:** sulha (also sulh, ṣulḥ)
- **Native script:** Arabic صلحة (sulḥa); صلح (ṣulḥ)
- **Language:** Arabic (Levantine colloquial and classical)
- **Region:** Levant (Palestinian, Israeli Arab, Druze, Bedouin, Jordanian, Lebanese communities); related forms across the Arab world
- **CAV pillars:** Ethno-Ethics; Semantic Ethics; Practice

## 2. One-line gloss

A community-led settlement of serious disputes, including homicide, in which a delegation of notables secures a truce, negotiates compensation and stages a public ceremony through which the injured family restores its honour by forgiving.

## 3. Etymology and semantic field

- **Evidence: the root.** The root *ṣ-l-ḥ* means to be sound, right or in good order. Related words are *iṣlāḥ* (reform, setting right) and *muṣālaḥa* (reconciliation).
- **Evidence: Qurʾanic usage.** "Settlement is best" (*wa-l-ṣulḥu khayr*, Q 4:128). "The believers are but brothers, so make settlement between your brothers" (Q 49:10, Sahih International).
- **Evidence: how participants gloss it.** Lang's Galilee informants rendered *sulh* as "reconciliation," "cooperation" or "forgiveness" (Lang 2002).
- **Evidence: related vocabulary.**
  - *Jaha* (*jāha*): the mediating delegation.
  - *Hudna*: truce.
  - *ʿAtwa*: Pely's term for the good-faith payment that secures the truce.
  - *Diya*: compensation.
  - *Sharaf*: honour.
  - *Musayara*: accommodating deference.
- **Interpretation.** The semantic centre is restoring a right order between groups. Forgiveness is the injured family's honourable act inside that restoration, not a private inner state.

## 4. How it is practiced

- **Evidence: the immediate aftermath** (Lang 2002, fieldwork in 16 Galilee towns and villages, 1992–1996).
  - After a killing, during *fawrat al-dam* ("the eruption of blood"), the killer's male relatives flee their homes.
  - A delegation seeks a *hudna* from the victim's family within hours.
- **Evidence: the jaha.**
  - Its notables may be Muslim, Christian and Druze.
  - Over months or years (a murder sulha takes "anywhere from six months to several years"), it shuttles between the families.
  - It persuades the injured family that they can "wipe away the stain" on their *sharaf* by forgiving.
  - Lang identifies three persuasive mechanisms: public shows of remorse by the offender's side, a reversal of *musayara*, and appeals to the injured family's magnanimity.
- **Evidence: the ceremony** (Lang 2002).
  - Hundreds or thousands of men attend; women and children watch from windows and the sidelines.
  - Compensation is handed over.
  - The jaha surrounds the killer to shield him, and the victim's father ties a knot in a white banner (*rayah*), "as if to show that the problem has been cleansed."
  - The parties drink bitter coffee at the victim's home, and the killer's family hosts a feast.
- **Evidence: relation to state law.**
  - Sulha "works in tandem with" Israel's state justice system, which gives it no legal recognition but cooperates.
  - Judges may give lighter sentences where sulha has been achieved, police may press the jaha to conclude, and prisoners may be given leave to attend (Lang 2002).
  - Pely (2011, East/West) finds that sulha mixes features of mediation and arbitration. He compares it with Western practice on confidentiality, venting and neutrality, and finds both overlaps and differences.

## 5. History and change over time

- **Evidence.** Lang's informants call sulha a "pre-Islamic custom" and claim it is in rapid decline, especially in cities. Yet major sulhas still take place among Bedouin, Druze, Muslims and Christians (Lang 2002).
- **Evidence.** Studies of sulha as a Middle Eastern conflict-resolution resource date from the 1990s (Jabbour 1996; Irani & Funk 1998). Pely's work in the 2010s studies sulha as practised today in Israel's Arab communities (Pely 2016).
- **Interpretation (Chronological Ethics).** Sulha has moved from a stateless-society mechanism into a parallel order beside states, and into an export concept in international peace-building literature.

## 6. Why the generic English word fails

- **Interpretation.**
  - "Forgiveness" puts the individual victim at the centre. In sulha, families and lineages forgive.
  - Mediators decide much of the timing and form.
  - The act is public, staged and reciprocal: the offender's side humbles itself, and the injured side gains honour by granting peace.
  - "Reconciliation" is closer but loses the honour economy. "Mediation" loses the ritual and the moral transformation. Sulha is all three at once.

## 7. Evidence on outcomes or social effects

- **Evidence.** Lang reports that despite the honour ideal of revenge ("One grave lies next to another"), "the vast majority of killings do not lead to counterkillings" in the communities she studied, and most families reconcile (Lang 2002).
- **Evidence.** Pely (2011, women) documents that women are formally excluded from sulha yet exert real influence behind the scenes.
- **Gap.** No controlled or longitudinal study of sulha's effect on repeat violence was found. Lang's statement is based on ethnography, not statistics.

## 8. Diplomatic and mediation relevance

- **Interpretation.**
  - Sulha is the most cited Middle Eastern model for "indigenous" conflict resolution. Irani and Funk (1998) argue that conflict resolution in the region should take Arab-Islamic rituals of reconciliation (sulh and musalaha) seriously rather than importing Western models wholesale.
  - Gellman and Vuinovich (2008) propose drawing on sulha principles in Israeli–Palestinian negotiations.
  - Its lessons are procedural: secure a truce fast, use respected third parties, let the offending side bear visible costs, and let the injured side's honour rise through the act of forgiving.
- **Caution on transfer.** Sulha works through dense kinship ties and shared notables. That structure is largely absent between states or national movements, and Lang (2002) shows how entangled it is with local and state politics.

## 9. Cautions

- **Patriarchy.** Men negotiate, attend and forgive; women are formally excluded (Pely 2011). Settlements can trade away individual women's interests.
- **Pressure and the state.** Police pressure, inequality between families and the reputation of notables can push families into settlement (Lang 2002).
- **Romanticization.** Sulha's decline in cities and its coexistence with state law are often left out of outside accounts. In Israel it also has a political dimension. Lang argues that the state tolerates and sometimes promotes sulha because it keeps the peace, and that sulha politics and state institutions reinforce each other through shared local leadership.
- **Name confusion.** "Musalaha" is also the name of a Christian reconciliation organization in Israel/Palestine. That ministry is not the customary practice.
- **Voice.** Most literature is by outsiders or by Palestinian Christian and academic insiders (Jabbour). Druze, Bedouin and Muslim practitioners' accounts are underrepresented.
