## 2. One-line gloss

A felt obligation to act in ways that honor one's self, family, and community, usually by doing more than is owed, judged against what one's circle already expects of a person like oneself.

## 3. Etymology and semantic field

**Evidence.** The word joins *phil-* ("love of") and *timē* ("honor, esteem, worth"). The standard Ancient Greek lexicon (Liddell–Scott–Jones) glosses *philotimia* as "love of honour or distinction, ambition," and notes it was "freq. in bad sense in early writers." Thucydides pairs it with *pleonexia* (grasping for more) in his account of civil strife (3.82), and Plato pairs it with contentiousness. Later senses include "lavish outlay for public purposes, munificence," and simply "honour, distinction, credit."

**Evidence.** In modern Greek ethnography, Herzfeld (1980) argued that "honour" and "shame" are "inefficient glosses" on Greek moral vocabularies. Using fieldwork in two rural communities, he analyzed *filotimo* alongside *egoismos* (self-regard). He found that the shared concern across these terms was less an absolute standard than "the ability to live up to already existing expectations."

**Interpretation.** The semantic field runs from *ambition* and *rivalry* (ancient negative pole), through *public generosity* (ancient civic pole), to *in-group duty and self-respect* (modern pole). English "honor" catches only a slice of this.

## 4. How it is practiced

**Evidence.** Vassiliou and Vassiliou (1973) interviewed a representative sample of 740 residents of Athens and Thessaloniki, aged 15 to 64, using the antecedent–consequent method developed with Harry Triandis (Triandis 1972). They described philotimo as "the highest Greek value, which regulates proper in-group behavior." Commonly cited expressions include hospitality, keeping one's word, supporting family, and repaying favors, owed above all to one's in-group **[uncertain: this list is not checked against the 1973 article's tables]**.

**Evidence.** Fotopoulou (2014) interviewed 40 people who use drugs in treatment at two state agencies in Thessaloniki. Participants explained their own decisions to stop using in terms of *filotimo*, which she describes as "a complex array of virtues that regulates behaviour towards one's family," and a wish to repair a "spoiled" identity.

**Interpretation.** Philotimo is not a ritual. It works as an everyday standard: people invoke it to praise ("he has philotimo"), to shame ("have you no philotimo?"), and to explain why they did more than they had to. The earlier sketch's emphasis on private, little-witnessed duty is only partly right. The standard is social, and the witness is one's circle, even when it is absent.

## 5. History and change over time

**Evidence.** Whitehead (1983) traces how Athenian honorific decrees of the fourth century BCE turned *philotimia* from a suspect elite drive into a civic virtue. The decrees rewarded citizens whose competitive spending benefited the city.

**Evidence.** Vassiliou and Vassiliou (1973) found that as "milieu complexity, education, and social involvement increase," the antecedents and consequences of philotimo shift. Conduct moves from in-group norms toward role expectations, and philotimo becomes more open to individual interpretation.

**Evidence.** Rozakou (2024) studied Greek police officers at migration-control sites during the overlapping austerity and migration crises. Officers known for anti-migrant attitudes also gave migrants medicine and food, and explained this care through *filotimo*. Rozakou reads this as officers defending their moral self-worth as agents of an "amoral state." She also shows that the same rhetoric "resonates with nativist claims to morality and moral superiority towards migrants."

**Interpretation.** For Chronological Ethics, philotimo is a clear case of a virtue word that changed its moral sign: a vice in early classical texts, a civic virtue in fourth-century Athens, a family and in-group value in mid-twentieth-century ethnography, and a contested national self-image under twenty-first-century crisis.

## 6. Why the generic English word fails

**Interpretation.**
- *Honor* in English suggests reputation defended against insult. Philotimo is just as often shown through generosity, and Herzfeld's evidence is about living up to expectations rather than avenging slights.
- *Pride* sounds self-regarding. Philotimo is relational: it is owed to someone.
- *Generosity* or *kindness* misses the element of obligation. With philotimo, failing to give is shameful, not merely ungenerous.
- *Integrity* (listed with HEXACO among the keywords of Mantzios's scale paper) leaves out the in-group frame that the 1970s data put at the center.

Mantzios (2021) defined philotimo as "a commitment to unconditional selfless acts that are aligned to a sense of a moral identity." That definition sits uneasily with the in-group findings of Vassiliou and Vassiliou (1973). **Interpretation:** CAV should treat that tension as data, not resolve it in favor of the more universal-sounding version.

## 7. Evidence on outcomes or social effects

- **Evidence (measurement).** Mantzios (2021) reports six studies (total N = 1,144) developing a Philotimo Scale, with tests of reliability, stability over time, factor structure, and validity against established scales. This is a measurement paper. It does not show effects on behavior.
- **Evidence (qualitative).** In Fotopoulou (2014), filotimo was part of how participants accounted for their own decisions to quit drugs and enter treatment. That is a self-account, not a measured effect.
- **Evidence (qualitative).** In Rozakou (2024), filotimo-framed care coexisted with xenophobia. The virtue did not displace exclusion; it ran alongside it.
- **Gap.** No study was found that measures philotimo's effect on cooperation, negotiation outcomes, or civic behavior **[uncertain: Greek-language literature not searched]**.

## 8. Diplomatic and mediation relevance

**Interpretation.**
- In disputes with Greek or Greek Cypriot parties, appeals framed as "respect" or "fairness" may miss what a party feels it is owed. A philotimo-literate mediator asks what generosity a party believes it has already extended without return, and what acknowledgment would let it extend more without losing face.
- Because philotimo is strongly in-group-oriented (Vassiliou and Vassiliou 1973), out-group parties may meet formal correctness rather than philotimo-driven generosity. Building relationships before bargaining may change which frame applies **[uncertain: no study tests this in negotiation]**.
- Rozakou (2024) is a warning. Appeals to a party's philotimo can produce real care, and can also flatter a sense of moral superiority over the people receiving the care.

## 9. Cautions

- **Romanticization and commercialization.** Philotimo is widely marketed as a uniquely Greek, "untranslatable" virtue. **Interpretation:** treating it as a national essence repeats the pattern Herzfeld (1980) criticized in "Mediterranean honour" scholarship.
- **Nativism.** The rhetoric can support claims of moral superiority over migrants (Rozakou 2024).
- **Patriarchal features.** Mediterranean honor vocabularies are often described as gendered. Whether and how philotimo sets different expectations for women and men was not established in the sources reviewed here **[uncertain]**.
- **Insider vs. outsider voice.** Most English-language sources are by outsiders, or by Greek scholars writing for international journals. Philotimo is lived and argued over in Greek. A Greek-language literature review and review by Greek-speaking scholars are welcome.
