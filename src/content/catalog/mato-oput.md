## 1. Entry details

- **Term:** mato oput ("drinking the bitter root"), with culo kwor (compensation for homicide)
- **Native script:** Acholi is written in Latin script: *mato oput*; *culo kwor*
- **Language:** Acholi (Luo, Western Nilotic)
- **Region:** Acholi sub-region, northern Uganda (and Acholi in South Sudan)
- **CAV pillars:** Ethno-Ethics; Chronological Ethics; Practice

## 2. One-line gloss

A reconciliation between clans after a killing: mediated truth-telling and compensation, then a ceremony in which both sides drink a bitter infusion of the *oput* tree from a shared calabash so that relations can resume.

## 3. Etymology and semantic field

- **Evidence.** *Mato* means "drinking"; *oput* is a tree whose root or bark gives the bitter drink. Its botanical identification is often given as *Harrisonia abyssinica* **[uncertain: not confirmed in a botanical source]**. *Culo kwor* is usually glossed as "paying for blood" or compensation for homicide **[uncertain: word-by-word gloss not confirmed in a dictionary]** (Liu Institute et al. 2005; Juba Annexure 2008).
- **Evidence.** The 2008 Annexure to the Juba accountability agreement defines Mato Oput as "the traditional ritual performed by the Acholi after full accountability and reconciliation has been attained," and Culo Kwor as "compensation to atone for homicide as practiced in Acholi and Lango cultures."
- **Interpretation.** Bitterness is the governing image: the parties swallow the bitterness of the past together. Forgiveness is not a separate step but is enacted through compensation and shared consumption.

## 4. How it is practiced

All details below are **Evidence** from *Roco Wat I Acoli* (Liu Institute et al. 2005, 54–58), based on elders' accounts and documented cases, unless marked otherwise.

- **Scope.** It applies to intentional or accidental killing between clans. It begins by separating the clans: social contact, sharing food and water, and intermarriage stop.
- **Voluntary admission.** The offending clan's admission "must be completely voluntary or not done at all."
- **Mediation.**
  - A go-between (*Larii Tal*) carries messages between the clans.
  - Elders establish what happened and negotiate compensation.
  - The case goes before the chief (*Rwot*).
  - Ker Kwaro Acholi, the paramount chiefs' institution, was trying to standardize compensation at 16 cows for intentional and 10 for accidental killing.
- **Ceremony.**
  - A stick is beaten.
  - A sheep from the offending clan and a goat from the injured clan are cut in half and exchanged.
  - The parties eat foods symbolizing the bitterness of the past.
  - Representatives kneel to drink the oput together from a calabash, then eat the animals' livers. All food must be finished.
- **Gender roles.** Women cook, brew and place the calabash; male elders lead (JRP 2012).
- **Limits.** It is not used for deaths in battle, and elders had not agreed on whether it can be applied to Lord's Resistance Army (LRA) atrocities.

## 5. History and change over time

- **Evidence.** The Liu Institute team recorded 50 cases, partially, for 2000–2005. More than half concerned killings from the 1960s–70s that were only completed after 2000. All 50 perpetrators were male (Liu Institute et al. 2005).
- **Evidence.** During the LRA war (from 1986), elders' authority and the cattle economy were badly damaged, and much of the population lived in displacement camps (Finnström 2008; Allen 2006).
- **Evidence.** From the late 1990s, NGOs, donors and the revived Ker Kwaro Acholi promoted mato oput as an Acholi alternative to prosecution. The 2000 Amnesty Act and the International Criminal Court's 2005 warrants for LRA leaders made the question urgent (Allen 2006, 2007; Baines 2007).
- **Evidence.** The 2007 Agreement on Accountability and Reconciliation between the Government of Uganda and the LRA states that traditional mechanisms "such as Culo Kwor, Mato Oput, Kayo Cuk, Ailuc and Tonu ci Koka… shall be promoted, with necessary modifications, as a central part of the framework for accountability and reconciliation" (art. 3.1). Joseph Kony never signed the final peace agreement.
- **Interpretation (Chronological Ethics).** Allen (2007) argues that "traditional justice" in northern Uganda was partly an invention of the international era: a repackaging of varied local rites into a single brand, promoted by outsiders and some leaders.

## 6. Why the generic English word fails

- **Interpretation.**
  - Mato oput is collective (clan to clan), so an individual's forgiveness is neither necessary nor sufficient.
  - It is conditional on truth and compensation.
  - It is ritually completed: once the oput is drunk, the bond is restored and normal relations, including marriage, can resume.
  - English "forgiveness" lacks the economic element (cattle, money) and the idea that unrepaired killing contaminates relations between groups.
  - "Restorative justice" captures more but suggests a modern program rather than an obligation embedded in kinship.

## 7. Evidence on outcomes or social effects

- **Evidence: population survey.** In 2007 (n = 2,875 adults in northern Uganda), 57% said those responsible for abuses should take part in traditional ceremonies (Pham et al. 2007).
  - Mato oput was the most often mentioned ceremony: 48% overall, 68% in Acholi districts, much lower in Lango (8.7%) and Teso (16.4%).
  - Only 8% chose "peace with traditional ceremonies" as their preferred route. 59% agreed that "it is important to have trials for the LRA leaders," while 80% preferred peace with amnesty over peace with trials.
- **Evidence: practical limits.** In practice, war-time cases rarely meet the ritual's conditions. Perpetrators and victims often cannot be identified, the process is long and costly, and crimes of this gravity are unprecedented for mato oput. Most respondents in a 2012 study had never taken part (JRP 2012).
- **Interpretation.** Mato oput has strong symbolic support in Acholi, but evidence that it has actually reconciled war-era perpetrators at scale is thin.

## 8. Diplomatic and mediation relevance

- **Evidence.** It is one of the few customary forgiveness practices written into a peace agreement (Juba 2007/2008), as a complement to formal accountability.
- **Interpretation.**
  - The case shows both the promise and the risk of using tradition in transitional justice. Local legitimacy and the language of repair can coexist with complementarity under the Rome Statute.
  - But outside promotion can freeze a varied practice into a brand, and the Annexure's "necessary modifications" leave open who decides what the practice means.
  - **Evidence:** the international track continued in parallel. On 4 February 2021, the International Criminal Court convicted former LRA commander Dominic Ongwen, himself abducted as a child, of war crimes and crimes against humanity (ICC 2021).

## 9. Cautions

- **Patriarchy.**
  - Compensation was traditionally also used "to marry a woman into the clan," with the child named after the deceased (JRP 2012).
  - Women are largely excluded from leadership roles, and the recorded perpetrators were all men.
- **Voluntariness and power.** Wartime pressures, aid incentives and chiefs' interests can undermine the rule that admission be voluntary.
- **Romanticization and invention.** Allen (2006, 2007) warns that outsiders and some elites oversold "Acholi forgiveness" as an alternative to justice.
- **Scale.** The practice was designed for single killings between known clans, not for mass atrocity.
- **Voice.** Acholi elders, women and survivors disagree among themselves (Baines 2007; JRP 2012). Community review is needed before publication.
