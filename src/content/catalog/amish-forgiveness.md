## 1. Entry details

- **Term:** Amish forgiveness, grounded in *Gelassenheit* (yieldedness)
- **Native script:** German *Gelassenheit*; Pennsylvania German *Uffgevva* ("giving up," submission) **[uncertain: orthography of the Pennsylvania German term]**
- **Language:** English, Pennsylvania German (Deitsch), High German in worship
- **Region:** Old Order Amish settlements, mainly in Pennsylvania, Ohio and Indiana, United States, and in Ontario, Canada
- **CAV pillars:** Sacred Axiology; Ethno-Ethics; Practice

## 2. One-line gloss

A religiously required, prompt and largely unconditional forgiveness of wrongdoers, understood as yielding to God's will and a condition of one's own forgiveness by God, practised within a tightly bounded community with its own discipline.

## 3. Etymology and semantic field

- **Evidence.**
  - *Gelassenheit* is a term of late-medieval German mysticism, taken up by 16th-century Anabaptists. For the Amish it means yielding to God, to the church and to others: humility, self-surrender and acceptance of suffering (Cronk 1981; Kraybill, Johnson-Weiner & Nolt 2013).
  - Forgiveness is tied to the Lord's Prayer ("forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors") and Matthew 6:14–15, which make one's own forgiveness by God conditional on forgiving others (Kraybill, Nolt & Weaver-Zercher 2007, 2010).
- **Interpretation.** In Amish speech, forgiveness is less an emotion than an act of obedience and self-surrender. It is a decision that may precede, rather than follow, healing.

## 4. How it is practiced

- **Evidence: the Nickel Mines shooting.** On 2 October 2006, Charles Carl Roberts IV, a non-Amish milk-truck driver, took hostage ten Amish girls at the West Nickel Mines School in Lancaster County, Pennsylvania. He killed five and wounded five before killing himself (Kraybill, Nolt & Weaver-Zercher 2007).
- **Evidence: what followed.**
  - Within hours, Amish neighbours visited the gunman's family to express forgiveness. Amish people attended his burial (Kraybill, Nolt & Weaver-Zercher 2007).
  - In 2007 the Nickel Mines Accountability Committee, which administered more than $4.3 million in donations gave a contribution to the gunman's widow. Its statement described forgiveness as "a journey, that you need help from your community of faith and from God… to make and hold on to a decision not to become a hostage to hostility" (AP 2007).
- **Evidence: an ongoing relationship.** Years later, the gunman's mother, Terri Roberts, was visiting one of the most severely injured survivors weekly. The Roberts family held gatherings for the victims' families, and an Amish mother said of them, "They're going two miles for every mile we go" (Murse 2011; Roberts 2015).
- **Evidence: within the community.** Forgiveness is woven into church life. Members confess before the congregation, and those under the ban (*Meidung*) are restored when they repent (Hostetler 1993; Kraybill, Nolt & Weaver-Zercher 2010).

## 5. History and change over time

- **Evidence.**
  - The Amish emerged from Swiss Anabaptism in a 1693 split led by Jakob Ammann (Hostetler 1993).
  - The *Martyrs Mirror* (1660), long kept in Amish homes, records Anabaptist martyrs who forgave their persecutors. The often-cited example is Dirk Willems, who turned back to rescue his pursuer from icy water (Kraybill, Nolt & Weaver-Zercher 2007).
- **Evidence.** Nickel Mines made "Amish forgiveness" a national and global reference. The response was widely praised, and also questioned as too fast or as a denial of anger (Kraybill, Nolt & Weaver-Zercher 2007).
- **Evidence.** In recent years, survivors' memoirs and scholarship have documented how forgiveness norms can work against victims inside Plain communities (Metzger 2022; Voelz 2024).
- **Interpretation (Chronological Ethics).** The term has moved from an in-group religious discipline to a public symbol of grace, and now to a contested case in debates about religious communities and abuse.

## 6. Why the generic English word fails

- **Interpretation.**
  - English "forgiveness" is usually conditional on remorse or comes at the end of an emotional process. Amish forgiveness is prompt, requested of the self rather than earned by the offender, and possible even when the offender is dead.
  - It is a communal practice: the community helps members "make and hold on to" the decision (AP 2007).
  - It sits beside, not against, grief and anger. Kraybill and colleagues distinguish forgiving from pardoning (forgoing legal consequences) and from reconciling.
  - English also lacks *Gelassenheit*, the background disposition that makes forgiveness a form of yielding.

## 7. Evidence on outcomes or social effects

- **Evidence.**
  - Accounts from the Roberts family and Amish neighbours describe lasting relationships and comfort on both sides (Murse 2011; Roberts 2015).
  - The gunman's widow wrote that "the instantaneous forgiveness and grace extended by the Amish community has touched our hearts" (quoted in Murse 2011).
  - Kraybill, Nolt and Weaver-Zercher (2007) report that Amish parents and children continued to grieve and struggle. Forgiveness did not end pain.
- **Evidence: internal harms.**
  - Metzger's (2022) qualitative analysis of a decade of private messages from victims argues that teachings on forgiveness and submission contribute to silencing sexual abuse among conservative Anabaptists.
  - Voelz (2024) notes that abuse may be treated as a sin handled by the church: "Once forgiven, the sin is erased from public communication." She identifies "forgiveness rituals and punishments that further traumatize victims" as a recurring memoir theme.
- **Gap.** No quantitative outcome studies were found.

## 8. Diplomatic and mediation relevance

- **Interpretation.**
  - The case shows a community responding to atrocity with forgiveness that does not depend on the offender's repentance. It counters assumptions that a victim's rage is inevitable, and it was offered across an insider–outsider line.
  - Its lessons are limited by context: a small, close religious community, with a dead offender and no political dispute.
  - It is most useful to mediators as a reminder that forgiveness can be decoupled from justice processes. It does not ask victims to forgo legal accountability, which in Nickel Mines was moot.

## 9. Cautions

- **Romanticization.** Media turned Nickel Mines into a feel-good parable and often omitted Amish grief, the community's own debate, and how unusual the circumstances were (Kraybill, Nolt & Weaver-Zercher 2007).
- **Coercion of victims.** Inside some communities, quick-forgiveness norms, confession procedures and expectations of reconciliation pressure abuse victims to forgive and keep silent (Metzger 2022; Voelz 2024). This partly resolves the study's earlier **[uncertain]** flag on victim pressure, though the evidence is qualitative and community practices vary.
- **Two directions.** **Interpretation:** Amish forgiveness of an outsider sits beside strict church discipline, including the ban (*Meidung*), toward members (Hostetler 1993).
- **Not monolithic.** Amish affiliations differ in practice. "The Amish" do not speak with one voice.
- **Voice.** Most published accounts are by outside scholars or by the offender's family. Amish people rarely publish. This entry relies on outsider interpretation.
