## 2. One-line gloss

The condition of beauty, harmony, balance, and right relation that Diné life, law, and healing aim to keep or restore. Diné authors usually place it within the larger frame of *Sa'ah Naagháí Bik'eh Hózhóón*.

## 3. Etymology and semantic field

**Evidence.** The non-Diné anthropologist Gary Witherspoon's study of Navajo language and thought presents hózhǫ́ as the term "most easily glossed as representing Navajo ideals of harmony and order" (as summarized by the University of Michigan Press, Witherspoon 1977). His fuller breakdown into senses such as beauty, order, goodness, and health was not checked against the text **[uncertain]**.

**Evidence.** Diné authors define it in their own terms:
- Former Navajo Nation Chief Justice Robert Yazzie writes that Navajo prayers and ceremonies "tell us of hozho—'the perfect state'" (Yazzie 1994).
- Diné nurse-scholar Michelle Kahn-John and Mary Koithan describe the Hózhó philosophy as "principles that guide one's thoughts, actions, behaviors, and speech" (Kahn-John and Koithan 2015).
- The Diné scholars in Lee (2014) set hózhǫ́ within *Sa'ah Naagháí Bik'eh Hózhóón* (SNBH), which Lee's volume describes as "generally understood to mean 'one's journey of striving to live a long, harmonious life.'"

**Interpretation.** The semantic field joins aesthetics, ethics, health, kinship (*k'é*), and cosmic order. It is relational and dynamic: something walked, kept, lost, and restored.

## 4. How it is practiced

**Evidence.**
- **Law and peacemaking.** Yazzie (1994) describes Navajo law, *beehaz'áanii*, as something fundamental that "life comes from." He contrasts "vertical" adversarial justice with "horizontal" Navajo justice among relatives and equals. In 1982 the Navajo Nation Judicial Conference created the Navajo Peacemaker Court. There a *naat'áanii* (peacemaker), a non-coercive leader chosen by the community, guides the parties and their relatives toward an agreement. Yazzie reported 210 peacemakers serving 89 chapters at the time of writing.
- **Common law.** Former Justice Raymond Austin (2009) shows how Navajo courts use hózhǫ́, *k'é* (kinship and relational duty), and *k'éí* (clan relations) as working legal doctrine. He treats disputes as disruptions of balance that remedies must address.
- **Governance.** In 2002 the Navajo Nation Council formally recognized the *Diné bi beenahaz'áanii* (Fundamental Laws of the Diné), codified at 1 N.N.C. §§201–206. The executive branch is called *Hózhóójí Nahat'á*. The education provisions speak of "living in balance with all creation, walking in beauty and making a living," and the text closes with "the beauty of happiness and harmony" (Navajo Nation Council 2002).
- **Health and everyday life.** Kahn-John and Koithan (2015) describe hózhǫ́ as a wellness philosophy lived through daily conduct and speech.

**Interpretation.** Hózhǫ́ is unusual among CAV terms: a sovereign government has embedded it in law and institutions.

## 5. History and change over time

**Interpretation.** The Peacemaker Court (1982) and the Fundamental Laws (2002) read as deliberate revitalizations after a long period of imposed Anglo-American court models, a history Yazzie (1994) and Austin (2009) both discuss. **Evidence.** The Navajo Supreme Court has held that the Fundamental Laws are acknowledged, not enacted, by the Council **[uncertain: case citation not checked]**.

**Evidence.** Kahn-John et al. (2021) interviewed 22 Diné people across three generations. Elders showed the deepest understanding of Hózhó, followed by adolescents. Adults showed an unexpected scarcity of understanding.

**Evidence.** Diné historian Jennifer Nez Denetdale (2006) warns that appeals to "tradition" in Navajo Nation politics are not neutral. Opponents used a traditional narrative against LeNora Fulton's 1998 run for Navajo Nation president, and male leaders have re-inscribed Western gender roles while claiming to speak for traditional philosophy. "Tradition is not without a political context."

**Interpretation.** Hózhǫ́'s meaning is being actively reworked by Diné people. An outside catalog should not try to settle it.

## 6. Why the generic English word fails

**Interpretation.**
- *Harmony*, *balance*, *beauty*, and *wellness* each catch one aspect. English "beauty" is mostly aesthetic; in hózhǫ́ it is also ethical, medical, and cosmic.
- English "harmony" suggests the absence of friction. Navajo peacemaking works through frank talk among relatives (Yazzie 1994).
- Hózhǫ́ cannot be separated from k'é and from Diné language, land, and ceremony.

**Correction to the earlier catalog sketch.** The earlier sketch rightly says hózhǫ́ "is not a wellness brand" and stresses Diné authority. It understates how far hózhǫ́ is already institutionalized: in Navajo statute, court doctrine, the name of the executive branch, and nursing and health scholarship. Diné institutions have already made it infrastructure, on their own terms.

## 7. Evidence on outcomes or social effects

- **Evidence.** A National Institute of Justice-funded evaluation of Navajo peacemaking (Gross 1999) reported that problems recurred in 29% of peacemaking cases compared with 64% of family-court cases. Participants also described reaching a sense of hozho. The study was small and non-randomized, and its author called for further research.
- **Evidence.** Kahn-John et al. (2021) report that Diné participants see cultural wisdom, including Hózhó, as a health-sustaining protective factor. This is qualitative evidence of meaning, not a measured outcome.
- **Evidence and caution.** Legal scholar Donna Coker (1999) studied peacemaking in domestic violence cases. She found it can strengthen battered women's autonomy, through reparations (*nályééh*), confronting the abuser's family, and stories that challenge subordination. She also found that some women were pressured to take part, that agreements were hard to enforce, and that some peacemakers favored keeping marriages together.

**Interpretation.** The evidence is promising but thin and dated. Do not cite the 29% vs. 64% figure without its limits.

## 8. Diplomatic and mediation relevance

**Interpretation.**
- The Fundamental Laws themselves address relations and agreements with other nations (1 N.N.C. §202). Any diplomatic relevance therefore runs through the Navajo Nation's own government, not through CAV.
- For outside mediators the lesson is procedural, not appropriative: ask what restoration would mean in the parties' own terms, and who has authority to say so (as the earlier sketch puts it).
- Navajo peacemaking has often been cited as a model for restorative justice. Coker's findings show why copying the form without the community, kinship accountability, and safeguards can harm weaker parties.

## 9. Cautions

- **Insider vs. outsider voice.** Witherspoon's account remains influential, but Diné scholars (Lee 2014; Kahn-John 2015, 2021; Denetdale 2006) and jurists (Yazzie 1994; Austin 2009) should be treated as primary authorities.
- **Sacred knowledge.** Some knowledge tied to hózhǫ́ is ceremonial and not CAV's to catalog. Diné review, through Navajo Nation channels or Diné scholars, is needed before any use.
- **Commercialization.** **Interpretation.** "Walk in beauty" circulates widely as a slogan in non-Native wellness and souvenir markets. No source on this was reviewed, so CAV should not make specific claims **[uncertain]**.
- **Romanticization.** **Interpretation.** Presenting hózhǫ́ as timeless and conflict-free erases Diné debate and history, including the politics of tradition that Denetdale describes.
- **Coercion and gender.** Appeals to traditional harmony have been used to exclude women from leadership (Denetdale 2006). Pressure to reconcile can override victims' safety (Coker 1999).
