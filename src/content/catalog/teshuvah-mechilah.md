## 1. Entry details

- **Term:** teshuvah (repentance, "return") and mechilah (the injured party's forgoing of a claim)
- **Native script:** Hebrew תשובה (teshuvah); מחילה (meḥilah)
- **Language:** Hebrew (rabbinic and liturgical); Aramaic in the Talmud
- **Region:** Jewish communities worldwide; codified in rabbinic law
- **CAV pillars:** Sacred Axiology; Semantic Ethics; Chronological Ethics

## 2. One-line gloss

A procedure in which a wrongdoer must repair the harm and personally ask the injured person for forgiveness, because for wrongs between people neither God nor the Day of Atonement can forgive in the victim's place.

## 3. Etymology and semantic field

- **Evidence.**
  - *Teshuvah* derives from the root *sh-w-b*, "to return," so repentance is pictured as turning back.
  - *Mechilah* comes from *m-ḥ-l*, which in rabbinic usage means to waive or forgo a claim, as a creditor forgoes a debt.
  - The Yom Kippur liturgy pairs it with *seliḥah* (pardon) and *kapparah* (atonement): "forgive us, pardon us, grant us atonement."
- **Interpretation.** The legal register matters. Mechilah is the victim's release of what the offender owes. It is a right that only its holder can waive, which is why it cannot be delegated.

## 4. How it is practiced

- **Evidence: the boundary between God and people.** Mishnah Yoma 8:9: "for transgressions between a person and God, Yom Kippur atones; however, for transgressions between a person and another, Yom Kippur does not atone until he appeases the other person." Mishnah Bava Kamma 8:7 adds that even full payment for an injury does not bring forgiveness until the offender asks, and that a victim who refuses is called "cruel."
- **Evidence: the procedure** (Maimonides, *Mishneh Torah*, Hilkhot Teshuvah 2:9–11):
  - Restitution alone is not enough; the offender "must appease him and ask him to forgive him," even for words alone.
  - If refused, he returns with three friends, up to three times. After that "the person who refuses to grant forgiveness is the one considered as the sinner." The exception is one's teacher, whom one approaches "even a thousand times."
  - The injured party must not "be cruel and refuse to be appeased," and should forgive "with a complete heart and a willing spirit."
  - If the victim has died, the offender confesses at the grave before ten people and repays the heirs.
- **Evidence: the three-request limit** has Talmudic roots (Yoma 87a, from the threefold "please" of Genesis 50:17). The Talmud also tells how Rav, though he was the injured party, went to a butcher who had insulted him on the eve of Yom Kippur to give him the chance to reconcile.
- **Evidence: the codified custom** (Shulchan Arukh, Orach Chayim 606:1). People appease anyone they have wronged before Yom Kippur. The gloss of Moses Isserles (Rema) adds two qualifications: refusing is permitted if meant "for the good of the one requesting," and one "need not forgive" a person who spread a bad name about one.
- **Interpretation.** In practice this produces the seasonal custom of asking forgiveness in the days before Yom Kippur, among family, friends and colleagues.

## 5. History and change over time

- **Evidence.** The core norms run from the Mishnah (c. 200 CE) and Talmud through Maimonides (12th c.) to the Shulchan Arukh (16th c.), with commentary continuing to today.
- **Evidence.** After the Holocaust the rule that only the victim can forgive became a public test case:
  - Wiesenthal's *The Sunflower* asks whether he should have forgiven a dying SS man who sought forgiveness from "a Jew." Its symposium responses divide sharply.
  - Jankélévitch (1996 [1971]) argued that Nazi crimes are imprescriptible and that no one can pardon them on the dead's behalf; he wrote that forgiveness "died in the death camps" **[uncertain: exact wording in the English translation not checked]**.
- **Interpretation (Chronological Ethics).** Nussbaum (2016) treats teshuvah, alongside Christian penance, as a source of the "transactional" model of forgiveness (confession, contrition, conditional pardon) that she criticizes. Whether one accepts her critique or not, the Jewish codes show that a highly specified, conditional forgiveness procedure is ancient, not a modern therapeutic invention.

## 6. Why the generic English word fails

- **Interpretation.**
  - *Conditional:* repentance, restitution and a personal request come first.
  - *Non-transferable:* no rabbi, community or deity can grant mechilah in the victim's name.
  - *Bounded:* the duty to ask has a limit (three approaches), and after that the moral burden shifts to the refuser.
  - *Obligatory but qualified:* the victim is told not to be cruel, yet may withhold forgiveness in defined cases.
  - English "forgiveness" carries none of this structure. Its therapeutic sense (letting go regardless of the offender) is closer to what the codes call *not bearing a grudge* than to mechilah itself.

## 7. Evidence on outcomes or social effects

- **Evidence.** In a comparison of American Jews and Protestants, Cohen, Malka, Rozin and Cherfas (2006) found that Jews were more likely to judge some offenses unforgivable. They more often gave theological reasons: offense severity, the rule that only the victim can forgive, and the requirement of repentance. Religious commitment was more strongly associated with rejecting the idea of unforgivable offenses among Protestants than among Jews.
- **Interpretation.** This is direct evidence that the codified grammar shapes lay moral judgment, which is a Semantic Ethics finding. No study was found on the effects of the pre–Yom Kippur custom on relationships or well-being.

## 8. Diplomatic and mediation relevance

- **Interpretation.**
  - *Non-transferability* bears on intergroup settings. A state can apologize, pay reparations and seek reconciliation, but in this grammar it cannot obtain forgiveness on behalf of the dead, nor can survivors' governments grant it.
  - Demanding that victims "forgive" on behalf of others can therefore read as a category mistake or an insult, which is a useful warning for mediators.
  - The procedure also gives a template: restitution first, a personal and witnessed request, a defined number of approaches, and a clear point at which responsibility shifts.

## 9. Cautions

- **Variation.** Practice differs across Orthodox, Conservative, Reform and secular communities. The codes state norms, not observed behaviour.
- **Coercion within a duty.** The rule against being "cruel" can pressure victims, including victims of abuse, to grant forgiveness. The Rema's exceptions show that authorities saw this risk, but they are narrow.
- **Boundary language.** Maimonides contrasts forgiving Israelites with "insensitive gentiles" whose "wrath is preserved forever" (Hilkhot Teshuvah 2:10). Quoting the procedure as universal ethics without noting this framing misrepresents the source.
- **Christian contrast.** Popular contrasts of "Jewish justice" with "Christian forgiveness" are a polemical stereotype. The Jewish sources themselves command forgiveness of the penitent.
- **Voice.** Halakhic interpretation belongs to living rabbinic and scholarly communities. This entry summarizes and does not rule.
