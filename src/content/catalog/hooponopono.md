## 1. Entry details

- **Term:** hoʻoponopono
- **Native script:** hoʻoponopono (ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi is written in Latin script; the ʻokina ʻ marks a glottal stop)
- **Language:** Hawaiian (ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi)
- **Region:** Hawaiian Islands; Native Hawaiian ʻohana (extended families)
- **CAV pillars:** Semantic Ethics; Sacred Axiology; Chronological Ethics
- **Scope note:** This entry covers the family process described by Mary Kawena Pukui. It is kept separate from Morrnah Simeona's "Self I-Dentity through Hoʻoponopono" and from the commercial *Zero Limits* mantra (see §9).

## 2. One-line gloss

A family conference, led by an elder or trusted healer, in which prayer, layered truth-telling, confession (*mihi*) and restitution are answered by *mutual* release (*kala*) from the fault, so that relationships are "set right."

## 3. Etymology and semantic field

- **Evidence.** *Hoʻo-* is a causative prefix ("to make, cause"); *pono* means right, correct, in perfect order, with about twenty related senses; the reduplicated *ponopono* means "in order, cared for." Both forms "connote what is socially-morally approved and desirable" (Pukui, Haertig & Lee 1972, 60). The Pukui–Elbert *Hawaiian Dictionary* gives a general sense (to put in order, correct, manage) and a specialized one: "mental cleansing: family conferences in which relationships were set right through prayer, discussion, confession, repentance, and mutual restitution and forgiveness" (Pukui & Elbert 1986, s.v.).
- **Evidence: the working vocabulary** (Pukui et al. 1972, 60–75):
  - *hala*: fault (later also "sin"), pictured as a cord binding offender, offense and victim; *hihia*: the spreading entanglement of ill feeling; *hoʻomauhala*: "holding fast the fault";
  - *mahiki*: peeling back layers of a problem ("Think of peeling an onion," in Pukui's words); *ʻoiaʻiʻo*: truthfulness; *hoʻomalu*: an imposed silence;
  - *mihi*: repentance, confession, apology; *kala*: to release, untie, unbind, let go.
- **Interpretation.** English "forgiveness" names one act by one party. The Hawaiian field splits it into *mihi* (the offender's move) and *kala* (a release that "must be 'untied,' not by one but both"). The ritual formula Pukui records translates as "I unbind you from the fault, and thus may I also be unbound from it" (Pukui et al. 1972, 75). Release is reciprocal by design.

## 4. How it is practiced

- **Evidence: who.** Essentially a family matter: the immediate family, or those most involved in the *pilikia* (trouble). A family senior or a healing *kahuna* (never a sorcerer) could lead. Pukui said "the ideal is to keep it in the family" (Pukui et al. 1972, 61).
- **Evidence: when.** To resolve quarrels, and traditionally to look for the relational cause of an illness. Pukui et al. report that a century earlier *kahuna* often asked whether hoʻoponopono had been held before treating a patient (1972, 66).
- **Evidence: form** (Pukui et al. 1972, 61–62):
  1. opening *pule* (prayer);
  2. statement of the problem;
  3. *mahiki*, working through each layer in turn;
  4. discussion channeled through the leader, who questions participants and can halt the talk;
  5. honest confession to the gods (or God) and to each other;
  6. restitution, or arrangements for it;
  7. mutual *mihi* and *kala*;
  8. closing prayer.
  
  The leader then decrees *hoʻomalu* on the whole subject, "both immediately and long after." In pre-Christian practice closing rites (*pani*) followed, with offerings and a feast; by 1971 an ordinary meal was usual.
- **Evidence: obligation to forgive.** Pukui gives the rule that one "could not *huli kua* (turn the back)" on a sincere penitent, on pain of the *ʻaumākua* (ancestral gods) turning their backs in turn (Pukui et al. 1972, 74).

## 5. History and change over time

- **Evidence.** The 1972 account comes from the Culture Committee of the Queen Liliʻuokalani Children's Center, where Pukui explained concepts to social workers and a psychiatrist (E. W. Haertig). Pukui reported taking part for 47 years, "from semi-Christian to Christian times" (61).
- **Evidence.** The same source says that "when Christianity came in, more than a century ago, hoʻoponopono went out." Because its prayers addressed the *akua* and *ʻaumākua*, the whole practice was labeled "pagan." Some families practiced it secretly. By 1971 Center staff had compiled a long list of distorted versions given the name, from fortune-telling to sorcery (69–70).
- **Evidence.** The Center then rebuilt the process as a family therapy. Ito (1985) shows how Hawaiian relational metaphors were kept in that adaptation. Shook (1985) documents uses in social work and drug treatment and warns that further individualization could destroy its impact as a process rooted in Hawaiian experience. Boggs and Chun (1990) analyze the discourse of actual sessions.
- **Interpretation.** The documented record is a 20th-century reconstruction from living memory, deliberately reconciled with Christianity. Claims about pre-contact form (pre-1778) rest mainly on that memory and on general Polynesian parallels **[uncertain: thin pre-20th-century record, as flagged in the forgiveness study]**.

## 6. Why the generic English word fails

- **Interpretation.**
  - *Forgiveness* is usually unilateral and inward. *Kala* is bilateral and spoken aloud in a prescribed formula.
  - *Forgiveness* is often detached from repair. Hoʻoponopono pairs release with restitution; without it the victim "continues to feel the loss" (75).
  - *Forgiveness* is an outcome. Hoʻoponopono is a *procedure* with a leader, rules of speech, enforced silences and a sealed ending.
  - Its object is relational order (*pono*) among kin and with the sacred, not the victim's peace of mind alone.

## 7. Evidence on outcomes or social effects

- **Evidence.** Mostly case-based. Pukui et al. (1972) and Shook (1985) present case narratives. Hurdle (2002) describes hoʻoponopono as a culturally based social-work intervention and reports its use with non-Hawaiian clients. Brinson and Fisher (1999) adapt it as a school-counseling group model.
- **Gap.** No controlled outcome study of the *traditional* family process was found. Studies using the name often test Simeona-derived or self-help versions, which say nothing about the traditional practice.

## 8. Diplomatic and mediation relevance

- **Interpretation.** Transferable design features: a facilitator through whom all speech passes; *hoʻomalu* as a sanctioned pause; *mahiki* as sequencing of grievances; a binding rule against reopening a settled matter. Wall and Callister (1995) draw lessons from it for Western mediation.
- **Limits.** It presupposes kinship, a shared spiritual frame and a leader everyone accepts: a model for *small-group* repair, not for interstate or mass-atrocity settings.

## 9. Cautions

- **Commercialization.** Simeona (1913–1992) developed an individual, self-directed form that her successors present as "an updated version of an ancient Hawaiian problem solving process" (SITH n.d.). *Zero Limits* (Vitale & Hew Len 2007) reduces it further to a four-phrase mantra sold for "wealth, health, peace." Neither is traditional hoʻoponopono, and CAV should never cite them as evidence of Hawaiian practice.
- **Romanticization.** Pukui's own text stresses decline, distortion and reconstruction, not an unbroken "ancient" tradition.
- **Power.** The leader had "complete authority" (68). Mutual release can pressure the less powerful member to *kala* before they are ready. Pukui et al. record an estranged wife who refused to take part, and treat formal severance (*ʻoki*) as an accepted outcome when release fails (70).
- **Voice.** Most published accounts are clinical or outsider-authored. Native Hawaiian practitioners and Hawaiian-language scholarship should review this entry before publication.
